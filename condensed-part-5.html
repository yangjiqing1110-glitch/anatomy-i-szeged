Identification of Basic Tissues — 48 Slides

All 48 slides of the histology identification list as fast-recall cards: stain, low-power architecture, high-power features, the discriminators, and the look-alike slides it must not be confused with.

48 topics · Condensed edition

V.1Kidney (HE)

Core answerA coronal section of the kidney (ren) stained with haematoxylin and eosin: a compound tubular gland in which the cortex contains renal corpuscles with glomeruli and two kinds of convoluted tubule, while the medulla contains parallel straight tubules, collecting ducts and vasa recta. The round glomerulus inside Bowman's capsule is the structure that identifies the organ at first glance.

Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

How to identify it at the exam
  • Round glomerulus inside a clear space inside a thin capsule — no other organ has this.
  • Side-by-side eosinophilic proximal and paler distal tubules in the cortex.
  • Medulla = parallel straight tubules and wide collecting ducts, no glomeruli.
  • Papilla lined by urothelium gives you the cortex-to-medulla direction.
Differential diagnosis
  • Liver: uniform lobules with sinusoids, no corpuscles, no tubules.
  • Thyroid gland: follicles filled with colloid, not tubular profiles.
  • Pancreas: serous acini plus islets, no glomeruli.
  • Ureter or urinary bladder: urothelium over a thick muscular wall, no cortex.

V.2Jejunum (HE)

Core answerA transverse section of the jejunum (jejunum) stained with HE: a small intestine in which the mucosa carries villi and crypts lined by simple columnar epithelium with goblet cells, the submucosa contains no glands, and the muscular layer has an inner circular and an outer longitudinal layer. Villi plus crypts together, and the absence of submucosal glands and of large lymphoid aggregates, identify it as jejunum rather than duodenum or ileum.

Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

How to identify it at the exam
  • Villi and crypts present together — the signature of the small intestine.
  • Simple columnar epithelium with goblet cells; no villus goblet-cell-free sheet of stomach type.
  • No glands in the submucosa (contrast duodenum) and no large Peyer's patches (contrast ileum).
  • Four-layered wall with a clear muscularis mucosae and a serosa.
Differential diagnosis
  • Duodenum: Brunner's mucous glands occupy the submucosa.
  • Ileum: large lymphatic nodules (Peyer's patches) in the lamina propria and submucosa.
  • Colon: crypts but no villi, and far more goblet cells.
  • Stomach: gastric pits and glands, no villi.

V.3Unicellular gland (PAS + H)

Core answerA PAS–haematoxylin preparation of an epithelium containing unicellular glands: the goblet cells (exocrinocyti caliciformes) appear as goblet-shaped cells whose mucin granules are strongly PAS-positive magenta, while the nuclei are counterstained blue with haematoxylin. A single mucus-secreting cell sitting inside an epithelium is the definition of a unicellular exocrine gland.

Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

How to identify it at the exam
  • Single goblet-shaped magenta cells standing in a row of non-staining epithelial cells.
  • The magenta material is inside the apical part of the cell, not in a duct or acinus.
  • Nucleus squeezed to the base — a gland cell that secretes directly to the surface.
  • Diastase control separates mucin from glycogen staining.
Differential diagnosis
  • Multicellular mucous gland (sublingual, submandibular mucous tubules): magenta cells grouped into acini/tubules with ducts and interlobular septa.
  • Gastric surface mucous cells: PAS-positive but form a continuous simple columnar sheet, not individual goblet cells.
  • Basement membrane or brush border: a thin continuous magenta line, not a cell-shaped drop.
  • Paneth cells: eosinophilic apical granules that are not PAS-positive mucin.

V.4Trachea (HE)

Core answerA transverse section of the trachea (trachea) stained with HE: respiratory epithelium (ciliated pseudostratified columnar with goblet cells) resting on a very thick basement membrane, a lamina propria with seromucous glands, and dorsally open horseshoe-shaped pieces of hyaline cartilage. The cartilage is what separates trachea from every other tubular organ in the slide set.

Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

How to identify it at the exam
  • Pseudostratified ciliated epithelium on a very thick basement membrane.
  • Hyaline cartilage plate in the wall, with a perichondrium.
  • Seromucous glands with serous demilunes between mucosa and cartilage.
  • Dorsal membranous wall = smooth trachealis muscle, no cartilage.
Differential diagnosis
  • Oesophagus: non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, no cartilage, has a muscularis mucosae.
  • Bronchus: cartilage in small irregular plates, a complete ring of smooth muscle between mucosa and cartilage, fewer glands.
  • Larynx: elastic cartilage and stratified squamous epithelium over the vocal fold.
  • Skin: keratinized stratified squamous epithelium with appendages.

V.5Oesophagus (HE)

Core answerA transverse section of the oesophagus (oesophagus) stained with HE: a thick non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium over a lamina propria, a well-developed muscularis mucosae, a submucosa with small mucous glands, and a muscular layer whose upper third is skeletal and lower third smooth muscle. The combination of stratified squamous epithelium with a muscularis mucosae and submucosal glands is diagnostic.

Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

How to identify it at the exam
  • Thick non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium without keratin and without goblet cells.
  • Conspicuous muscularis mucosae and small submucosal mucous glands.
  • Star-shaped lumen with mucosal folds.
  • Two muscle layers of skeletal, mixed or smooth type depending on the level.
Differential diagnosis
  • Trachea: ciliated pseudostratified epithelium, cartilage plates, no muscularis mucosae.
  • Stomach (cardia): simple columnar epithelium with gastric pits; no stratified squamous sheet.
  • Vagina: similar stratified squamous epithelium, but a fibromuscular wall without muscularis mucosae and without glands.
  • Thin skin: keratinized stratified squamous epithelium with appendages.

V.6Finger pad (HE)

Core answerA vertical section through the pad of a fingertip — thick, glabrous skin stained with HE: a very thick keratinized epidermis with a prominent stratum corneum and a visible stratum lucidum, dermal papillae carrying Meissner corpuscles, eccrine sweat ducts spiralling to the surface, and Pacinian corpuscles deep in the subcutaneous tissue. There are no hair follicles and no sebaceous glands.

Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

How to identify it at the exam
  • Thick skin: stratum corneum thicker than the living epidermis, and a visible stratum lucidum.
  • No hair follicles, no sebaceous glands, no apocrine glands — only eccrine sweat glands.
  • Sweat ducts spiralling through the epidermis to pores on the crests.
  • Meissner corpuscles in the dermal papillae; Pacinian corpuscles deep.
Differential diagnosis
  • Thin (hairy) skin: thin stratum corneum, no stratum lucidum, hair follicles and sebaceous glands present.
  • Lip: shows a transition from keratinized skin to non-keratinized mucosa with labial glands.
  • Oesophagus or vagina: non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, no skin appendages.
  • Sole skin: the same thick type in principle, so argue the site from the overall architecture and, if present, the Meissner corpuscle density.

V.7Submandibular gland (HE)

Core answerA section of the submandibular gland (glandula submandibularis) stained with HE: a mixed seromucous salivary gland, predominantly serous, in which basophilic serous acini and pale mucous tubules are capped by serous demilunes, and the secretion drains through intercalated, striated and interlobular ducts. The combination of mixed acini, demilunes and striated ducts is the classic examination picture.

Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

How to identify it at the exam
  • Two acinar populations in one lobule: dark-blue serous acini and pale mucous tubules.
  • Serous demilunes capping mucous tubules.
  • Striated ducts: pink columnar ducts with basal striations.
  • Lobules separated by septa with interlobular ducts and adipose tissue.
Differential diagnosis
  • Parotid gland: pure serous acini, abundant adipose tissue, no demilunes, no mucous tubules.
  • Sublingual gland: predominantly mucous units with poorly developed striated ducts.
  • Pancreas: serous acini but with islets of Langerhans and no striated ducts.
  • Tracheal glands: the same seromucous design, but inside the wall of the airway with cartilage nearby.

V.8Tendon (HE)

Core answerA longitudinal section of a tendon (tendo) stained with HE: dense regular connective tissue (textus connectivus compactus regularis) made of parallel bundles of strongly eosinophilic type I collagen fibres, with rows of flattened, spindle-shaped fibrocyte nuclei between the bundles and no cells or vessels inside them.

High-power key features

Type I collagen bundlesParallel, wavy, strongly eosinophilicTransmit muscle force; high tensile strength
Fibrocytes / tenocytesFlattened nuclei with pointed ends, in rowsSynthesis and maintenance of the matrix
EndotenonLoose connective tissue between bundles, with vesselsNutritive and sliding spaces

Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

How to identify it at the exam
  • Parallel eosinophilic collagen bundles running across the whole field.
  • Thin, sharp-ended fibrocyte nuclei in rows between bundles.
  • No cells inside the bundles, no striation, no lacunae.
  • Endotenon septa with vessels at regular intervals.
Differential diagnosis
  • Smooth muscle (longitudinal): spindle cells with central cigar-shaped nuclei and real cytoplasm; bundles not wavy; no endotenon.
  • Peripheral nerve (longitudinal): wavy fibres with myelin sheaths and perineurium, not homogeneous eosinophilic bundles.
  • Fibrocartilage: chondrocytes in lacunae arranged in rows between coarse collagen bundles.
  • Dense irregular connective tissue (dermis): bundles run in all directions.

V.9Adipose tissue (HE)

Core answerWhite adipose tissue (textus adiposus albus) stained with HE: tightly packed unilocular adipocytes (adipocyti uniloculares) whose single large lipid droplet has been dissolved out during paraffin processing, leaving clear, round spaces limited by a thin eosinophilic rim of cytoplasm with a flattened peripheral nucleus — the "chicken-wire" pattern.

High-power key features

FeatureWhite adipose tissueBrown adipose tissue
AdipocyteUnilocular, one large dropletMultilocular, many small droplets
NucleusFlattened, peripheralRound, central
CytoplasmThin rim, few organellesAbundant, eosinophilic, many mitochondria
VascularityModerateVery rich

Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

How to identify it at the exam
  • Empty, round, closely packed vacuoles with a "chicken-wire" pattern.
  • Nuclei flattened at the very periphery of the spaces.
  • No fibres crossing the spaces; delicate capillaries between adipocytes.
  • To prove lipid, ask for a frozen section + Sudan or Oil Red O.
Differential diagnosis
  • Loose connective tissue: abundant fibres and many cell types between the spaces; fat cells are only a minority.
  • Mucous connective tissue (Wharton's jelly): sparse cells, abundant gelatinous matrix, not a uniform vacuole sheet.
  • Processing artefacts: irregular cracks or clefting rather than uniform round spaces with marginal nuclei.
  • Brown adipose tissue: multilocular cells with central nuclei.

V.10Adipose tissue (frozen section, Sudan

Core answerA frozen section of adipose tissue stained with a fat-soluble dye (Sudan III/IV or Oil Red O): because the tissue has never touched alcohol or xylene, the lipid remains in place and stains bright red, proving that the clear spaces of the HE slide are indeed fat and the cells are adipocytes.

High-power key features

MethodColour of lipidWhat it proves / comment

Sudan black BBlue-blackAlso used for myelin and for phospholipid
Oil Red ORedCommon alternative for neutral lipid
Osmium tetroxideBlackReduces to black metallic osmium in unsaturated lipid (also myelin)
HE on paraffinNot stained (dissolved)Shows empty spaces only; the negative control for the argument

Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

How to identify it at the exam
  • Bright red globules filling cells that look empty on HE.
  • Frozen section, water-based staining — no alcohol or xylene anywhere in the protocol.
  • Blue nuclei at the periphery of the red droplets, matching the adipocyte shape.
  • The next step in the answer: compare with the HE slide of the same tissue.
Differential diagnosis
  • HE adipose tissue: identical architecture but empty spaces; do not call the spaces "fat" on HE alone.
  • Sebaceous gland: lipid also stains red but the cells form alveoli with holocrine secretion.
  • Steatotic liver or foam cells: red droplets inside hepatocytes or macrophages, not one droplet per cell in a fatty sheet.
  • Ice-crystal artefact: irregular clefts that also fail to stain uniformly.

V.11Hyaline cartilage (HE)

Core answerHyaline cartilage (cartilago hyalina) stained with HE: a smooth, glassy, basophilic matrix containing chondrocytes in lacunae, often in isogenous groups, with a perichondrium on the free surfaces (absent from articular surfaces). The matrix protein fibres are "masked", so no fibres can be seen — that is the meaning of hyaline.

Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

How to identify it at the exam
  • Glassy, homogeneous basophilic matrix with no visible fibres.
  • Chondrocytes in lacunae, often in isogenous groups.
  • Perichondrium on a free surface; absent on articular surfaces.
  • No blood vessels inside the tissue.
Differential diagnosis
  • Elastic cartilage: looks similar on HE, but an orcein stain reveals a branching elastic network.
  • Fibrocartilage: coarse eosinophilic collagen bundles with chondrocytes in rows, no perichondrium.
  • Bone: eosinophilic matrix, osteons and canaliculi; lacunae are not round and empty in decalcified bone.
  • Tracheal gland adenoma or thyroid colloid: no lacunae and no chondrocytes.

V.12Elastic cartilage (orcein + H)

Core answerElastic cartilage (cartilago elastica) stained with orcein and haematoxylin: the same chondrocytes in lacunae and the same perichondrium as hyaline cartilage, but the matrix contains a conspicuous, branching network of elastic fibres stained by orcein, which is the only way to tell it from hyaline cartilage. It is found in the auricle, the epiglottis and the auditory tube.

Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

How to identify it at the exam
  • A brown to black branching network of fibres running through cartilage matrix.
  • Chondrocytes in lacunae and a perichondrium — it is still cartilage.
  • The orcein stain is the discriminator against hyaline cartilage.
  • Site: auricle, epiglottis or auditory tube.
Differential diagnosis
  • Hyaline cartilage: no elastic network on orcein; the commonest confusion if the stain is not considered.
  • Elastic connective tissue (aorta, ligamentum flavum): elastic fibres without lacunae or chondrocytes.
  • Fibrocartilage: coarse eosinophilic collagen bundles and rows of chondrocytes.
  • Auricle skin: contains elastic fibres in the dermis but also epidermis and appendages.

V.13Fibrocartilage (HE)

Core answerFibrocartilage (cartilago fibrosa) stained with HE: coarse, strongly eosinophilic bundles of type I collagen in parallel or crossing arrays, with oval chondrocytes lying in lacunae in rows between the bundles, very little basophilic matrix and no distinct perichondrium. It is the structural transition between dense connective tissue and hyaline cartilage.

Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

How to identify it at the exam
  • Strongly eosinophilic collagen bundles plus chondrocytes in lacunae arranged in rows.
  • No perichondrium and very little basophilic matrix.
  • Hybrid look: half tendon, half cartilage.
  • Site: intervertebral disc, symphysis, meniscus, tendon insertion.
Differential diagnosis
  • Tendon: parallel collagen bundles but spindle-shaped fibrocyte nuclei with sharp poles, never lacunae or rows of round cells.
  • Hyaline cartilage: glassy basophilic matrix, perichondrium, isogenous groups.
  • Elastic cartilage: elastic network and perichondrium on orcein.
  • Bone: lamellar or trabecular eosinophilic matrix with osteocytes and canaliculi.

V.14Bone (ground section)

Core answerA ground section of compact lamellar bone (os compactum): an undecalcified slice of the diaphysis ground thin and mounted, showing osteons (osteona) as concentric lamellae around central Haversian canals, interstitial lamellae between them, Volkmann canals and empty osteocyte lacunae with their canaliculi. No cells survive the grinding, so the slide shows the architecture of the matrix only.

Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

How to identify it at the exam
  • Concentric lamellae around central canals — osteons and nothing else.
  • Empty osteocyte lacunae and fine canaliculi, no cells with nuclei.
  • Interstitial and circumferential lamellae complete the architecture.
  • Say the technique: undecalcified ground section, calcified matrix retained.
Differential diagnosis
  • Decalcified bone (HE): osteocytes visible in lacunae, eosinophilic matrix, marrow spaces, cutting cones.
  • Calcified hyaline cartilage: chondrocytes in lacunae, no osteons or Haversian canals.
  • Fibrocartilage: coarse collagen bundles and rows of chondrocytes.
  • Tooth ground section: dentinal tubules and enamel, not osteons.

V.15Endochondral ossification (HE)

Core answerA longitudinal section through a developing long bone or fetal rib stained with HE: a cartilaginous model is replaced by bone in an ordered sequence, so from the epiphysis towards the diaphysis the epiphyseal growth cartilage shows resting, proliferating, hypertrophic, calcified and eroded zones before the ossification zone, while a periosteal bone collar forms around the shaft and trabeculae are lined by osteoblasts and resorbed by osteoclasts.

Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

How to identify it at the exam
  • Bands of cartilage changing in a fixed order from the epiphysis to the diaphysis.
  • Trabeculae with a basophilic cartilage core and an eosinophilic bone surface.
  • Periosteal bone collar and periosteum on the shaft.
  • Osteoblasts lining trabeculae and osteoclasts in Howship lacunae.
Differential diagnosis
  • Intramembranous ossification: bone trabeculae directly in condensed mesenchyme, no cartilage zones (skull vault, maxilla).
  • Adult long bone: only osteons and marrow, no growth cartilage.
  • Pure hyaline cartilage: no vessels, no trabeculae, no marrow spaces.
  • Fracture callus: disorganised mixture of cartilage and woven bone at a fracture site, without the ordered zones.

V.16Smooth muscle (HE)

Core answerSmooth muscle (textus muscularis nonstriatus) stained with HE: bundles or sheets of spindle-shaped leiomyocytes (leiomyocyti) with medium-eosinophilic cytoplasm and a single central, elongated cigar-shaped nucleus; there is no cross-striation, and in cross-section nuclei are seen only in the widest cell profiles.

Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

How to identify it at the exam
  • Spindle cells with central cigar-shaped nuclei and no cross-striation.
  • Cross-section: variable-diameter round profiles, nuclei only in the widest.
  • Bundles interlacing rather than running in one strict direction.
  • Describe both planes of section — longitudinal and transverse — in the same answer.
Differential diagnosis
  • Skeletal muscle (longitudinal): cross-striation and peripheral nuclei.
  • Cardiac muscle: central nuclei but branching cells and intercalated discs.
  • Tendon: parallel collagen bundles with sharp-ended nuclei and no cytoplasm.
  • Myofibroblasts or dense connective tissue: collagen dominates and the nuclei are more slender and wavy.

V.17Skeletal muscle (cross section, HE)

Core answerA transverse section of skeletal muscle stained with HE: fascicles of polygonal muscle fibres (myofibrae striatae) of varying diameter, each with eosinophilic sarcoplasm and several flattened nuclei displaced to the periphery under the sarcolemma, with endomysium between the fibres and perimysium between the fascicles.

In HE the axoplasm is eosinophilic while the lipid myelin is dissolved during preparation, so the nerve reads as bundles of pink wavy ribbons separated by pale clefts and dotted with elongated blue nuclei.

  • Smooth muscle, longitudinal section: spindle cells with a single central cigar-shaped nucleus and homogeneous eosinophilic cytoplasm; there is no perineurium and no fascicular architecture, and the cells form one continuous sheet.
  • Tendon: dense regular collagen bundles are strongly eosinophilic, but only rows of fibroblast nuclei lie between them; there are no axons, no endoneurium and no perineurium, and the bundles are straight rather than wavy.
  • Skeletal muscle, longitudinal section: cross-striations and multiple peripheral nuclei; the fibres are much thicker and unbranched.
  • Peripheral nerve, cross section: same organ, but the fascicles are round and the fibres are dots, not ribbons.

  • Large vein: thin media, thickest adventitia with longitudinal collagen and smooth muscle bundles, irregular collapsed lumen, valves possible.
  • Pulmonary trunk: also elastic, but its media is thinner because pulmonary arterial pressure is far lower than aortic pressure.
  • Umbilical artery: a muscular artery whose media contains no elastic membranes at all, although it is a large vessel.

V.32Aorta (resorcin-fuchsin)

Core answerThe same aortic wall stained with resorcin-fuchsin, a selective elastic stain that colours elastin brown-purple to blue-black. The outcome is a media of dozens of continuous wavy dark lamellae running around the lumen, with unstained smooth muscle cells between them: this is the definitive histological proof that the vessel is an elastic artery.

  • Each dark wavy line is one fenestrated elastic membrane (membrana elastica fenestrata); small discontinuities along the line are its fenestrae.
  • Between the membranes lie the smooth muscle cells, unstained or only faintly counterstained, their elongated nuclei lying parallel to the lumen.
  • The internal elastic membrane is the first dark lamella of the series and the external elastic membrane the last; there is no sharp change of structure across either, which is typical of elastic arteries.

Reading the colours

How to identify it at the exam
  • Dozens of parallel, evenly spaced, wavy dark lamellae filling a thick media: elastic stain of an elastic artery, i.e. aorta or its large branches.
  • The number of membranes, not their presence, is the discriminator: many = elastic artery, one or two = muscular artery.
  • The dark bands are continuous around the lumen and give the wall its corrugated ("accordion") profile.
  • If the dark material is a fine network in the adventitia only, you are looking at a vein or a small vessel.
Differential diagnosis
  • Muscular artery with the same stain: only the internal elastic membrane (and sometimes an external one) is strongly coloured, so the media shows 1–2 lines instead of dozens.
  • Orcein instead of resorcin-fuchsin: the same target, but red-brown rather than blue-black; never read the colour as a different structure.
  • Verhoeff's haematoxylin: elastin is black, but nuclei are black too, which mimics numerous fine lamellae if the section is over-stained.
  • HE section of the same aorta: architecture is identical, but the elastic membranes are merely eosinophilic and cannot be counted reliably.

V.33Artery, vein (HE)

Core answerThe slide shows an artery and its companion vein side by side in transverse section, HE. The artery has a thick wall, a small round uncollapsed lumen and a muscular media, so its wall-to-lumen ratio is high; the vein has a thin wall, a large irregular collapsed lumen, a thin media and an adventitia that is its thickest coat. Both are paired because they share one connective tissue sheath in a neurovascular bundle.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Three-point rule: lumen shape (round vs collapsed), wall-to-lumen ratio (high vs low), which coat is thickest (media vs adventitia).
  • The wavy internal elastic membrane is the artery's signature; the vein has nothing comparable.
  • The vein's wall fades into the surrounding connective tissue, the artery's adventitia is a distinct boundary.
  • Both vessels have three coats — the slide is a comparison, so name both sets, not just the artery.
Differential diagnosis
  • Elastic artery (aorta): no single internal elastic membrane; instead the media is striped by dozens of wavy elastic bands (see V.31 and V.32).
  • Large vein: adventitia is enormously thickened and contains longitudinal smooth muscle bundles; medium veins have valves.
  • Lymphatic vessel: even thinner wall, no erythrocytes in the lumen, numerous valves, irregular calibre.
  • Peripheral nerve: no lumen at all, only fascicles and sheaths.

  • Nerve: the wavy fascicles of a nerve may catch the eye, but there is no lumen and no elastic membrane.

Erythrocytes dominate the field; leucocytes and platelets are the minority to be identified one by one.

Reading the colours

How to identify it at the exam
  • Erythrocytes have no nucleus; every nucleated cell in the field is a leucocyte.
  • Classify each leucocyte in two steps: nucleus shape first, then granule colour.
  • Neutrophil = lobed nucleus + neutral pink granules; eosinophil = bilobed + brick-red granules; basophil = obscured nucleus + dark blue-black granules.
  • Lymphocyte = round dense nucleus with a thin rim; monocyte = largest cell, indented nucleus, abundant grey-blue cytoplasm.
  • Platelets are anucleate fragments, not cells, and must not be counted as white cells.
Differential diagnosis
  • Monocyte versus large lymphocyte: the monocyte has a folded or kidney-shaped nucleus, more cytoplasm and vacuoles; the large lymphocyte still has a round nucleus occupying most of the cell.
  • Eosinophil versus neutrophil with toxic granulation: eosinophil granules are larger, uniform and brick-red, and the nucleus has two lobes.
  • Basophil versus stain precipitate: precipitate lies on top of cells and is irregular and structureless, whereas basophil granules belong to one cell and surround its nucleus.
  • Crenated erythrocytes (spiculated artefact of drying) must not be reported as pathological poikilocytosis on a poor smear.

  • Bone trabeculae plus a densely cellular space filled with several lineages at once = bone marrow.

  • Trachea: a single type of epithelium (respiratory) throughout, C-shaped hyaline cartilage, no stratified squamous lining and no mucosal folds of this kind.
  • Oesophagus: stratified squamous non-keratinized epithelium everywhere, no cartilage, muscularis mucosae, submucosa and two layers of muscularis externa with skeletal muscle in the upper third.
  • Bronchus: cartilage present only as irregular plates around the whole circumference, respiratory epithelium throughout, no striated muscle.
  • Epiglottis alone: elastic cartilage with two epithelial surfaces (lingual and laryngeal) and taste buds on the lingual side.

V.43Trachea (HE)

Core answerThe slide is the trachea in transverse section, HE: a hollow airway lined by respiratory epithelium resting on a conspicuously thick basement membrane, with seromucous glands in the submucosa and a series of C-shaped hyaline cartilage rings that are open posteriorly and closed there by the trachealis muscle. The C-shape is visible only because the section is transverse.

High-power key features

  • Epithelium: ciliated columnar cells, goblet cells and basal cells; the cilia are the proof that this is the respiratory tract and not the oesophagus.
  • Glands: mixed seromucous glands with characteristic serous demilunes capping mucous tubules; they keep the surface moist and trap particles.
  • Cartilage: hyaline, with a perichondrium and chondrocytes in isogenous groups; the annular ligaments between successive rings are dense fibrous tissue.
  • Membranous wall: bundles of smooth muscle (trachealis) with elastic and collagen fibres; contraction narrows the lumen, as in coughing.

How to identify it at the exam
  • C-shaped hyaline cartilage with a posterior gap filled by smooth muscle: trachea, no alternative.
  • Respiratory epithelium on a thick basement membrane, with goblet cells and cilia.
  • Seromucous glands with serous demilunes in the submucosa.
  • The lumen is held open; there is no muscularis mucosae and no stratified squamous epithelium.
Differential diagnosis
  • Oesophagus: stratified squamous non-keratinized epithelium, a muscularis mucosae, two layers of muscularis externa (skeletal in the upper third) and no cartilage — the differences are tabulated above.
  • Bronchus: cartilage is present as irregular plates around the entire circumference rather than one C-shaped ring, smooth muscle forms a complete layer inside the cartilage, and the lumen is smaller.
  • Bronchiole: no cartilage, no glands and no goblet cells; a thick layer of smooth muscle and a folded mucosa identify it.
  • Larynx: contains stratified squamous epithelium over the vocal folds and striated muscle besides cartilage.

V.44Lung (HE)

Core answerThe slide is lung, HE: a spongy organ of alveoli — thin-walled air spaces separated by capillary-rich interalveolar septa — together with bronchioles, respiratory bronchioles and alveolar ducts, and a pleural surface covered by mesothelium. In the fields where alveoli dominate there is no cartilage and no gland, which is what separates lung from trachea and bronchus.

High-power key features

  • Type I pneumocyte: extremely flattened cell; its nucleus is flat and its cytoplasm thins to a film over the capillary, covering most of the alveolar surface.
  • Type II pneumocyte: cuboidal cell bulging into the alveolar lumen, with vacuolated (lamellar) cytoplasm; it secretes surfactant and is the progenitor of type I cells.
  • Alveolar macrophage (dust cell): large, free or attached cell with phagocytosed particles; it is the lung's scavenger.
  • Interalveolar septum: a continuous capillary sandwiched between type I cells, with reticular and elastic fibres, fibroblasts and Kohn's pores that connect neighbouring alveoli.

The blood-gas barrier

How to identify it at the exam
  • Empty alveolar spaces of different sizes separated by thin capillary-rich septa: lung parenchyma.
  • An airway with no cartilage and no glands = bronchiole, i.e. inside the lung.
  • Type II pneumocytes (cuboidal, bulging) and alveolar macrophages identify the alveolar wall at high power.
  • Pleura at the edge confirms the organ; anthracotic pigment is a normal finding in adults.
Differential diagnosis
  • Trachea or bronchus: cartilage is present — as C-shaped rings in the trachea, as irregular plates in a bronchus — so their presence excludes the purely alveolar part of the lung.
  • Oesophagus: a muscular tube with stratified squamous epithelium; no air spaces at all.
  • Adipose tissue: also "empty spaces", but they are round, of uniform size, each with a nucleus at the margin, and there are no septa with capillaries.
  • Emphysema: enlarged air spaces with destroyed septa — a pathological version of the same architecture, not a different organ.

V.45Lung (orcein + H)

Core answerThe same lung stained with orcein, which colours elastic fibres red-brown, and haematoxylin, which colours nuclei blue. The point of the slide is the elastic skeleton of the lung: elastic fibres in the interalveolar septa, at the mouths of the alveoli, along the bronchioles, around the vessels and, most obviously, in the pleura, whose continuous elastic layer is a reliable landmark.

  • Pleura: mesothelium on the surface, then a continuous elastic lamina, then connective tissue — a layered structure that identifies the organ's surface.
  • Bronchiole: a ring of dense elastic fibres in the lamina propria, just inside the smooth muscle.

  • Elastic fibres are densest at the alveolar mouths and in the bronchiole wall — exactly where mechanical stress is greatest.

  • Elastic aorta (V.32): elastic fibres also demonstrated, but they form dozens of parallel lamellae in a vessel wall, not a mesh around air spaces.
  • Elastic cartilage: orcein-positive too, but the elastic fibres lie in a chondromucoid matrix with chondrocytes in lacunae.
  • Collagen stain or trichrome: colours collagen, not elastin — the pattern would be a blue/green fibrous background instead of a discrete fibre net.

V.46Umbilical cord (HE)

Core answerThe slide is the umbilical cord (funiculus umbilicalis) in transverse section, HE: two umbilical arteries and one umbilical vein embedded in Wharton's jelly, a mucous connective tissue, and covered by a simple squamous-to-cuboidal amniotic epithelium. There are no nerves and no other structures in a normal cord.

High-power key features

  • Umbilical artery: a very thick media of smooth muscle, but the wall is remarkable for a negative feature — it contains no elastic membranes, which is unusual for an artery of this size and is a useful discriminator in favour of the umbilical cord.
  • Umbilical vein: thin media, wide lumen, frequently folded on itself; no valves.
  • Wharton's jelly: pale, myxoid, almost empty-looking matrix with scattered spindle-shaped fibroblasts and macrophages; there are no adipocytes and no dense collagen bundles.
  • Amniotic epithelium: one layer of flat to cuboidal cells; no cilia, no goblet cells, no keratin.

Reading the colours

How to identify it at the exam
  • Two arteries and one vein in a myxoid stroma, covered by a single epithelial layer: umbilical cord.
  • Wharton's jelly — mucous connective tissue with almost no cells — replaces ordinary connective tissue.
  • No nerves, no skeletal muscle, no duct with a thick muscle coat: the cord is only vessels plus jelly.
  • The umbilical arteries lack elastic membranes, which distinguishes them from systemic muscular arteries.
Differential diagnosis
  • Spermatic cord (V.35): contains the ductus deferens (tiny lumen, very thick three-layered muscle coat, pseudostratified epithelium), a pampiniform venous plexus, striated cremaster muscle and fascial coverings — none of which is present here.
  • Placenta (V.47): chorionic villi with trophoblast and intervillous maternal blood; there is no villus here.
  • Artery and vein slide (V.33): two systemic vessels with internal elastic membranes and a connective tissue stroma, not three vessels in Wharton's jelly.
  • Mucoid connective tissue elsewhere (e.g. vitreous): has no amniotic epithelium and no paired arteries.

V.47Placenta (HE)

Core answerThe slide is the placenta, HE: a fetal part — the chorionic plate with its chorionic villi — and a maternal part — the decidual plate — with intervillous spaces between the villi filled with maternal blood. Each villus has a core of fetal connective tissue with capillaries and Hofbauer cells and is covered by syncytiotrophoblast, beneath which a cytotrophoblast layer persists during the first half of pregnancy.

High-power key features

  • Syncytiotrophoblast: a continuous multinucleated eosinophilic layer with a brush border on the villus surface; it is the interface with maternal blood and the source of hCG and other placental hormones.
  • Cytotrophoblast (Langhans cells): a single cuboidal layer beneath the syncytium, continuous only in the first half of pregnancy and important for regeneration.
  • Villus core: loose, jelly-like connective tissue with fetal capillaries, fibroblasts, myofibroblasts and Hofbauer cells — specialised macrophages that regulate local immune reactions and vascular growth.
  • Fibrinoid: amorphous, strongly eosinophilic material on and around villi (Langhans', Rohr's and Nitabuch's fibrinoid), which supports the villi and seals damaged surfaces.

The placental barrier

How to identify it at the exam
  • Villi with a double trophoblast cover, a cellular core and fetal capillaries, floating in maternal blood: placenta.
  • Hofbauer cells in the villus core and fibrinoid on the villus surface are the two specific details.
  • Two parts must be named: the fetal chorionic plate with villi and the maternal decidual plate with septa.
  • The intervillous space has no endothelial lining — that is what "haemochorionic" means.
Differential diagnosis
  • Umbilical cord (V.46): three vessels in Wharton's jelly with an amniotic epithelial surface; no villi and no intervillous space.
  • Uterine endometrium: glands, spiral arteries and stroma; no trophoblast and no villi.
  • Amnion and chorion laeve: membranes without villi; the amnion is a simple epithelium on connective tissue.
  • Hydatidiform mole: oedematous, avascular villi with trophoblast proliferation — a pathological exaggeration of the normal villous architecture.

V.48Chicken embryo (HE)

Core answerThe slide is a transverse section through the body of a chicken embryo at the somite stage, HE: a whole-body cross-section in which the three germ layers are present at once and the axial organs lie in a fixed order — neural tube dorsally in the midline, notochord immediately ventral to it, somites flanking them, the dorsal aortae and the endodermal gut tube below, and the intraembryonic coelom between the somatic and splanchnic layers of the lateral plate mesoderm.

High-power key features

  • Neural tube: a thick wall of pseudostratified neuroepithelium around a narrow neural canal. The sulcus limitans divides its wall into a dorsal alar plate (sensory) and a ventral basal plate (motor).
  • Notochord: a solid rod of cells immediately ventral to the neural tube, enclosed in a sheath; it is the embryonic inducer of the neural plate and the axial skeleton.
  • Somite: a compact, rounded mass of paraxial mesoderm lateral to the neural tube, with an epithelium-like surface; it is the segmental unit of the body.
  • Dorsal aortae: small paired vessels lined by endothelium, ventral to the somites and lateral to the gut; blood cells may be visible inside the lumen.

Reading the colours

How to identify it at the exam
  • Find the neural tube at the midline; the notochord is immediately ventral to it and the somites are lateral to both.
  • All three germ layers must be identifiable: ectoderm on the surface, mesoderm between, endoderm lining the gut tube.
  • The presence of a notochord excludes adult central nervous tissue, and the presence of a gut tube plus somites excludes a placental or cord section.
  • The section is whole-body: skin and body wall, gut and neural axis are present in one field.
Differential diagnosis
  • Adult spinal cord section: a neural tube-like structure, but there is no notochord, no somites, no surface ectoderm, and the cord is surrounded by meninges, bone and muscle.
  • Umbilical cord or placenta (V.46, V.47): extraembryonic structures; no neural tube, no notochord, no somites.
  • Another level of the same embryo: more cranial sections show the pharyngeal arches and heart, more caudal ones the tail bud; the axial organs are the constant features.
  • Teratoma: contains all three germ layers but disorganised, without an axis, a neural tube or a notochord.
