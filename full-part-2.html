II

Anatomy of the Lower Limb

The pelvic ring and its diameters, hip, knee, ankle and foot, the arches and surgical lines of the foot, plus the arteries, veins, plexuses and every topographic region from the femoral triangle to the sole.

II.1The bony pelvis: hip bone, sacrum and coccyx.

Core answerThe bony pelvis (pelvis ossea) is a ring formed by the two hip bones (os coxae), the sacrum and the coccyx, united by the two sacroiliac joints and the pubic symphysis, and completed by the sacrotuberous and sacrospinous ligaments. Each hip bone is a composite of three bones — ilium, ischium and pubis — which meet in the acetabulum and fuse there during puberty and early adult life. The sacrum is five fused sacral vertebrae; the coccyx is three to four rudimentary vertebrae. The ring transmits the weight of the trunk to the lower limbs, protects the pelvic viscera and gives attachment to the muscles of the trunk, gluteal region and thigh.

1.General plan, orientation and functions

The pelvis is the part of the trunk below the abdomen. Its skeleton is a bony ring: behind, the sacrum and coccyx are wedged between the two hip bones; in front the hip bones meet at the pubic symphysis; the ring is closed posterolaterally by the sacrotuberous and sacrospinous ligaments, which convert the sciatic notches of the hip bone into the greater and lesser sciatic foramina. The internal (pelvic) surfaces face the pelvic cavity, the external surfaces belong to the gluteal region of the lower limb.

In the anatomical position the pelvis is oriented so that the anterior superior iliac spines and the front edge of the top of the pubic symphysis lie in the same vertical plane. Therefore the plane of the pelvic inlet is tilted and faces anterosuperiorly, while the bodies of the pubic bones and the pubic arch lie almost horizontally, facing the ground. This orientation explains why the inlet is a sloping plane (see II.2) and why the pelvic floor is concave downwards.

  • Transmission of load: body weight passes through the sacrum to the acetabula and the femora; in sitting, through the ischial tuberosities.
  • Protection: of the bladder, rectum and genital organs.
  • Attachment: abdominal wall, back and gluteal muscles, thigh muscles, pelvic floor and perineal muscles.
  • Obstetric function: the bony birth canal is the least distensible part of the passage of the fetus.

2.The hip bone (os coxae)

The hip bone is a flat, irregular bone formed by the fusion of three primary bones. At birth the three parts are separate and meet in the triradiate cartilage inside the acetabulum; fusion starts in puberty and is completed in early adult life, leaving the acetabular floor as a thin plate of bone. The ilium lies superiorly, the pubis anteroinferiorly and the ischium posteroinferiorly.

3.The acetabulum and the obturator foramen

The acetabulum is the cup-shaped socket on the lateral surface of the hip bone, formed where ilium, ischium and pubis fuse. Its wall has a nonarticular and an articular part. The nonarticular part is the rough acetabular fossa in the central and inferior part of the floor, continuous with the acetabular notch at the lower margin of the cup. The articular part is the smooth crescent-shaped lunate surface, broadest superiorly where most body weight is transmitted; it is deficient inferiorly at the notch. The notch is bridged by the transverse acetabular ligament, which turns it into the acetabular foramen. The fossa gives attachment to the ligament of the head of the femur; vessels and nerves reach the joint through the notch.

The obturator foramen is a large, roughly oval opening enclosed by the pubis and ischium. It is closed by the obturator membrane except at its superolateral corner, where a gap between the membrane and the obturator groove of the superior pubic ramus forms the obturator canal for the obturator nerve and vessels. The membrane gives origin to the obturator internus (deep surface) and obturator externus (external surface) muscles.

4.The sacrum

The sacrum (os sacrum) is a single wedge-shaped bone formed by the fusion of five sacral vertebrae; it is the keystone of the pelvic ring. Its base faces upwards and articulates with the fifth lumbar vertebra through the L5/S1 intervertebral disc and the superior articular processes. The anterior projecting upper margin of the base is the sacral promontory, an important obstetric landmark. The lateral expansions of the base are the alae, which carry the auricular surfaces for the sacroiliac joints. The apex faces downwards and articulates with the coccyx.

  • Pelvic surface: concave, smooth, with the four pairs of anterior sacral foramina for the anterior rami of S1–S4 and the lateral sacral vessels; the ridges between them are fused intervertebral discs.
  • Dorsal surface: convex, with the median sacral crest (fused spinous processes), the paired lateral sacral crests (fused transverse processes) and the four pairs of posterior sacral foramina.
  • Sacral canal: the continuation of the vertebral canal; it ends below as the sacral hiatus, where the laminae of the lowest sacral vertebra are missing.
  • Auricular surface: the ear-shaped articular area on the lateral part, facing the ilium; behind it is the rough sacral tuberosity for the interosseous sacroiliac ligaments.
  • Lateral margin: forms part of the margin of the greater sciatic foramen and gives attachment to the sacrotuberous and sacrospinous ligaments.

The number of sacral vertebrae may vary: the first sacral vertebra can remain separate from the rest (lumbarisation of S1), or the fifth lumbar vertebra may be fused to the sacrum (sacralisation of L5). Such variants alter the radiological count of vertebrae and the apparent level of the sacroiliac joints. The sacral hiatus is the route of caudal epidural anaesthesia; it is closed by the sacrococcygeal membrane and flanked by the sacral cornua.

5.The coccyx

The coccyx (os coccygis) is the remains of the tail: three to four rudimentary vertebrae, usually fused into one bone. Its base bears the coccygeal cornua, which articulate with the sacral cornua, and transverse processes; its apex is free. The first coccygeal vertebra may remain separate. The coccyx gives attachment to the anococcygeal ligament, the levator ani and coccygeus muscles, part of the gluteus maximus and the sacrotuberous ligament. It is freely movable and is pushed backwards during defecation and childbirth; a fall onto the buttocks may fracture or dislocate it, causing coccygodynia.

Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Pelvic fractures are classified by the number of breaks in the bony ring: type 1 = no ring disruption (iliac wing); type 2 = single break (e.g. pubic ramus fracture with symphyseal diastasis); type 3 = double break; type 4 = acetabular fractures. Concealed blood loss is the main early danger.
  • Iliac crest is a standard site for autologous bone graft (cancellous bone and marrow); the apophysis of the crest appears and fuses in adolescence and must not be mistaken for a fracture.
  • Sacral hiatus may be absent or closed, and the sacrum may show spina bifida occulta; a sacral fracture is easily missed on plain films, so CT is used.
  • On an AP pelvic radiograph the ring, the sacroiliac joints, the symphysis, the obturator foramina, the teardrop and the iliopectineal line are checked systematically; asymmetry of the obturator foramina suggests a ring injury.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Which structures pass through the greater sciatic foramen? — Above piriformis: superior gluteal nerve and vessels; below piriformis: sciatic nerve, inferior gluteal nerve and vessels, pudendal nerve, internal pudendal vessels, posterior cutaneous nerve of thigh, nerves to obturator internus and quadratus femoris.
  • Why is the acetabular notch important? — It transmits vessels and nerves to the joint and is closed by the transverse acetabular ligament; the lunate surface is deficient here.
  • Difference between the greater and lesser sciatic foramina — the greater is above the sacrospinous ligament and ischial spine, the lesser is below them, between the sacrospinous and sacrotuberous ligaments.
  • How do you sex a pelvis? — Inlet shape, subpubic angle, ischial spines, sacral proportions, outlet and false pelvis proportions; the female pelvis is built for parturition.

II.2The structure and diameters of the bony pelvis.

Core answerThe pelvic brim (linea terminalis) divides the pelvis into a greater (false) pelvis above and a lesser (true) pelvis below. The true pelvis has an inlet, a cavity and an outlet, and it is described by three sets of diameters — anteroposterior, transverse and oblique. In the inlet the narrowest anteroposterior diameter is the obstetric conjugate (about 11 cm), the transverse diameter is the largest (about 13 cm) and the oblique diameters are about 12 cm; the diagonal conjugate (about 12.5 cm) is the clinically measurable diameter from which the obstetric conjugate is estimated. The narrowest part of the canal is at the ischial spines (interspinous distance, about 10 cm).

1.Division of the pelvis: false and true pelvis

The linea terminalis is the oblique line that runs on each side from the sacral promontory across the ala of the sacrum and the sacroiliac joint to the arcuate line of the ilium, then along the pecten pubis (pectineal line) to the pubic crest and the pubic symphysis. It is the bony margin of the pelvic inlet (aditus pelvis).

  • Greater (false) pelvis (pelvis major): the space above the inlet, between the iliac alae and below the abdominal cavity. It contains abdominal organs (caecum, sigmoid colon, loops of small intestine) and is not part of the birth canal.
  • Lesser (true) pelvis (pelvis minor): the bony-walled canal between the inlet and the outlet. It contains the pelvic viscera and the pelvic floor closes its outlet.

The margin of the inlet is: promontory and ala of the sacrum posteriorly, arcuate line and pecten pubis laterally, pubic crest and pubic symphysis anteriorly. The margin of the outlet is: the tip of the coccyx and the inferior margin of the sacrotuberous ligament posteriorly, the ischial tuberosities laterally, and the pubic arch (inferior pubic ligament) anteriorly. Between inlet and outlet the true pelvis has a wall made of the sacrum, coccyx, hip bones below the linea terminalis, the sacrotuberous and sacrospinous ligaments and the obturator internus and piriformis muscles.

4.Planes and axes of the pelvis

  • The plane of the pelvic inlet is inclined at about 60° to the horizontal in the standing position, so that the inlet looks anterosuperiorly.
  • The plane of the pelvic outlet is inclined at about 15° to the horizontal; the two planes are therefore not parallel.
  • The axis of the inlet is perpendicular to the inlet plane and points posteroinferiorly; the axis of the outlet is perpendicular to the outlet plane and points anteroinferiorly. The pelvic axis is the curve joining these axes — it is concave posteriorly and is followed by the fetal head (the obstetric curve).
  • The pelvic inclination is described clinically by the relation of the inlet plane to the horizontal; it can be altered by posture, hip flexion and lumbar lordosis, which is why the lithotomy or squatting position widens the effective outlet.

5.Clinical use and measurement

The diameters cannot be measured directly through the intact soft tissues, so clinical pelvimetry estimates them from palpable landmarks (diagonal conjugate, ischial spines, sacral curve, subpubic angle) and imaging measures them exactly. The diagonal conjugate is the only diameter that can be reached digitally; the obstetric conjugate is then calculated. MRI gives the diameters without ionising radiation and is therefore preferred in pregnancy; CT is used after trauma. A pelvis whose diameters are below the conventional values is called a contracted pelvis and predisposes to obstructed labour, while a very wide subpubic angle and a roomy outlet predispose to rapid delivery.

  • Obstetric conjugate is the single most important diameter: it is the narrowest fixed AP measurement the fetal head must pass.
  • Contracted pelvis — one or more diameters below the normal range; causes prolonged labour, arrest of descent and fetal distress; assessed by clinical pelvimetry, MRI or CT.

Likely follow-up questions
  • Why is the transverse diameter of the inlet larger than the AP? — Because the promontory projects into the inlet in the midline, shortening the AP diameters while the linea terminalis is wide transversely.
  • How do you measure the true conjugate clinically? — By the diagonal conjugate on vaginal examination, then subtracting about 1.5–2 cm.
  • Where is the narrowest part of the pelvic canal? — At the level of the ischial spines (interspinous distance, about 10 cm).
  • What closes the pelvic outlet? — The pelvic floor (levator ani and coccygeus with their fasciae), not bone.

II.3The joints and ligaments of the pelvis. The statics and radiological anatomy of the pelvis.

Core answerThe pelvic ring contains three joints: the two sacroiliac joints (a synovial joint anteriorly combined with a very strong posterior syndesmosis), the pubic symphysis (secondary cartilaginous) and the sacrococcygeal joint. They are reinforced by the interosseous, anterior and posterior sacroiliac ligaments, the iliolumbar, sacrotuberous and sacrospinous ligaments, and the superior and inferior pubic ligaments. Together they make the ring a self-locking arch: the sacrum is suspended between the ilia, body weight drives it into nutation, and the ligaments convert this into a stable, elastic transmission of load to the acetabula. Radiology uses the AP pelvic film (teardrop, Shenton's line, iliopectineal line), inlet and outlet views, oblique views, CT and MRI.

1.The sacroiliac joint

The sacroiliac joint is the articulation of the auricular surface of the sacrum with the matching auricular surface of the ilium. It is a hybrid: a narrow synovial cavity exists in the anterior part (diarthrosis), while the posterior and superior part is a syndesmosis filled by the strongest of its ligaments. The surfaces are ear-shaped and irregularly ridged, which gives the joint a high resistance to shear. The capsule is short and tight.

2.Pubic symphysis, sacrococcygeal joint and obturator membrane

The pubic symphysis (symphysis pubica) is a secondary cartilaginous joint between the symphyseal surfaces of the two pubic bones. Each surface is covered by hyaline cartilage and the two are united by a fibrocartilaginous interpubic disc, which is often a slit-like cavity in its upper part. It is reinforced above by the superior pubic ligament and below by the inferior (arcuate) pubic ligament, which runs across the subpubic angle and forms the upper border of the pubic arch. Movement is minimal (a few millimetres), but during pregnancy relaxation allows a small separation; a gap above about 10 mm is called diastasis.

The sacrococcygeal joint is a symphysis between the apex of the sacrum and the base of the coccyx, with an intervening fibrocartilaginous disc. It is held by anterior, posterior and lateral sacrococcygeal ligaments, and it allows the coccyx to be flexed forwards (defecation) and pushed backwards (childbirth). The obturator membrane is a fibrous sheet closing the obturator foramen; its superolateral defect, with the obturator groove, forms the obturator canal for the obturator nerve and vessels.

3.Statics of the pelvis

The pelvis behaves as an arch or ring: the sacrum is the keystone, the hip bones are the side pillars and the pubic symphysis is the tie-beam. Under vertical load the sacrum tends to move downwards, forwards and into nutation between the ilia; the sacroiliac ligaments and the sacrotuberous and sacrospinous ligaments resist this and give the joint its self-locking, force-closing character. The load path is therefore:

Body weight → L5/S1 → sacrum → sacroiliac joints → arcuate line and ilium → acetabulum → femoral head ⇒ standing and walking; when sitting → ischial tuberosities.

  • Trabecular lines in the ilium follow the two force paths: one from the sacroiliac joint to the acetabulum (along the arcuate line) and one from the sacroiliac joint to the ischial tuberosity; in the sacrum the trabeculae run from the upper end plate towards the auricular surfaces.
  • Compression and tension: the upper part of the pelvic ring is compressed while the lower part, especially the pubic region, is kept in tension by the arcuate ligament.
  • Muscles assist statics: the pelvic floor and abdominal wall act as a closed pressure chamber; the gluteus maximus and hamstrings extend the hip and stabilise the sacroiliac joint; the iliopsoas and rectus femoris pull anteriorly; the iliofemoral ligament prevents the pelvis from tilting backwards in the upright stance without muscular effort.
  • Hip flexion and lumbar lordosis alter the pelvic inclination; in the later months of pregnancy the centre of gravity shifts forward and lumbar lordosis increases to keep the trunk balanced over the hip joints.

4.Radiological anatomy of the pelvis

  • AP radiograph: the intact ring should be traced — iliac crests, sacroiliac joints (their width and symmetry), symphysis pubis, obturator foramina (symmetrical in shape and size), sacral foramina and arcuate lines. The iliopectineal line (from the arcuate line to the pubic crest) and the ilioischial line outline the acetabulum; Köhler's teardrop is the U-shaped shadow of the acetabular floor and the medial wall of the acetabulum.
  • Shenton's line: the continuous curve formed by the medial border of the femoral neck and the superior border of the obturator foramen; a break indicates a fracture of the neck or a dislocation.
  • Inlet and outlet views: the inlet view (tube tilted caudally) shows the pelvic ring and the AP displacement of fractures; the outlet view (tube tilted cranially) shows the sacrum, the symphysis and vertical displacement.
  • Oblique (Judet) views of the acetabulum and the Lauenstein view of the hip (limb abducted and laterally rotated) show the acetabular rim, the fovea and the femoral head in profile.
  • CT is the method of choice for pelvic ring and acetabular fractures and for sacral fractures; MRI shows the bone marrow, the sacroiliac joints in inflammation and is used for obstetric pelvimetry; ultrasound is the screening method for the infant hip.
  • In the child the triradiate cartilage of the acetabulum is radiolucent and closes during puberty; in the adolescent the iliac crest apophysis is visible.
Must-memorise
  • Sacroiliac joint = synovial anteriorly + syndesmosis posteriorly; strongest ligament = interosseous sacroiliac.
  • Pubic symphysis = secondary cartilaginous joint; superior and inferior (arcuate) pubic ligaments.
  • Sacrotuberous and sacrospinous ligaments resist upward tilting of the sacrum and create the sciatic foramina.
  • Weight path: vertebral column → sacrum → sacroiliac joint → ilium → acetabulum → femur; sitting → ischial tuberosities.
  • Radiology: teardrop, Shenton's line, iliopectineal and ilioischial lines; inlet and outlet views.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Sacroiliac dysfunction causes low back and buttock pain, often referred to the groin or posterior thigh; it is a common cause of pain in pregnancy.
  • Ankylosing spondylitis (HLA-B27-associated) begins with sacroiliitis: blurring, erosions, sclerosis and finally fusion of the joint — a key radiological sign on the AP film.
  • Symphyseal diastasis after childbirth or trauma widens the symphysis and destabilises the ring; disruption of the ring can be double (pubic rami plus posterior injury) and is associated with major retroperitoneal bleeding.
  • Coccygodynia after a fall on the buttocks; the coccyx is examined on a lateral radiograph.
Likely follow-up questions
  • What kind of joint is the sacroiliac joint? — A synovial joint in front combined with a strong posterior syndesmosis; the auricular surfaces are covered by fibrocartilage and the interosseous ligament is the strongest bond.
  • What is nutation? — Anterior/inferior movement of the promontory with posterior movement of the apex, decreasing the AP diameter of the inlet; counternutation is the reverse.
  • Which ligaments stabilise the pelvis in standing? — Interosseous and posterior sacroiliac, sacrotuberous and sacrospinous; the iliofemoral ligament prevents the pelvis from dropping backwards.
  • How does the pelvis transmit weight when sitting? — Through the ischial tuberosities and their ligaments, not through the acetabula.

II.4The hip joint: gross and radiological anatomy, movements and the participating muscles.

Core answerThe hip joint (articulatio coxae) is a synovial ball-and-socket (spheroid) joint between the spherical head of the femur and the acetabulum of the hip bone. It is multiaxial — flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, medial and lateral rotation and circumduction — but it is built for stability and weight-bearing rather than mobility: the acetabulum is deep, the labrum and transverse acetabular ligament enlarge its rim, and a strong capsule is reinforced by the iliofemoral, pubofemoral and ischiofemoral ligaments. Its arterial supply comes mainly from the medial circumflex femoral artery and its innervation from the femoral, obturator and superior gluteal nerves and the nerve to quadratus femoris.

1.Type and articular surfaces

  • Femoral surface: the head of the femur is spherical and covered by hyaline cartilage except at the fovea (fovea capitis) on its medial aspect, where the ligament of the head attaches.
  • Acetabular surface: the crescent-shaped lunate surface, broadest superiorly where the load is greatest; the nonarticular acetabular fossa lies centrally and inferiorly and contains loose connective tissue and fat.
  • The acetabular labrum is a fibrocartilaginous collar on the rim which deepens the socket; inferiorly it bridges the acetabular notch as the transverse acetabular ligament and turns the notch into a foramen.
  • The acetabulum covers nearly the whole hemisphere of the head, so the joint is intrinsically stable; the articular cavity is a narrow slit because the capsule and labrum are tight against the head.

2.Capsule, ligaments and synovial membrane

The fibrous membrane is thick and strong. Medially it attaches to the margin of the acetabulum, the transverse acetabular ligament and the adjacent margin of the obturator foramen; laterally to the intertrochanteric line in front and to the neck just proximal to the intertrochanteric crest behind. The synovial membrane lines the capsule, covers the nonarticular neck inside the joint and forms a sleeve around the ligament of the head; it does not cross the articular cartilage.

In flexion the ligaments unwind and the joint becomes looser.

3.Movements and participating muscles

Because the femoral neck projects at about 125° and slightly forwards, rotation of the femur is best understood as the greater trochanter moving forwards (medial rotation) or backwards (lateral rotation). Ranges: flexion is the most extensive movement (about 120° with the knee flexed), extension is limited to about 10–20° by the iliofemoral ligament, abduction and rotation each reach roughly 30–45°, and adduction is limited by the opposite limb.

4.Blood supply and innervation

  • Arteries: branches of the obturator artery, the medial and lateral circumflex femoral arteries, the superior and inferior gluteal arteries and the first perforating branch of the deep artery of the thigh form a network around the joint. The retinacular (ascending cervical) branches of the medial circumflex femoral artery, running up the neck under the capsule, are the main supply of the adult femoral head in addition to the small artery of the ligament of the head; this is why an intracapsular neck fracture threatens the head.
  • Nerves: articular branches of the femoral nerve, the obturator nerve, the superior gluteal nerve and the nerve to quadratus femoris — a classical illustration of Hilton's law (the nerves crossing a joint supply it and the muscles acting on it).

5.Radiological anatomy

  • AP radiograph of the hip/pelvis: the femoral head sits concentrically in the acetabulum with a uniform joint space; the neck-shaft angle is about 125°; the greater trochanter is level with the centre of the head; the fovea, the acetabular rim and the teardrop (Köhler) are visible; Shenton's line is continuous.
  • Lauenstein view (hip abducted and laterally rotated) profiles the femoral neck and the fovea and is used for neck fractures.
  • Ultrasound is the screening method for the infant hip (dysplasia, effusion); the cartilaginous femoral head is invisible on early radiographs, whereas the ossific nucleus appears in the first months of life.
  • CT with 3D reconstruction is used for acetabular and complex femoral fractures; MRI demonstrates avascular necrosis, labral tears, marrow oedema and joint effusion; arthrography outlines the capsule and the labrum.

6.Clinical correlations

  • Fracture of the femoral neck: intracapsular fractures interrupt the retinacular supply and cause avascular necrosis and non-union; intertrochanteric fractures are extracapsular and heal better. The limb is shortened and laterally rotated.
  • Dislocation: posterior dislocation is the commoner type after a dashboard injury — the limb is shortened, adducted and medially rotated; sciatic nerve injury may occur.
  • Trendelenburg sign: a lesion of the superior gluteal nerve (gluteus medius and minimus) makes the pelvis drop on the swing side during single-leg stance. Trendelenburg gait is a common exam question.
  • Developmental dysplasia of the hip is screened by ultrasound; Legg–Calvé–Perthes disease and slipped capital femoral epiphysis are childhood hip disorders causing limping and pain.
  • Osteoarthritis of the hip produces narrowing of the superior joint space, osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis and cysts; it is a common indication for joint replacement.
Must-memorise
  • Ball-and-socket joint; articular surfaces = head of femur (fovea nonarticular) + lunate surface of the acetabulum.
  • Three ligaments: iliofemoral (strongest), pubofemoral, ischiofemoral; spiral fibres are taut in extension.
  • Main blood supply of the head: retinacular branches of the medial circumflex femoral artery; the artery of the ligament of the head is small.
  • Innervation: femoral, obturator, superior gluteal nerves and nerve to quadratus femoris (Hilton's law).
  • Lateral rotators: piriformis, obturator internus + gemelli, quadratus femoris, obturator externus, gluteus maximus.
  • Abduction and medial rotation: superior gluteal n. (L4–S1).
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Shenton's line broken = fracture of the femoral neck or dislocation.
  • Intracapsular neck fracture → avascular necrosis of the head; an extracapsular (intertrochanteric) fracture preserves the blood supply.
  • Hip effusion is best shown by ultrasound; the joint is the deepest joint of the body and is not easily palpated.
  • Congenital dislocation: limitation of abduction, asymmetry of the gluteal folds, Ortolani/Barlow tests, ultrasound screening.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Why is the hip joint stable? — Deep acetabulum, labrum and transverse acetabular ligament, tight capsule reinforced by three strong ligaments, atmospheric pressure and the large articular surface.
  • Which structures limit extension, adduction and abduction? — Extension by the iliofemoral (with the ischiofemoral) ligament; abduction by the pubofemoral ligament; adduction of the extended limb by the iliofemoral ligament; medial rotation by the ischiofemoral ligament.
  • What happens to the obturator internus tendon? — It bends 90° around the ischium between the ischial spine and tuberosity and passes through the lesser sciatic foramen to the greater trochanter.
  • Where is the fovea and what attaches there? — On the medial surface of the head; the ligament of the head of the femur.

II.5The femur, tibia, fibula. Connections between tibia and fibula.

Core answerThe femur is the longest and strongest bone of the body: head, neck, greater and lesser trochanters, a shaft with the linea aspera and two condyles distally. The tibia is the medial, weight-bearing bone of the leg, with a plateau proximally, a sharp anterior border and the medial malleolus distally; the fibula is the slender, non-weight-bearing lateral bone, ending as the lateral malleolus. Tibia and fibula are joined by the proximal tibiofibular joint (synovial), the interosseous membrane and the distal tibiofibular syndesmosis; together these hold the two bones as one unit and form the ankle mortise.

1.The femur

The femur (os femoris) articulates with the acetabulum proximally and with the tibia and patella distally. Its proximal end has a spherical head with the nonarticular fovea for the ligament of the head, a cylindrical neck projecting superomedially at about 125° and slightly forwards, and the greater and lesser trochanters joined anteriorly by the intertrochanteric line and posteriorly by the intertrochanteric crest, which bears the quadrate tubercle. The trochanteric fossa (medial surface of the greater trochanter) lodges the insertion of obturator externus; the lesser trochanter receives the iliopsoas.

The shaft is bowed forwards and is triangular in cross-section, with anterior, medial and lateral surfaces and medial, lateral and posterior borders; the posterior border is the broad rough linea aspera, which splits above into the pectineal line, gluteal tuberosity and the two lips. Distally the femur expands into the medial and lateral condyles separated by the intercondylar fossa, above which are the medial and lateral epicondyles; the adductor tubercle lies just posterosuperior to the medial epicondyle and receives the hamstring part of adductor magnus. Anteriorly the condyles form the patellar surface. The patella, the largest sesamoid bone, lies in the quadriceps tendon: its base receives the quadriceps tendon, its apex gives attachment to the patellar ligament, and its posterior surface has larger lateral and smaller medial facets separated by a ridge.

2.The tibia

The tibia is the medial and much larger bone of the leg and the only one that transmits weight from the knee to the ankle. Proximally it is expanded into medial and lateral condyles whose superior surfaces form the tibial plateau; between them is the intercondylar region with the intercondylar eminence and its medial and lateral tubercles, which gives attachment to the menisci and cruciate ligaments. The medial condyle is larger and better supported over the shaft; its posterior surface bears a groove for semimembranosus. Below the condyles, anteriorly, is the palpable tibial tuberosity for the patellar ligament.

The shaft is triangular with three borders (anterior — the sharp, subcutaneous shin; interosseous; medial) and three surfaces (medial — subcutaneous; lateral; posterior — crossed by the oblique soleal line). Distally the tibia expands into the box-like distal end whose inferior surface is the tibial plafond and whose medial projection is the medial malleolus; the posterior surface of the distal end has a vertical groove for the tendon of tibialis posterior, and the lateral surface has the fibular notch for the distal fibula.

3.The fibula

The fibula is the lateral bone of the leg. It is much thinner than the tibia, is enclosed by muscles except at its ends, and does not take part in the knee joint or in weight-bearing — its role is to give attachment to muscles and to form the lateral wall of the ankle mortise. Its proximal end is the head, which bears a facet for the lateral tibial condyle, the styloid process and an impression for the biceps femoris; the neck separates it from the shaft, and the common fibular nerve lies against the posterolateral aspect of the neck. The shaft has anterior, posterior and interosseous borders and lateral, posterior and medial surfaces; the posterior surface is divided by the medial crest. Distally the fibula ends in the spade-shaped lateral malleolus, whose medial surface bears the facet for the lateral talus, whose posteromedial aspect has the malleolar fossa for the posterior talofibular ligament, and whose posterior surface has a groove for the tendons of fibularis longus and brevis.

5.Clinical correlations

  • Femoral shaft fracture: can lose a large volume of blood into the thigh, causes shortening and external rotation, and may be complicated by fat embolism and by compartment syndrome of the thigh.
  • Femoral neck fracture: intracapsular fractures threaten the retinacular blood supply of the head (see II.4).
  • Tibial plateau fracture: often intra-articular, split or depressed, with associated meniscal and ligamentous damage; CT is used when plain films are equivocal.
  • Tibial shaft fracture: the thin subcutaneous anteromedial surface makes it a common site of an open fracture; the tibia has a poor blood supply in its lower third, so union is slow.
  • Fibular neck injury: the common fibular nerve is damaged by a blow, cast or fracture, producing foot drop and loss of sensation over the lateral leg and dorsum of the foot.
  • Maisonneuve fracture of the proximal fibula is associated with a distal tibiofibular syndesmosis tear and an unstable ankle.
  • Osgood–Schlatter disease: traction apophysitis of the tibial tuberosity in adolescents.
Must-memorise
  • Femur: head, fovea, neck (about 125°), greater and lesser trochanters, intertrochanteric line and crest, linea aspera, condyles, intercondylar fossa, adductor tubercle.
  • Tibia: plateau, intercondylar eminence, tibial tuberosity, anterior border, soleal line, fibular notch, medial malleolus.
  • Fibula: head, styloid process, neck (common fibular nerve), lateral malleolus, malleolar fossa.
  • Three connections: proximal tibiofibular joint (synovial), interosseous membrane (with two apertures), distal tibiofibular syndesmosis.
  • Patella = largest sesamoid bone, inside the quadriceps tendon, largest lateral facet.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Radiology of the knee: the tibial plateau, intercondylar tubercles, patella and fibular head are identified on the AP film; the epiphyseal plates close in late adolescence and must not be mistaken for fractures.
  • Lateral film of the leg: shows the tibial shaft, the fibula and the interosseous membrane; the joint space of the proximal tibiofibular joint is seen.
  • Fibular graft is harvested from the middle third after subperiosteal dissection, preserving the common fibular nerve.
  • Ankle film: the distal tibiofibular overlap and clear space judge the syndesmosis; widening means diastasis.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Why does the fibula not transmit weight? — It has a thin shaft, is enclosed by muscles, and its distal end is tethered to the tibia by the syndesmosis; only a small fraction of the load passes through it.
  • What is the linea aspera and what attaches to it? — A broad posterior crest on the femur: adductors, short head of biceps femoris, vastus medialis and lateralis, and the intermuscular septa.
  • Which nerve is related to the fibular neck? — The common fibular nerve, which is palpable and vulnerable there.
  • What are the apertures of the interosseous membrane for? — The anterior tibial vessels above and the perforating branch of the fibular artery below.

II.6The knee joint: gross and radiological anatomy, movements and participating muscles.

Core answerThe knee joint (articulatio genus) is the largest synovial joint of the body. It is a compound joint: a tibiofemoral part (a modified, bicondylar hinge) and a patellofemoral part (a plane joint between patella and femur). Two fibrocartilaginous menisci improve congruence; the joint is stabilised by the tibial and fibular collateral ligaments and by the anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments. Its main movements are flexion and extension, with rotation of the flexed leg; in full extension the knee is locked by medial rotation of the femur on the tibia and by the change in shape of the femoral articular surfaces, and the popliteus muscle unlocks it.

1.Type and articular surfaces

  • Femoral surfaces: the rounded posterior parts of the two condyles articulate in flexion, the broad flat inferior parts in extension — so the contact area increases and the joint becomes more stable as it extends. Anteriorly the condyles form a V-shaped patellar surface (trochlear groove).
  • Tibial surfaces: the superior surfaces of the medial and lateral condyles (tibial plateau), concave centrally and flatter at the margins; the medial is oval and larger, the lateral circular; between them is the intercondylar region.
  • Patellar surfaces: the posterior surface of the patella with a larger lateral and a smaller medial facet separated by a ridge; the patella directs the pull of the quadriceps anteriorly, preventing tendon wear on the femur.
  • All three articular surfaces share a single articular cavity; the menisci and the cruciate ligaments are inside it or enclosed by its capsule.

2.The menisci

The menisci are C-shaped fibrocartilaginous discs, one on each side, attached by their horns to facets in the intercondylar region of the tibia. The medial meniscus is attached peripherally to the capsule and to the tibial collateral ligament, so it is less mobile and more often injured; the lateral meniscus is not attached to the capsule, is more mobile, and is connected to the popliteus tendon, which passes between it and the capsule. The two are joined anteriorly by the transverse ligament of the knee. The menisci improve congruence between the changing femoral surfaces and the flat tibial plateau, share the load, absorb shock and help to distribute synovial fluid; after meniscectomy the cartilage of the compartment degenerates.

3.Capsule, synovial membrane and bursae

The fibrous membrane is extensive and is partly formed by tendinous expansions. Medially it blends with the tibial collateral ligament and is attached to the medial meniscus; laterally it is separated from the fibular collateral ligament by a bursa and does not attach to the lateral meniscus. Anteriorly it is attached to the margins of the patella and is reinforced by the vastus muscles; posteromedially by the oblique popliteal ligament (an expansion of semimembranosus); anterolaterally by a fibrous expansion of the iliotibial tract. The popliteus tendon passes through an aperture in the posterolateral capsule.

The synovial membrane attaches to the margins of the cartilage and to the upper and lower margins of the menisci. It reflects round the cruciate ligaments, excluding them from the cavity, and anteriorly it covers the infrapatellar fat pad, forming the fringed alar folds and the midline infrapatellar fold. It forms two pouches: the suprapatellar bursa, a large superior extension between femur and quadriceps (so an effusion collects there and distends the suprapatellar region), and the small subpopliteal recess between the lateral meniscus and the popliteus tendon. Separate, non-communicating bursae include the subcutaneous prepatellar and the deep and subcutaneous infrapatellar bursae.

Locking. In the standing position the knee is locked to spare the extensor muscles. Three factors act together: (1) the articular surfaces change from the small curved posterior parts of the condyles in flexion to the broad flat inferior parts in extension, so the joint surfaces become larger and more stable; (2) during the last part of extension the femur rotates medially on the tibia (the "screw-home" movement), which tightens all the ligaments; and (3) the line of gravity passes anterior to the knee joint, which keeps the joint extended. Unlocking is initiated by the popliteus muscle, which laterally rotates the femur on the tibia and so releases the tight ligaments before flexion begins.

6.Radiological anatomy and clinical correlations

  • AP radiograph: femoral and tibial condyles, intercondylar eminence, joint space (medial and lateral compartments should be symmetric), patella superimposed on the distal femur, fibular head. In osteoarthritis the medial compartment is usually the first to narrow.
  • Lateral radiograph: patellofemoral joint space, tibial tuberosity, the infrapatellar fat pad and suprapatellar bursa — a visible effusion or a fat–fluid level indicates an intra-articular fracture or lipohaemarthrosis; the fabella may be present.
  • Sunrise (skyline) view shows the patellofemoral articulation and patellar tracking; MRI is the method of choice for menisci, cruciate and collateral ligaments and cartilage; ultrasound demonstrates effusion, tendons and collateral ligaments; arthroscopy allows direct inspection and treatment.
  • Meniscal tears: vertical, horizontal and bucket-handle patterns; pain, locking, clicking and delayed swelling; MRI is diagnostic.
  • Ligament injuries: the ACL is injured by non-contact pivoting and by valgus force; Lachman's, anterior drawer and pivot shift tests; the PCL by a direct blow on a flexed knee (dashboard injury) with a positive posterior drawer and sag sign.
  • Unhappy triad: combined tear of the tibial collateral ligament, medial meniscus and ACL, after a valgus blow to the lateral knee.
  • Collateral ligament injuries: the tibial collateral ligament is injured by a valgus force, the fibular collateral ligament by a varus force.
Must-memorise
  • Compound joint: tibiofemoral (modified hinge) + patellofemoral (plane); single articular cavity.
  • Medial meniscus is attached to the capsule and tibial collateral ligament (less mobile); lateral meniscus is mobile and related to the popliteus tendon.
  • ACL prevents anterior tibial displacement and hyperextension; PCL prevents posterior displacement and is stronger.
  • Tibial collateral ligament is attached to the medial meniscus; the fibular collateral ligament is cord-like and separated from the capsule by a bursa.
  • Locking = shape change + medial rotation of the femur on the tibia + line of gravity anterior to the joint; unlocking = popliteus.
  • Knee innervation: femoral, obturator, tibial and common fibular nerves.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Suprapatellar effusion: the suprapatellar bursa communicates with the joint, so fluid collects above the patella — best seen on the lateral film and by ultrasound.
  • ACL rupture: a pop is heard, haemarthrosis develops within hours; MRI confirms; reconstruction is indicated in active patients.
  • Meniscal tear: delayed effusion, joint-line tenderness, locking; bucket-handle fragments displace into the intercondylar notch.
  • Osteoarthritis: joint-space narrowing, osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis and cysts, often medial first.
  • Patellar dislocation is usually lateral, because the pull of the quadriceps has a valgus component and the medial patellofemoral ligament is the main restraint.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Why is the knee most stable in extension? — The flat femoral surfaces contact the plateau, the ligaments are tightened by medial femoral rotation and the joint surfaces are largest.
  • What is the screw-home mechanism? — The terminal medial rotation of the femur on the tibia during extension, which locks the joint; it reverses at the start of flexion (popliteus).
  • Which meniscus is more often torn and why? — The medial, because it is fixed to the capsule and tibial collateral ligament and is therefore less mobile.
  • Where do the cruciate ligaments attach? — Intercondylar area of the tibia below; intercondylar fossa of the femur above; they cross in the sagittal plane and are intracapsular but extrasynovial.
  • What does the tibial collateral ligament have to do with meniscal injury? — Its deep fibres attach to the medial meniscus, so valgus injury may tear both (part of the unhappy triad).

II.7The ankle (talocrural) joint: gross and radiological anatomy, movements and participating muscles.

Core answerThe ankle joint (articulatio talocruralis) is a synovial hinge joint between the trochlea of the talus and the ankle mortise formed by the distal end of the tibia, the medial malleolus and the lateral malleolus of the fibula; the fibula is held to the tibia by the distal tibiofibular syndesmosis. It allows dorsiflexion and plantarflexion and is stabilised medially by the strong deltoid ligament and laterally by the anterior and posterior talofibular and calcaneofibular ligaments. Because the talar trochlea is wider anteriorly, the joint is tightest and most stable in dorsiflexion and loosest in plantarflexion, which explains the frequency of inversion sprains.

1.Type, articular surfaces and the ankle mortise

  • Tibial surface: the smooth inferior surface of the distal tibia (the plafond) forms the roof of the mortise; the medial malleolus forms its short medial wall and bears the medial malleolar facet.
  • Fibular surface: the lateral malleolus, which extends further inferiorly than the medial, forms the long lateral wall of the mortise and bears the lateral malleolar facet.
  • Talar surface: the trochlea (superior articular surface) is shaped like a half-cylinder tipped onto its flat side: convex from front to back, slightly concave from side to side, and wider anteriorly than posteriorly; the medial and lateral malleolar surfaces are on its sides. All articular surfaces are covered by hyaline cartilage.
  • The socket is a bracket-shaped mortise; the joint is a hinge because the talus can move essentially only around a transverse axis, while the mortise holds it from the sides.
  • The joint capsule is attached near the articular margins; it is thin anteriorly and posteriorly and is reinforced by the ligaments on the sides.

4.Stability and biomechanics

  • Shape: the trochlea is wider anteriorly than posteriorly, so in dorsiflexion the wide part is wedged in the mortise and the joint is congruent and stable; in plantarflexion the narrow posterior part is in the mortise and the joint is loose — the loose-packed position is plantarflexion, the close-packed position is dorsiflexion.
  • Ligaments: the deltoid ligament is much stronger than the lateral ligament, which is why forced inversion tears the lateral ligament while forced eversion usually fractures the medial malleolus instead of tearing the deltoid.
  • Syndesmosis: the distal tibiofibular ligaments maintain the mortise; a tear allows the fibula to move away from the tibia (diastasis) and destroys the hinge.
  • Load: the talus transmits the whole body weight to the foot; in standing the load passes from the tibial plafond into the talus and then divides between the calcaneus and the forefoot.
  • The axis of the joint runs obliquely, downwards and laterally, so that dorsiflexion and plantarflexion are accompanied by a small amount of rotation — one reason the ankle is not a perfect hinge.

5.Radiological anatomy

  • AP radiograph: the tibial plafond, the medial and lateral malleoli, the talar trochlea and the clear joint space; the distal tibiofibular joint is assessed for overlap of the two bones.
  • Lateral radiograph: the talus, calcaneus and the posterior process of the talus; the joint line and the talar dome; the calcaneal tendon and the fat planes.
  • Mortise view: taken with the leg internally rotated about 15–20° so that the mortise is seen without the fibula superimposed; the medial clear space, the tibiofibular clear space and the joint line should be uniform — a widened clear space indicates a syndesmotic or deltoid injury.
  • Stress views (forced inversion or external rotation) show abnormal talar tilt or widening if the ligaments are torn; CT is used for complex fractures and osteochondral lesions; MRI shows ligaments, tendons, marrow oedema and cartilage; ultrasound examines tendons (calcaneal, fibular) and effusions.
  • The Ottawa ankle rules guide the decision to radiograph: pain near the malleoli plus bone tenderness or inability to bear weight; a similar rule applies to the foot (base of the fifth metatarsal, navicular).

6.Clinical correlations

  • Inversion sprain: the most common ankle injury; the anterior talofibular ligament is torn first, then the calcaneofibular ligament; the joint is stable in dorsiflexion and unstable in plantarflexion.
  • Bimalleolar (Pott's) fracture: fracture of both malleoli with subluxation of the talus; trimalleolar fracture adds the posterior tibial margin (posterior malleolus).
  • Danis–Weber classification of ankle fractures is based on the level of the fibular fracture relative to the syndesmosis: type A below, type B at the level, type C above it — type C is unstable because the syndesmosis is torn.
  • Maisonneuve fracture: tear of the distal tibiofibular syndesmosis with a high fracture of the proximal fibula; the ankle is unstable although the fibular fracture is far proximally.
  • Calcaneal tendon rupture: a sudden snap, a palpable gap, inability to stand on tiptoe, positive Thompson test.
  • Tarsal tunnel syndrome: compression of the tibial nerve and its branches behind the medial malleolus under the flexor retinaculum.
Must-memorise
  • Hinge joint: talar trochlea + tibial plafond + medial and lateral malleoli (ankle mortise).
  • Trochlea wider anteriorly → tight in dorsiflexion, loose in plantarflexion; close-packed position = dorsiflexion.
  • Deltoid ligament = 4 parts: tibionavicular, tibiocalcaneal, posterior tibiotalar, anterior tibiotalar; strong, limits eversion.
  • Lateral ligament = anterior talofibular, posterior talofibular, calcaneofibular; ATFL is the most commonly injured.
  • Dorsiflexors: deep fibular nerve (L4–S1). Plantarflexors: tibial nerve (S1–S3) except fibularis longus and brevis (superficial fibular n.).
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Mortise view is the key film for the syndesmosis; asymmetry of the clear spaces means instability.
  • Ankle sprain vs. fracture: localised tenderness over the ATFL or the malleoli, and the Ottawa rules decide on imaging.
  • Diastasis of the distal tibiofibular joint requires a syndesmotic screw to restore the mortise.
  • Osteochondral lesion of the talar dome after inversion trauma — MRI is required.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Why is the ankle more stable in dorsiflexion? — The wider anterior part of the talar trochlea becomes wedged in the mortise, the joint surfaces are congruent and the ligaments are taut.
  • Where do inversion and eversion take place? — Not at the talocrural joint but at the subtalar and transverse tarsal joints; the ankle only dorsiflexes and plantarflexes.
  • Which ligament is torn first in a sprain? — The anterior talofibular ligament.
  • Which bones form the mortise? — Tibia (roof and medial wall), fibula (lateral wall), held together by the distal tibiofibular syndesmosis.

II.8Anatomy of the intertarsal joints and surgical lines of the foot. Movements of the foot: participating muscles.

Core answerThe intertarsal joints are the numerous synovial joints between the tarsal bones: the subtalar joint, the talocalcaneonavicular joint and the calcaneocuboid joint (the talonavicular part of the former, together with the calcaneocuboid joint, forms the transverse tarsal joint), and the cuneonavicular, intercuneiform and cuneocuboid joints; the cubonavicular connection is fibrous. They allow inversion and eversion, and pronation and supination of the forefoot. Two surgical lines are defined on them: Chopart's line, through the transverse tarsal (talonavicular and calcaneocuboid) joints, and Lisfranc's line, through the tarsometatarsal joints. Inversion is produced mainly by tibialis anterior and posterior, eversion by fibularis longus, brevis and tertius.

2.The subtalar joint

The subtalar joint (articulatio subtalaris) is the posterior articulation between talus and calcaneus: the large posterior calcaneal facet on the inferior surface of the talus and the corresponding posterior talar facet on the superior surface of the calcaneus. Its cavity is enclosed by a synovial membrane and a fibrous capsule. It allows gliding and rotation which, together with the talocalcaneonavicular joint, produce inversion and eversion. It is stabilised by lateral, medial and posterior talocalcaneal ligaments and, most importantly, by the interosseous talocalcaneal ligament, which lies in the tarsal sinus (formed by the calcaneal sulcus and the sulcus tali) and separates the posterior from the anterior compartment of the subtalar complex.

3.Talocalcaneonavicular and calcaneocuboid joints — the transverse tarsal joint

The talocalcaneonavicular joint is a complex joint in which the head of the talus articulates with the calcaneus below (anterior and middle calcaneal facets on the sustentaculum tali), with the plantar calcaneonavicular (spring) ligament inferiorly and with the navicular in front. Its capsule is reinforced posteriorly by the interosseous talocalcaneal ligament, superiorly by the talonavicular ligament and inferiorly by the spring ligament. The spring ligament spans the gap between the sustentaculum tali and the navicular, supports the head of the talus, takes part in the joint and resists depression of the medial arch.

The calcaneocuboid joint is a synovial joint between the anterior surface of the calcaneus and the posterior surface of the cuboid. It is reinforced by the bifurcate ligament (Y-shaped, from the anterior calcaneus to the cuboid and navicular), the long plantar ligament and the short (plantar calcaneocuboid) plantar ligament. The talonavicular part of the talocalcaneonavicular joint and the calcaneocuboid joint together form the transverse tarsal (Chopart) joint — some descriptions name the whole talocalcaneonavicular and calcaneocuboid joints, which is functionally equivalent. It is the main site of inversion and eversion of the forefoot on the hindfoot in addition to the subtalar joint; the axes of its two components are nearly parallel, so they work as one functional joint.

4.The other intertarsal, tarsometatarsal and toe joints

  • Cuneonavicular, intercuneiform and cuneocuboid joints are plane synovial joints with only limited gliding; the cubonavicular connection is fibrous and normally immobile.
  • Tarsometatarsal (Lisfranc) joints are plane joints between the three cuneiforms and the cuboid behind and the bases of the five metatarsals in front. The base of the second metatarsal is recessed between the medial and lateral cuneiforms — it is the keystone of the tarsometatarsal complex — and is tied to the medial cuneiform by the strong Lisfranc ligament. The bases of metatarsals II–V also articulate with each other (intermetatarsal joints).
  • Metatarsophalangeal joints are condyloid: each has collateral and plantar ligaments, and the heads of the metatarsals are joined by the deep transverse metatarsal ligaments. Two sesamoid bones lie in the tendons of flexor hallucis brevis under the head of metatarsal I.
  • Interphalangeal joints are hinges; the great toe has one, the other toes have two.
  • The tendons of the long and short flexors run in fibrous digital sheaths in the toes, and the extensor tendons expand into extensor hoods over the proximal phalanges.

6.Movements of the foot and the participating muscles

Inversion turns the sole inwards, eversion turns it outwards; these are the compound result of subtalar and transverse tarsal motion. Pronation rotates the front of the foot laterally relative to the back (eversion + abduction + dorsiflexion of the forefoot), and supination is the reverse. The range of inversion–supination of the forefoot is about 60°, roughly twice that of eversion–pronation (about 30°).

  • Rupture of the spring ligament leads to acquired flat foot with talonavicular subluxation; the arch collapses.
  • Tarsal coalition (calcaneonavicular or talocalcaneal) causes a rigid flat foot with pain and restricted subtalar movement.

  • Where exactly does inversion occur? — Predominantly at the subtalar joint, with a contribution from the transverse tarsal (talonavicular and calcaneocuboid) joints.
  • Which ligament supports the head of the talus? — The plantar calcaneonavicular (spring) ligament; it also completes the socket of the talocalcaneonavicular joint.
  • What is the surgical importance of Chopart's and Lisfranc's lines? — They are the levels of transverse tarsal and tarsometatarsal amputation, and the sites of characteristic dislocations.
  • Which muscles evert the foot? — Fibularis longus and brevis (superficial fibular nerve), assisted by fibularis tertius and extensor digitorum longus (deep fibular nerve).

II.9The anatomy of the foot arches. Radiological anatomy of the foot.

Core answerThe bones of the foot do not lie in a flat plane but are arranged into a medial longitudinal, a lateral longitudinal and a transverse arch (arcus pedis), which absorb and distribute the forces of body weight during standing and walking. The arches are maintained by three groups of structures: the shape of the bones (wedges and keystones), the ligaments — plantar calcaneonavicular (spring), short and long plantar ligaments and the plantar aponeurosis (aponeurosis plantaris) — and the muscles, chiefly tibialis posterior, tibialis anterior and fibularis longus, together with the short muscles of the sole. On radiographs the talus, calcaneus, navicular, cuboid, cuneiforms, metatarsals and phalanges are identified, and the longitudinal arch is assessed on a weight-bearing lateral film.

The arch has two pillars: the posterior pillar is the calcaneal tuberosity (heel), the anterior pillars are the heads of the metatarsals. The summit of the medial arch lies at the level of the talus and navicular. The medial longitudinal arch is the highest and most elastic and is the one described as "the arch of the foot"; the lateral arch is flatter and transmits more of the load. It is important that the arches are not rigid: under body weight they flatten slightly, storing elastic energy and protecting the vessels and nerves of the sole.

4.Function and biomechanics

  • Weight distribution: in standing, about half the load passes through the calcaneal tuberosity and the rest through the heads of the metatarsals, mainly the first; the arches act as springs that spread the peak pressure over time.
  • Elastic energy: the arch is flattened at mid-stance and recoils at toe-off, contributing to the efficiency of gait.
  • Protection: the concavity of the sole keeps the plantar vessels and nerves away from the ground.
  • Windlass mechanism: at toe-off the metatarsophalangeal joints are extended, the plantar aponeurosis winds around the metatarsal heads, the arch rises and the calcaneus is pulled forwards; this stabilises the foot for push-off.
  • Adaptation: on uneven ground the subtalar and transverse tarsal joints allow the forefoot to twist relative to the hindfoot (supination/pronation), so the whole sole can stay in contact.

5.Radiological anatomy of the foot

  • Bones: 7 tarsals (talus, calcaneus, navicular, cuboid, medial, intermediate and lateral cuneiforms), 5 metatarsals and 14 phalanges — 26 bones per foot. The metatarsals are numbered I–V from medial to lateral; the first is the shortest and thickest, the second the longest.
  • AP radiograph: the talus, calcaneus, navicular, cuboid, cuneiforms, metatarsals and phalanges; the talonavicular and calcaneocuboid joint spaces; the base of the fifth metatarsal (a common fracture site) and the sesamoids under the head of metatarsal I.
  • Oblique (medial) view: profiles the lateral metatarsals and the cuboid; the Lisfranc alignment is checked — the medial border of metatarsal II should line up with the medial border of the intermediate cuneiform, and metatarsal III with the lateral cuneiform.
  • Lateral (weight-bearing) view: the only view on which the longitudinal arch can be assessed; it also shows the sinus tarsi, the calcaneal tuberosity, the talar dome and the subtalar joint.
  • CT for complex fractures (calcaneus, Lisfranc), MRI for stress fractures, plantar fascia and ligaments, ultrasound for the plantar fascia, tendons and effusions.
  • Development: at birth usually only the talus and calcaneus are ossified; the other tarsal bones and the metatarsal and phalangeal epiphyses ossify postnatally, the navicular usually being last — a useful point in paediatric radiology.

6.Clinical correlations

  • Pes planus (flat foot): loss of the medial longitudinal arch, congenital or acquired (rupture of tibialis posterior tendon or of the spring ligament); the talar head drops and the heel goes into valgus.
  • Pes cavus: abnormally high medial arch, often with claw toes, in neurological disease (e.g. peroneal muscular atrophy).
  • Plantar fasciitis: degeneration of the plantar aponeurosis at its calcaneal attachment with heel pain and a calcaneal spur; MRI or ultrasound shows thickening.
  • Stress (march) fracture of a metatarsal (commonly the second or third) in soldiers, runners and dancers.
  • Tarsal coalition and accessory navicular are causes of a painful flat foot in adolescents.
  • Fracture of the calcaneus after a fall from height flattens the arch and must be assessed with CT; associated lumbar and lower limb injuries are common.
Must-memorise
  • Three arches: medial longitudinal (calcaneus–talus–navicular–cuneiforms–MT I–III), lateral longitudinal (calcaneus–cuboid–MT IV–V), transverse (cuboid, cuneiforms, metatarsal bases).
  • Static support: spring ligament, short and long plantar ligaments, plantar aponeurosis.
  • Dynamic support: tibialis posterior (chief, medial arch), tibialis anterior, fibularis longus (transverse arch), long flexors and the short muscles of the sole.
  • Keystones: talus (medial arch), cuboid (lateral arch), intermediate cuneiform (transverse arch).
  • 26 bones per foot: 7 tarsals + 5 metatarsals + 14 phalanges.
  • Windlass mechanism = plantar aponeurosis tightened by toe extension at toe-off.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Weight-bearing lateral film is essential to judge the arch; a non-weight-bearing film can make a flat foot look normal.
  • Plantar fasciitis: heel pain on first steps in the morning, tenderness at the medial calcaneal tuberosity, thickened fascia on imaging.
  • Rupture of the tibialis posterior tendon produces an acquired flat foot in an adult, with a "too many toes" sign and loss of the medial arch.
  • Accessory navicular (os naviculare) is a common normal variant causing medial foot pain, especially in adolescents.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Which muscle is the main dynamic support of the medial arch? — Tibialis posterior, assisted by tibialis anterior and the long flexors.
  • Which structures support the transverse arch? — Fibularis longus tendon crossing the sole, the deep transverse metatarsal ligaments and the transverse head of adductor hallucis, with the intermediate cuneiform as keystone.
  • What happens to the arches in a fracture of the calcaneus? — The arch is flattened; the calcaneus loses height and the talus becomes more horizontal; CT is needed.
  • How do you assess the arch radiologically? — On a weight-bearing lateral film, by the height of the medial arch above the ground and by the relationship of the talus, navicular and metatarsals.

II.10Arteries of the lower limb, anastomoses between the branches of the femoral artery.

Core answerThe lower limb is supplied by the femoral artery (arteria femoralis), the continuation of the external iliac artery beneath the inguinal ligament. It descends through the femoral triangle and the adductor canal, passes through the adductor hiatus as the popliteal artery, and ends behind the knee by dividing into the anterior tibial and posterior tibial arteries; the posterior tibial gives the fibular artery and continues into the sole as the medial and lateral plantar arteries. The superior gluteal, inferior gluteal and obturator arteries from the internal iliac reinforce the proximal supply. Anastomoses around the hip (cruciate and trochanteric), the knee (genicular network) and the ankle protect the limb when a main artery is occluded.

1.Plan of the arterial tree

Abdominal aorta → common iliac a. → external iliac a. → (under the inguinal ligament) femoral a. → adductor canal → adductor hiatus → popliteal a. → anterior tibial a. and posterior tibial a. → (anterior tibial) dorsalis pedis a. → deep plantar a. → deep plantar arch; (posterior tibial) fibular a. and medial/lateral plantar aa.

Three arteries enter the thigh from the pelvis: the femoral artery (external iliac), and the obturator and inferior gluteal arteries (internal iliac). The superior gluteal artery (internal iliac) enters the gluteal region through the suprapiriform foramen above the piriformis; the inferior gluteal artery passes through the infrapiriform foramen below it.

2.Femoral artery: course and branches

The femoral artery begins where the external iliac artery passes under the inguinal ligament. In the femoral triangle it lies midway between the anterior superior iliac spine and the pubic symphysis, where its pulse is easily palpated; the femoral vein is posteromedial and the femoral nerve lateral to it. It runs vertically through the triangle, enters the adductor canal (canalis adductorius) deep to the sartorius, and leaves the thigh through the adductor hiatus (hiatus adductorius) in the adductor magnus muscle as the popliteal artery.

  • Four superficial branches arise in the femoral triangle: superficial epigastric, superficial circumflex iliac, superficial external pudendal and deep external pudendal arteries — supplying the lower abdominal wall, upper thigh and perineum.
  • Deep artery of the thigh (arteria profunda femoris) — the largest branch, arising from the lateral side of the femoral artery; the main supply of the thigh.
  • Descending genicular artery — arises in the adductor canal and contributes to the anastomosis around the knee.

3.Deep artery of the thigh (profunda femoris)

The deep artery of the thigh passes posteriorly between the pectineus and adductor longus, then between the adductor longus and adductor brevis, and then between the adductor longus and adductor magnus; it perforates the adductor magnus distally to anastomose with branches of the popliteal artery. It has two circumflex femoral branches and three perforating branches.

  • Lateral circumflex femoral artery (a. circumflexa femoris lateralis) — passes deep to the sartorius and rectus femoris; gives ascending (to the femoral neck, joining the medial circumflex), transverse and descending branches.
  • Medial circumflex femoral artery (a. circumflexa femoris medialis) — passes medially between the pectineus and iliopsoas, then between the obturator externus and adductor brevis; its acetabular branch enters the hip joint through the acetabular notch and anastomoses with the obturator artery, and its terminal branches ascend to the trochanteric fossa and supply the femoral head and neck.
  • Perforating arteries (first, second and third) — pierce the adductor magnus near the linea aspera and supply the posterior compartment of the thigh; their ascending and descending branches form a longitudinal channel that links the hip and knee anastomoses.

4.Popliteal artery and the genicular branches

The popliteal artery is the deepest neurovascular structure in the popliteal fossa and is therefore difficult to palpate. It enters the fossa on the upper medial side under the semimembranosus, descends obliquely with the tibial nerve, and ends at the lower border of the popliteus muscle by dividing into the anterior tibial and posterior tibial arteries. Its branches are:

  • Two sural arteries — to the gastrocnemius, soleus and plantaris.
  • Superior lateral, superior medial, inferior lateral and inferior medial genicular arteries (aa. genus) — for the knee joint and its anastomotic network.
  • Middle genicular artery — to the cruciate ligaments and interior of the knee joint.

Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Femoral access: the femoral artery is palpated just below the inguinal ligament; it is the standard route for angiography, angioplasty and catheterisation of the contralateral limb, abdominal and thoracic vessels.
  • Popliteal aneurysm: the popliteal artery is aneurysmal when its diameter exceeds 7 mm; because it tends to thrombose and embolise rather than rupture, the danger is distal ischaemia, and duplex Doppler is the first-line diagnosis.
  • Knee dislocation and distal femoral fracture may injure the popliteal artery, which is tethered by the adductor hiatus above and the soleus arch below; the limb is checked for distal pulses and Doppler signals.
  • Peripheral arterial disease: occlusion of the femoral or popliteal artery causes claudication and loss of the distal pulses; the genicular and cruciate anastomoses may keep the limb viable after gradual occlusion.
  • Compartment syndrome of the leg follows tibial or fibular arterial injury and swelling inside a closed fascial compartment; the deep fibular and tibial nerves are the most sensitive to ischaemia.

  • Where exactly does the femoral artery become the popliteal artery? — At the adductor hiatus, as it passes through the adductor magnus muscle.
  • Which arteries form the cruciate anastomosis and why is it called cruciate? — The transverse branches of the medial and lateral circumflex femoral arteries cross between the inferior gluteal and first perforating arteries, giving a cross-like arrangement.
  • How is the head of the femur supplied? — Mainly by the medial circumflex femoral artery, with contributions from the lateral circumflex femoral and the acetabular branch of the obturator artery through the ligament of the head.
  • Which artery pierces the interosseous membrane and at which end? — The anterior tibial artery, through the aperture in the upper part of the membrane.
  • What is the clinical value of the genicular anastomosis? — It preserves the distal limb after ligation or slow occlusion of the femoral or popliteal artery, and it explains the warm knee despite an occluded vessel.

II.11Venous and lymphatic drainage of the lower limb; clinical significance of the perforating veins.

Core answerBlood returns from the lower limb through two connected systems: a superficial system in the subcutaneous tissue — the great saphenous vein (vena saphena magna) and the small saphenous vein (vena saphena parva), both arising from the dorsal venous arch — and a deep system that follows the arteries (anterior and posterior tibial, popliteal, femoral, external iliac veins). Superficial blood is conducted into the deep veins by perforating veins; incompetent valves in these veins, or in the saphenous junctions, produce varicose veins. Lymph from the limb drains to the superficial and deep inguinal nodes (and from the lateral leg and foot to the popliteal nodes), then to the external iliac nodes.

1.General plan

  • The deep veins accompany the arteries as paired venae comitantes (tibial and fibular veins), which unite to form the popliteal vein and then the femoral vein.
  • The superficial veins lie in the subcutaneous tissue, are not accompanied by arteries, and drain into the deep veins through the saphenous openings and perforating veins.
  • Both systems contain valves that direct flow proximally and inward; the calf muscle pump (gastrocnemius, soleus) and the venous valves are the two mechanisms of venous return.
  • Direction of flow: capillaries of skin/subcutis → superficial veins → perforating veins → deep veins → iliac veins → inferior vena cava.

2.Superficial veins

All superficial veins begin from the dorsal venous arch over the metatarsals, which receives the dorsal digital veins and communicates with the plantar veins through perforating vessels.

3.Deep veins

  • Anterior tibial and posterior tibial veins (with the fibular vein) are paired venae comitantes of the corresponding arteries in the leg.
  • They unite behind the knee to form the popliteal vein (vena poplitea), which lies superficial to the popliteal artery and passes proximally through the adductor hiatus.
  • Above the adductor hiatus the popliteal vein becomes the femoral vein (vena femoralis), which traverses the adductor canal and the femoral triangle in the intermediate compartment of the femoral sheath, and becomes the external iliac vein under the inguinal ligament.
  • Deep tributaries include the deep vein of the thigh (v. profunda femoris), the circumflex femoral veins, and the gluteal and obturator veins, which drain to the internal iliac system.

4.Perforating veins and their clinical significance

Perforating veins (vv. perforantes) pierce the deep fascia and connect the superficial with the deep venous system. Their valves normally allow flow only from superficial to deep, so that the muscle pump can empty the subcutaneous plexus. They are found in the thigh (mid-thigh perforators), in the leg (communicating with the posterior tibial and fibular veins) and around the ankle and foot.

Lymphatic vessels of the lower limb follow the veins: superficial vessels accompany the great saphenous vein to the superficial inguinal nodes, while those of the lateral foot and leg accompany the small saphenous vein to the popliteal nodes; deep vessels accompany the deep vessels to the deep inguinal nodes.

  • Lymph: limb → superficial/deep inguinal nodes → external iliac nodes; the lateral foot and leg also drain to the popliteal nodes.
  • Great saphenous vein is the longest vein of the body and the preferred conduit for coronary and peripheral bypass grafts.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Varicose veins are dilated, tortuous superficial veins produced by incompetent valves; they are commoner in women and aggravated by pregnancy and standing; complications are pigmentation, eczema and venous ulceration.
  • Deep vein thrombosis (DVT): Virchow triad — venous stasis, injury of the vessel wall, hypercoagulable states. Calf-vein thrombi may propagate into the femoral and iliac veins and embolise to the pulmonary arteries.
  • DVT is diagnosed mainly by duplex Doppler sonography (compression ultrasound) and prevented by anticoagulant injections and graduated compression stockings.
  • The saphenous opening is the site of the great saphenous termination and of superficial inguinal lymphadenopathy; a varicose saphenous vein here may be the first sign of an underlying pelvic or abdominal mass.
  • Lymphoedema of the limb follows obstruction or removal of inguinal and iliac nodes; the popliteal nodes are impalpable and enlarged only in deep infection or malignancy of the leg.
Likely follow-up questions

  • Why is the small saphenous vein at risk during surgery on the back of the leg? — It lies in the superficial fascia with the sural nerve, which may be injured by stripping.
  • What is the direction of flow in a normal perforating vein and why? — From superficial to deep, because its valves face the deep system.
  • Where do the lymphatics of the little toe drain? — Along the small saphenous vein to the popliteal nodes, and thence to the inguinal nodes.
  • Which veins are used for coronary bypass and why? — The great saphenous vein; its length, calibre and superficial position allow long grafts.

II.12Branching system of the lumbar plexus.

Core answerThe lumbar plexus (plexus lumbalis) is formed by the anterior rami of spinal nerves L1–L3 and most of L4, with a contribution from T12 (subcostal nerve). It lies in the substance of the psoas major muscle and gives six named branches: iliohypogastric (L1), ilio-inguinal (L1), genitofemoral (L1–L2), lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh (L2–L3), femoral (L2–L4) and obturator (L2–L4). The rest of the L4 root joins L5 as the lumbosacral trunk and passes to the sacral plexus.

1.Formation and position

  • Roots: anterior rami of T12 (small contribution), L1, L2, L3 and the greater part of L4. The remainder of L4 and all of L5 form the lumbosacral trunk.
  • Site: the plexus is formed in the substance of the psoas major muscle, anterior to the transverse processes of the lumbar vertebrae, on the posterior abdominal wall.
  • Relation to psoas: branches emerge from the muscle in three directions — anteriorly the genitofemoral nerve, medially the obturator nerve, and laterally the iliohypogastric, ilio-inguinal, femoral and lateral cutaneous nerves of the thigh.
  • Sympathetic fibres reach the plexus through grey rami communicantes from the lumbar sympathetic trunk; they are distributed with the branches to vessels, sweat glands and erector pili muscles.

2.Branches: course and distribution

  • Iliohypogastric nerve (L1) — arises together with the ilio-inguinal nerve from the L1 root; runs across the quadratus lumborum and pierces the transversus abdominis, then continues between the transversus and internal oblique. It supplies both muscles and ends as a lateral cutaneous branch (skin over the posterolateral gluteal region) and an anterior cutaneous branch (skin above the pubis).
  • Ilio-inguinal nerve (L1) — runs below and roughly parallel to the iliohypogastric nerve, crosses the iliacus, enters the inguinal canal, and emerges through the superficial inguinal ring. It supplies the internal oblique and transversus abdominis and the skin of the upper medial thigh and of the root of the penis and anterior scrotum (or the mons pubis and labium majus).
  • Genitofemoral nerve (L1, L2) — pierces the anterior surface of the psoas major and divides into two branches. The genital branch passes through the inguinal canal and supplies the cremaster muscle (in men) and the skin of the anterior scrotum or mons pubis and labium majus. The femoral branch passes under the inguinal ligament lateral to the femoral artery and supplies skin over the upper central part of the anterior thigh.
  • Lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh (L2, L3) — emerges from the lateral border of the psoas, runs across the iliacus and leaves the abdomen either through the gap under the inguinal ligament just medial to the anterior superior iliac spine or through the ligament itself. It is purely sensory to the lateral side of the thigh, as far as the knee.
  • Femoral nerve (L2–L4) — formed within the psoas, it runs in the groove between the psoas and iliacus and reaches the thigh under the inguinal ligament, lateral to the femoral artery and outside the femoral sheath. In the abdomen it supplies the iliacus and pectineus; in the thigh it supplies the sartorius, the four heads of the quadriceps femoris and the articularis genus. Its sensory branches supply the anterior thigh, the anteromedial knee, the medial side of the leg and the medial side of the foot (through its terminal saphenous nerve).
  • Obturator nerve (L2–L4) — emerges from the medial border of the psoas, descends along the lateral pelvic wall and leaves the pelvis through the obturator canal. Its anterior division supplies the adductor longus, adductor brevis and gracilis, and often gives a branch to the pectineus (which is mainly supplied by the femoral nerve); its posterior division supplies the obturator externus and the adductor part of the adductor magnus. It carries sensation from the medial side of the thigh and gives a branch to the knee joint.

The part of the anterior ramus of L4 that does not enter the lumbar plexus descends over the ala of the sacrum and joins the anterior ramus of L5 to form the lumbosacral trunk (truncus lumbosacralis). The trunk crosses the pelvic brim medial to the psoas and enters the pelvis, where it joins the sacral plexus. This anatomical continuity explains why an L4 or L5 lesion may produce signs in both the lumbar and the sacral territories, and why the lumbosacral trunk is the most medial structure at risk in pelvic fractures.

Must-memorise
  • Roots: L1–L3 + most of L4 (+ T12); the rest of L4 joins L5 as the lumbosacral trunk.
  • Six branches: iliohypogastric (L1), ilio-inguinal (L1), genitofemoral (L1–L2), lateral cutaneous nerve of thigh (L2–L3), femoral (L2–L4), obturator (L2–L4).
  • Relation to psoas: anterior — genitofemoral; medial — obturator; lateral — iliohypogastric, ilio-inguinal, femoral, lateral cutaneous nerve of thigh.
  • The femoral nerve is the only branch that supplies an entire compartment of the thigh (anterior).
  • The obturator nerve is the motor nerve of the medial compartment.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Femoral neuropathy (pelvic or retroperitoneal tumour, haematoma, diabetes, hip surgery): weakness of knee extension, wasting of the quadriceps, loss of the knee jerk (L3–L4) and sensory loss over the anterior thigh and medial leg.
  • Obturator neuropathy (pelvic surgery, obturator hernia, lithotomy position): weak thigh adduction, an unstable, wide-based gait, and sensory loss on the medial thigh.
  • Meralgia paraesthetica is entrapment of the lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh under the inguinal ligament, producing burning pain and numbness of the lateral thigh; it is common in obesity, pregnancy and tight belts.
  • Ilio-inguinal nerve injury is a classic cause of chronic groin pain after open inguinal hernia repair; the nerve may be divided deliberately during mesh repair.
  • Lumbar disc herniation compresses a root rather than a peripheral nerve, producing a radicular pattern with pain in the corresponding dermatome and weakness in the corresponding myotome.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Which is the thickest branch of the lumbar plexus? — The femoral nerve, the largest branch of the plexus.
  • Which branch has no motor fibres in the lower limb? — The lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh is purely sensory.
  • Through which opening does the obturator nerve leave the pelvis? — The obturator canal, in the obturator membrane.
  • Why does a femoral nerve lesion not abolish hip flexion? — Hip flexion is also produced by the psoas major (direct branches of L1–L3), the adductors (obturator nerve, L2–L4) and the tensor fasciae latae (superior gluteal nerve, L4–S1), so a femoral nerve lesion weakens the movement but does not abolish it.
  • How is the lumbar plexus tested clinically? — Knee extension (femoral, L3–L4) and thigh adduction against resistance (obturator, L2–L4), with the corresponding sensory areas and the patellar reflex.

II.13Branching system of the sacral plexus. The branches of the tibial and common fibular (peroneal) nerves.

Core answerThe sacral plexus (plexus sacralis) is formed by the lumbosacral trunk (L4, L5) and the anterior rami of S1–S4. It lies on the anterior surface of the piriformis and gives the sciatic nerve (L4–S3), the superior gluteal (L4–S1), inferior gluteal (L5–S2), pudendal (S2–S4), posterior cutaneous nerve of the thigh (S1–S3), the nerves to the obturator internus, gemelli, quadratus femoris and piriformis, and the perforating cutaneous nerve. The sciatic nerve divides into the tibial nerve (L4–S3, ventral divisions) and the common fibular nerve (L4–S2, dorsal divisions); the tibial nerve supplies the posterior thigh, all muscles of the posterior compartment of the leg and the sole, while the common fibular supplies the anterior and lateral compartments of the leg and the dorsum of the foot.

1.Formation and position

  • Roots: lumbosacral trunk (anterior ramus of L4 plus L5) and the anterior rami of S1, S2, S3 and part of S4. The anterior ramus of S4 contributes only a ventral division.
  • Site: formed on the anterior surface of the piriformis muscle, on the posterolateral wall of the pelvis; sacral roots reach it through the anterior sacral foramina.
  • Exits: most branches leave the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen; the superior gluteal nerve and vessels pass through its suprapiriform part, the others through the infrapiriform part. A few nerves (nerve to piriformis, nerves to levator ani and coccygeus) remain within the pelvis, and the pudendal nerve and nerve to obturator internus re-enter through the lesser sciatic foramen after looping around the ischial spine.
  • Visceral fibres: the pelvic splanchnic nerves (S2–S4) arise from the same roots and carry preganglionic parasympathetic fibres to the pelvic viscera.

3.The sciatic nerve and its two divisions

  • The sciatic nerve is the largest nerve of the body. It forms on the piriformis, leaves the pelvis through the infrapiriform foramen, crosses the posterior surfaces of the obturator internus, the gemelli and the quadratus femoris, and reaches the posterior thigh at the lower border of the quadratus femoris.
  • It is enclosed in a common connective-tissue sheath and divides into its two parts at a variable level, often in the lower third of the thigh, but the two divisions remain functionally separate throughout their course.
  • The tibial part (L4–S3; ventral divisions) supplies the hamstrings except the short head of biceps femoris, the hamstring part of adductor magnus, and all muscles of the posterior leg and the sole.
  • The common fibular part (L4–S2; dorsal divisions) supplies the short head of biceps femoris, the anterior and lateral compartments of the leg, the extensor digitorum brevis and, partly, the first two dorsal interossei.

4.Branches of the tibial nerve

  • In the popliteal fossa: muscular branches to the gastrocnemius, soleus, plantaris (these arise between the two heads of gastrocnemius) and the popliteus; the sural nerve arises here between the heads of gastrocnemius and is joined later by a communicating branch from the common fibular nerve.
  • In the leg: branches to the deep group — tibialis posterior, flexor digitorum longus, flexor hallucis longus — arising deep to the soleus; the medial calcaneal branches arise near the ankle and supply the heel.
  • At the ankle: the nerve passes through the tarsal tunnel behind the medial malleolus (lateral to the posterior tibial artery) and divides into the medial plantar and lateral plantar nerves.
  • Medial plantar nerve: supplies the abductor hallucis, flexor digitorum brevis, flexor hallucis brevis and first lumbrical; its cutaneous branches supply the medial two-thirds of the sole and the medial three and a half digits.
  • Lateral plantar nerve: supplies the quadratus plantae, the second to fourth lumbricals, adductor hallucis, flexor digiti minimi brevis and all the interossei; its cutaneous branches supply the lateral third of the sole and the lateral one and a half digits.

5.Branches of the common fibular nerve

  • Course: the common fibular nerve leaves the popliteal fossa along the medial border of the biceps femoris tendon, winds around the neck of the fibula deep to the fibres of the fibularis longus, and divides into the superficial and deep fibular nerves.
  • Superficial fibular nerve: passes into the lateral compartment and supplies fibularis longus and fibularis brevis; it pierces the deep fascia of the lower leg and supplies skin of the lower lateral leg and most of the dorsum of the foot and toes (except the first web space).
  • Deep fibular nerve: passes into the anterior compartment and supplies tibialis anterior, extensor hallucis longus, extensor digitorum longus and fibularis tertius; it continues with the anterior tibial artery and enters the dorsum of the foot lateral to the dorsalis pedis artery, where it supplies extensor digitorum brevis, partially the first two dorsal interossei, and the skin of the first web space and adjacent sides of toes I and II.

II.14Sensory innervation of the skin of the lower limb.

Core answerThe skin of the lower limb has two superimposed patterns of innervation. The segmental (radicular) pattern is the dermatome: L1–S3 for the limb, with S4 and S5 for the perineum. The peripheral pattern is the territory of the cutaneous branches of the lumbar and sacral plexuses — iliohypogastric, ilio-inguinal, genitofemoral, lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh, femoral (through its saphenous branch), obturator, posterior cutaneous nerve of the thigh, perforating cutaneous, and the tibial and common fibular branches. Clinically the two patterns are distinguished by the shape of the sensory loss: a dermatome is a transverse band, a peripheral nerve territory follows the course of a named nerve.

1.Segmental innervation: the dermatomes

A dermatome is the area of skin supplied by the somatic sensory fibres of one spinal nerve. In the lower limb the dermatomes are arranged as oblique bands that spiral down the limb: L1–L3 pass down the anterior and medial thigh, L4–L5 cross the knee and leg, and S1–S3 run down the posterior thigh and leg onto the lateral foot. Because adjacent dermatomes overlap, only a limited autonomous zone of each dermatome can be tested reliably.

3.How the two patterns are tested

  • Sensation: light touch with cotton wool, pinprick, and temperature, compared side to side; the autonomous zones listed above are used because adjacent dermatomes overlap.
  • Radicular pattern: loss confined to a dermatomal band, often with pain in the same segment, aggravated by coughing or straining; the straight-leg-raising test is positive in lumbar root compression.
  • Peripheral pattern: loss in the territory of one named nerve, which may be smaller or larger than a single dermatome and often has a sharp boundary on the limb.
  • Corresponding motor and reflex tests make the segmental diagnosis: hip flexion L1–L2; knee extension L3–L4 (patellar reflex); knee flexion L5–S2; plantarflexion S1–S2 (calcaneal reflex); toe adduction S2–S3.

4.Clinical correlations

  • Meralgia paraesthetica — compression of the lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh (L2–L3) under the inguinal ligament gives burning pain and numbness of the lateral thigh with no motor loss.
  • L4 versus femoral nerve — an L4 root lesion affects the medial leg and great toe, weakens knee extension and tibialis anterior, and reduces the patellar reflex; a femoral neuropathy adds weakness of hip flexion and an absent patellar reflex with a smaller sensory field.
  • L5 versus common fibular nerve — an L5 root lesion also weakens hip abduction (gluteus medius) and foot inversion (tibialis posterior), which are spared in a common fibular palsy; both cause weakness of dorsiflexion and eversion.
  • S1 versus tibial nerve — an S1 root lesion reduces the ankle jerk and gives numbness of the little toe and lateral foot; a tibial nerve lesion adds weakness of the intrinsic foot muscles and loss of sole sensation.
  • Referred pain — the hip joint refers pain to the knee through the femoral and obturator nerves, so hip disease may present as knee pain.
Must-memorise
  • Autonomous zones: L1 groin, L2 lateral thigh, L3 lower medial thigh, L4 medial great toe, L5 medial digit II, S1 little toe, S2 back of thigh, S3 gluteal fold, S4–S5 perianal skin.
  • Leg and foot nerve map: saphenous medial, sural lateral, superficial fibular dorsum, deep fibular first web space, tibial branches sole and heel.
  • Segmental reflex map: patellar reflex L3–L4; calcaneal reflex S1–S2.
  • Radicular loss = band; peripheral loss = named nerve territory.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Herpes zoster of a lower thoracic or lumbar dermatome produces a painful vesicular rash in a band, and post-herpetic neuralgia may persist in the same segment.
  • Diabetes mellitus produces a length-dependent peripheral neuropathy in a stocking distribution, beginning in the toes and sole, unlike a single dermatome or a single nerve territory.
  • The sural nerve is harvested for nerve biopsy and for nerve grafting because its loss produces only a small area of numbness on the lateral foot.
  • Compartment syndrome of the leg first produces pain and paraesthesia in the territory of the deep fibular nerve (first web space), before motor signs appear.
  • Spinal cord lesions may leave a sensory level; a conus medullaris or cauda equina lesion produces saddle anaesthesia over S3–S5 with perianal numbness.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Which dermatomes overlap the knee? — L3, L4 and L5; the patellar reflex tests L3–L4.
  • Which nerve supplies the medial side of the foot? — The saphenous nerve (femoral), while the lateral side of the foot is supplied by the sural nerve.
  • What is a dermatome and what is an autonomous zone? — A dermatome is the skin supplied by one spinal nerve; the autonomous zone is the part of it with least overlap from neighbouring segments.
  • Why does an L5 radiculopathy not equal a common fibular palsy? — The L5 root also supplies the gluteus medius and tibialis posterior through other nerves, which remain normal in a fibular palsy.

II.15Muscles of the hip. Supra- and infrapiriform foramina (hiatuses).

Core answerThe gluteal region contains the muscles that move the hip joint: a superficial group — gluteus maximus, gluteus medius, gluteus minimus and tensor fasciae latae — which abduct and extend the hip, and a deep group — piriformis, obturator internus, superior gemellus, inferior gemellus and quadratus femoris — which laterally rotate it. The iliopsoas is the powerful flexor of the hip. The greater sciatic foramen is divided by the piriformis into a suprapiriform foramen, which transmits the superior gluteal nerve and vessels, and an infrapiriform foramen, which transmits the sciatic nerve, the inferior gluteal nerve and vessels, the pudendal nerve and internal pudendal vessels, the posterior cutaneous nerve of the thigh, and the small nerves to the gemelli and quadratus femoris.

1.Groups and general actions

  • Superficial abductors and extensors: gluteus minimus, gluteus medius, gluteus maximus and tensor fasciae latae. They abduct and extend the femur and stabilise the pelvis on the stance leg.
  • Deep lateral rotators: piriformis, obturator internus, superior gemellus, inferior gemellus and quadratus femoris. They laterally rotate the extended femur and abduct the flexed femur (except the quadratus femoris, which is a pure lateral rotator).
  • Flexor: the iliopsoas (psoas major and iliacus), which passes from the posterior abdominal wall through the gap under the inguinal ligament to the lesser trochanter.
  • The gluteal nerves and vessels lie in the plane between the superficial and deep groups, which is why surgical approaches to the hip must respect this plane.

  • Suprapiriform foramen = superior gluteal nerve + superior gluteal vessels only.

  • Deep rotators: piriformis (S1, S2); obturator internus and superior gemellus (L5–S2); inferior gemellus and quadratus femoris (L4–S1).
  • Safe site for gluteal intramuscular injection: upper lateral quadrant of the buttock.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Trendelenburg sign: if the gluteus medius and minimus (superior gluteal nerve) are weak, the pelvis drops on the unsupported side during single-leg stance, and the trunk lurches to the weak side (Trendelenburg gait).
  • Superior gluteal nerve injury may follow a posterior approach to the hip (total hip arthroplasty, intramedullary nailing) or a pelvic fracture, producing abduction weakness and a positive Trendelenburg test.
  • Gluteal intramuscular injection into the lower medial quadrant risks the sciatic nerve and the inferior gluteal vessels; the upper lateral quadrant is used.
  • Piriformis syndrome — the sciatic nerve may be compressed by a tight piriformis, giving deep buttock pain and sciatic-like radiation.
  • Ischial bursitis and tenderness over the ischial tuberosity relate to the hamstring origins, which sit just below the gluteus maximus.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Which muscle forms the landmark of the greater sciatic foramen? — The piriformis; it divides the foramen into supra- and infrapiriform parts.
  • Which two muscles act as abductors and medial rotators of the hip? — Gluteus medius and gluteus minimus (with tensor fasciae latae).
  • Why do the deep rotators abduct a flexed femur? — Their line of pull changes when the femur is flexed, so the lateral rotators become abductors.
  • Which nerve would be injured in a badly placed gluteal injection into the lower medial quadrant? — The sciatic nerve, with resulting weakness of the hamstrings and all leg and foot muscles.
  • How does gluteus maximus differ from the other gluteal muscles? — It is the only muscle supplied by the inferior gluteal nerve and the only powerful extensor of the hip.

II.16Subinguinal hiatus. Femoral canal.

Core answerThe subinguinal hiatus (hiatus subinguinalis) is the gap between the inguinal ligament above and the anterior margin of the pelvic bone and pectineal line below, through which structures pass between the abdomen and the anterior thigh. It is divided by the iliopectineal arch into a lateral lacuna musculorum (iliopsoas, femoral nerve, lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh) and a medial lacuna vasorum, which is filled by the femoral sheath. The sheath has three compartments: the femoral artery laterally, the femoral vein in the middle, and the femoral canal medially, which contains lymphatics and whose upper opening — the femoral ring — is the site of femoral hernia.

2.Lacuna musculorum

The lacuna musculorum (lacuna musculorum) lies lateral to the iliopectineal arch and is the lateral half of the subinguinal hiatus. It transmits:

  • Iliopsoas (psoas major and iliacus) — the muscle fills the lacuna and glides over the pelvic brim on its way to the lesser trochanter.
  • Femoral nerve — lies in the groove between the psoas and iliacus, lateral to the femoral artery, and is not enclosed by the femoral sheath.
  • Lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh — passes through the lateral part of the gap, just medial to the anterior superior iliac spine, or through the inguinal ligament itself.

The lateral part of the lacuna is therefore the route of the great flexor of the hip and of the motor nerve of the anterior thigh; a haematoma or tumour spreading here produces a femoral neuropathy with weak knee extension and absent knee jerk rather than a groin lump.

3.Lacuna vasorum and the femoral sheath

The lacuna vasorum is the medial part of the subinguinal hiatus. It is occupied by the femoral sheath (vagina femoralis), a funnel-shaped sleeve of fascia continuous above with the transversalis fascia and the iliac fascia; below, it blends with the connective tissue of the vessels. The femoral canal (canalis femoralis) is the medial, conical compartment of the femoral sheath. 5.Clinical significance

  • Femoral hernia — abdominal viscera pass through the femoral ring, along the femoral canal and emerge through the saphenous opening into the medial thigh. The hernial sac appears below and lateral to the pubic tubercle (an inguinal hernia appears above and medial to it).
  • The neck of the femoral canal is narrow, so femoral hernias are prone to irreducibility and strangulation; they are more frequent in women (wider pelvis) and in middle-aged and elderly people, and are usually acquired, not congenital.
  • Surgical approach: reduction is usually obtained through an incision over the groin, opening the anterior wall of the canal; the lacunar ligament may have to be divided to release the constriction, with care for the femoral vein laterally.
  • Vascular access: the femoral artery and vein are reached just below the inguinal ligament; from medial to lateral the structures are femoral canal, femoral vein, femoral artery and femoral nerve, so the nerve is avoided by puncturing at or medial to the artery (venous access is medial to the artery) — never lateral to it. The femoral canal is medial to the vein, so a large medial haematoma after puncture is avoided by keeping the needle on the artery.
Must-memorise
  • Subinguinal hiatus = inguinal ligament above + pelvic bone/pectineal line below; divided by the iliopectineal arch.
  • Lacuna musculorum: iliopsoas, femoral nerve, lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh.
  • Lacuna vasorum: femoral sheath — artery (lateral), vein (intermediate), femoral canal (medial).
  • Femoral ring: anterior inguinal ligament, posterior pectineal ligament, medial lacunar ligament, lateral femoral vein.
  • Femoral hernia: below and lateral to the pubic tubercle; inguinal hernia: above and medial.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Femoral hernia versus inguinal hernia — the relation to the pubic tubercle is the key bedside test; femoral hernias have a much higher risk of strangulation and are therefore repaired early even if small.
  • Femoral vein puncture is used for central venous access, pacing wires and catheterisation of the right heart and iliac veins; the artery is lateral and the femoral nerve further lateral still.
  • Femoral neuropathy from a retroperitoneal or groin haematoma compresses the nerve in the lacuna musculorum but leaves the femoral vessels and their pulses normal.
  • Radiology: ultrasound distinguishes a femoral hernia from an inguinal one; CT shows the neck of the sac medial to the femoral vein and below the inguinal ligament.
Likely follow-up questions
  • What divides the subinguinal hiatus into two lacunae? — The iliopectineal arch, running from the inguinal ligament to the iliopectineal eminence.
  • Why is the femoral nerve not compressed by a femoral hernia? — It lies lateral to the femoral sheath, in the lacuna musculorum, so the hernia in the femoral canal cannot involve it directly.
  • What is the clinical landmark of the femoral ring on the surface? — The pubic tubercle; a femoral hernia lies below and lateral to it, an inguinal hernia above and medial to it.
  • What does the femoral canal contain in the normal subject? — Lymphatic vessels and deep inguinal lymph nodes embedded in fat.
  • Which structure lies immediately lateral to the femoral ring? — The femoral vein, which is at risk when the lacunar ligament is divided during hernia repair.

II.17Femoral triangle, adductor canal, popliteal fossa.

Core answerThe femoral triangle (trigonum femorale) is the wedge-shaped depression below the inguinal ligament bounded by the sartorius laterally and the adductor longus medially, with the inguinal ligament as its base; from lateral to medial it contains the femoral nerve, femoral artery, femoral vein and femoral canal. Its apex leads into the adductor canal, the subsartorial tunnel bounded by the vastus medialis, adductor longus and adductor magnus, which transmits the femoral vessels and the saphenous nerve to the adductor hiatus. The popliteal fossa is the diamond-shaped space behind the knee containing, from superficial to deep, the tibial nerve, popliteal vein and popliteal artery, with the common fibular nerve along its lateral border.

  • Femoral triangle boundaries: inguinal ligament (base), sartorius (lateral), adductor longus (medial).
  • Contents of the femoral triangle, lateral to medial: nerve, artery, vein, lymphatics ("NAVL").
  • Adductor canal contents: femoral artery, femoral vein, saphenous nerve, nerve to vastus medialis.

Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Femoral pulse and access: palpation at the midpoint of the inguinal ligament; the artery is the route for arterial cannulation, angiography and angioplasty.
  • Femoral hernia emerges through the femoral canal at the medial end of the femoral triangle, below and lateral to the pubic tubercle.
  • Saphenous nerve entrapment in the adductor canal causes medial knee and leg pain without motor deficit; the nerve is also at risk during varicose vein stripping.
  • Popliteal artery aneurysm (diameter exceeding 7 mm) is diagnosed by duplex Doppler and treated surgically because of thromboembolism and distal ischaemia.
  • Baker's cyst (synovial cyst of the knee) presents as a popliteal swelling and must be distinguished from a popliteal aneurysm and from popliteal lymphadenopathy by ultrasound.
  • Knee dislocation can stretch or tear the popliteal artery at the adductor hiatus, and the common fibular nerve along the fibular neck.
Likely follow-up questions
  • What forms the apex of the femoral triangle and what does it lead into? — The crossing of the sartorius and adductor longus; it continues as the adductor canal.
  • Where does the femoral artery become the popliteal artery? — In the adductor hiatus, at the lower end of the adductor canal.
  • Which structures pass through the roof of the popliteal fossa? — The small saphenous vein and the posterior cutaneous nerve of the thigh.
  • Why is the popliteal artery the deepest structure of the fossa? — It lies directly on the floor (capsule and popliteus), which is why its pulse is difficult to feel, especially in a muscular or obese patient.
  • Which nerve is at risk in a distal femoral fracture? — The popliteal artery, and the common fibular nerve at the fibular neck after knee injury.

II.18Topography of the medial and lateral malleolar regions.

Core answerThe malleolar regions are the retromalleolar parts of the ankle region, one behind each malleolus. In the medial malleolar region all structures pass deep to the flexor retinaculum in the tarsal tunnel: from medial to lateral the tendons of tibialis posterior and flexor digitorum longus, the posterior tibial artery and veins, the tibial nerve, and the tendon of flexor hallucis longus. In the lateral malleolar region the tendons of fibularis longus and fibularis brevis pass behind the lateral malleolus under the fibular retinacula, with the sural nerve and small saphenous vein superficial to them. The malleolar regions are the site of the main tendinous, vascular and nervous passages between the leg and the foot.

1.Bony and general features

  • Medial malleolus of the tibia and lateral malleolus of the fibula form the ankle mortise with the trochlea of the talus; the distal tibia and fibula are united by the tibiofibular syndesmosis.
  • The posterior surface of the medial malleolus bears a broad groove for the tibialis posterior and flexor digitorum longus tendons; the posterior surface of the talus and the sustentaculum tali carry the groove for the flexor hallucis longus.
  • The medial surface of the lateral malleolus has the malleolar fossa for the posterior tibiofibular ligament; the lateral surface of the calcaneus bears the fibular trochlea over which the fibular tendons glide.
  • Both regions are crossed by the cutaneous branches of the superficial veins and nerves of the foot, and the deep fascia of the leg thickens here into the retinacula.

2.Medial malleolar region and the tarsal tunnel

The tarsal tunnel is the fibro-osseous space behind the medial malleolus: its walls are the medial malleolus, the medial and posterior surfaces of the talus, the medial surface of the calcaneus and the sustentaculum tali, and its roof is the flexor retinaculum, which extends from the medial malleolus to the medial and inferior surface of the calcaneus.

Superficial to the flexor retinaculum, in the subcutaneous tissue, pass the great saphenous vein and the saphenous nerve in front of the medial malleolus, and the medial calcaneal branches of the tibial nerve over the heel.

3.Lateral malleolar region

Behind the lateral malleolus the tendons of the lateral compartment of the leg pass through a groove and under two retinacula:

  • Superior fibular retinaculum — extends between the lateral malleolus and the calcaneus.
  • Inferior fibular retinaculum — attaches to the lateral surface of the calcaneus around the fibular trochlea and blends above with the inferior extensor retinaculum.
  • Fibularis brevis — its tendon lies anterior to that of fibularis longus at the malleolus and is held in the upper compartment at the fibular trochlea.
  • Fibularis longus — lies posterior to the brevis tendon, passes below the fibular trochlea, enters the groove on the cuboid and crosses the sole to the medial cuneiform and base of metatarsal I.
  • Superficial to the retinacula run the sural nerve and the small saphenous vein, posterior to the lateral malleolus.
  • Anterior to the lateral malleolus are the tendons of the extensor digitorum longus and fibularis tertius and the sinus tarsi with the cervical ligament and interosseous talocalcaneal ligament.

  • Tarsal tunnel boundaries: bony groove (medial malleolus, talus, calcaneus, sustentaculum tali) + flexor retinaculum.
  • Order in the tunnel: tibialis posterior → flexor digitorum longus → posterior tibial artery → tibial nerve → flexor hallucis longus.
  • Lateral region: fibularis brevis anterior, fibularis longus posterior at the lateral malleolus.
  • Superficial to the retinacula: great saphenous vein and saphenous nerve medially, small saphenous vein and sural nerve laterally.
  • Posterior tibial pulse = midway between the medial malleolus and the calcaneus; dorsalis pedis pulse = dorsum of the foot between the EHL and EDL tendons.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Tarsal tunnel syndrome — compression of the tibial nerve behind the medial malleolus (post-traumatic fibrosis, ganglion, varicose veins, tenosynovitis) causes burning pain in the sole, worse at night, with sensory loss in the plantar territory and later weakness of the intrinsic foot muscles.
  • Ankle sprain is usually an inversion injury: the anterior talofibular ligament is injured first, then the calcaneofibular ligament; the deltoid (medial) ligament is much stronger and usually survives.
  • Malleolar fractures are classified by their relation to the tibiofibular syndesmosis (Danis–Weber); a medial malleolar fracture is commonly associated with a fibular fracture or a syndesmotic injury.
  • Dislocation of the fibular tendons at the lateral malleolus follows rupture of the superior fibular retinaculum and causes a snapping sensation behind the lateral malleolus.
  • Great saphenous vein cut-down is performed anterior to the medial malleolus, the most accessible part of the vein in an emergency.
Likely follow-up questions
  • What is the tarsal tunnel and what forms its roof? — The fibro-osseous space behind the medial malleolus, roofed by the flexor retinaculum.
  • Which tendon lies in a separate groove on the talus and sustentaculum tali? — The flexor hallucis longus tendon, the most posterolateral structure in the tunnel.
  • Which nerve supplies the heel? — The medial calcaneal branches of the tibial nerve (and the sural nerve laterally).
  • Which structures cross the lateral malleolar region superficially? — The small saphenous vein and the sural nerve, posterior to the malleolus.
  • Why is the medial malleolus more prone to pressure necrosis from a cast? — It is subcutaneous and overlies the tarsal tunnel, so pressure here compresses the tibial nerve and vessels.

II.19Dorsum of the foot: muscles, fasciae, tendons, tendon sheaths, vessels and nerves.

Core answerThe dorsum of the foot is covered by thin skin and loose superficial fascia containing the dorsal venous arch, the great and small saphenous veins and the cutaneous nerves (saphenous, superficial fibular, deep fibular, sural). Its deep fascia forms the superior and inferior extensor retinacula, under which the long extensor tendons and their synovial sheaths pass: tibialis anterior, extensor hallucis longus, extensor digitorum longus and fibularis tertius. The intrinsic muscles are the extensor digitorum brevis and extensor hallucis brevis, both supplied by the deep fibular nerve. The arterial supply is the dorsalis pedis artery, with the deep fibular nerve lateral to it; the lateral part of the dorsum is supplied by the superficial fibular nerve.

1.Skin, superficial fascia and superficial veins

  • Skin is thin, mobile and hair-bearing on the dorsum; it is loosely attached by a thin layer of superficial fascia, so dorsalis pedis pulsation and tendon contours are visible.
  • Dorsal venous arch (arcus venosus dorsalis pedis) lies over the metatarsals and receives the dorsal digital veins; the great saphenous vein arises from its medial end and the small saphenous vein from its lateral end.
  • Cutaneous nerves of the dorsum: the saphenous nerve (medial side), superficial fibular nerve (most of the dorsum and toes), deep fibular nerve (first web space) and sural nerve (lateral side and little toe).

2.Deep fascia and retinacula

The deep fascia of the dorsum is continuous above with the deep fascia of the leg and below with the dorsal fascia of the toes. It is thickened into two extensor retinacula:

  • Superior extensor retinaculum — a transverse thickening of deep fascia just above the ankle joint, attached to the anterior borders of the tibia and fibula.
  • Inferior extensor retinaculum — Y-shaped; its base attaches to the lateral side of the upper surface of the calcaneus, one arm crosses medially to attach to the medial malleolus, and the other wraps around the medial side of the foot to the plantar aponeurosis.
  • Fibular retinacula — superior and inferior, holding the fibularis longus and brevis tendons on the lateral side of the foot.
  • The retinacula prevent bowstringing of the extensor tendons during dorsiflexion.

4.Tendon sheaths

Each long extensor tendon has a synovial sheath where it passes under the extensor retinacula; the sheaths extend a short distance into the dorsum of the foot. The sheath of the extensor hallucis longus and that of the tibialis anterior extend furthest distally and are the ones most commonly involved in tenosynovitis. The sheaths are separate from each other, so infection spreads along a single tendon rather than across the dorsum.

5.Vessels of the dorsum

  • Dorsalis pedis artery (arteria dorsalis pedis) — continuation of the anterior tibial artery as it crosses the ankle joint; passes over the talus, navicular and intermediate cuneiform, and between the two heads of the first dorsal interosseous muscle becomes the deep plantar artery joining the deep plantar arch.
  • Branches: medial and lateral tarsal arteries, the arcuate artery (giving three dorsal metatarsal arteries), the first dorsal metatarsal artery and the deep plantar artery.
  • The pulse of the dorsalis pedis is felt against the tarsal bones between the tendons of the extensor hallucis longus and the extensor digitorum longus to the second toe.
  • Dorsal metatarsal arteries anastomose with the plantar metatarsal arteries through perforating branches, so the dorsum and sole form one functional arterial unit.

Must-memorise
  • Dorsum muscles: extensor digitorum brevis and extensor hallucis brevis — both deep fibular nerve (S1, S2); they are the only intrinsic foot muscles not supplied by the tibial nerve.
  • Order under the extensor retinacula: tibialis anterior, extensor hallucis longus, dorsalis pedis artery, extensor digitorum longus and fibularis tertius.
  • Dorsalis pedis branches: tarsal, arcuate, first dorsal metatarsal, deep plantar.
  • Cutaneous map: saphenous medial, sural lateral, superficial fibular most of the dorsum, deep fibular first web space.
  • Arterial pulses of the foot: dorsalis pedis on the dorsum, posterior tibial behind the medial malleolus.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Absent dorsalis pedis pulse may be normal when the artery is replaced by a large perforating branch of the fibular artery; the posterior tibial pulse and Doppler are then used to assess the foot.
  • Laceration of the dorsum of the foot may divide extensor tendons and the dorsalis pedis artery; the tendons retract and must be repaired, and the artery is ligated if necessary because of the rich collateral supply.
  • Foot drop (deep fibular nerve palsy) abolishes active dorsiflexion and extension of the toes, and the dorsalis pedis pulse remains palpable.
  • The dorsal venous arch is a common site for peripheral venous cannulation in children and in patients with difficult veins.
  • Tenosynovitis of the extensor sheaths presents as a painful, swollen dorsum with pain on passive flexion of the toes; it may follow a penetrating injury or gonococcal infection.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Which muscle is the only intrinsic muscle of the dorsum supplied by the deep fibular nerve and why does it matter clinically? — Extensor digitorum brevis; it is a landmark for the deep fibular nerve and is tested by extending toes II–IV against resistance.
  • What forms the extensor hood and what is its function? — The expansion of the extensor tendons over the proximal phalanges, receiving intrinsic muscle insertions and distributing force so that the toe flexes at the metatarsophalangeal joint while extending at the interphalangeal joints.
  • Where is the dorsalis pedis artery palpated? — On the dorsum, lateral to the extensor hallucis longus tendon and medial to the tendon to the second toe.
  • Which nerve supplies the skin between the first and second toes? — The deep fibular nerve.
  • What is the venous drainage of the dorsum? — The dorsal venous arch, with the great saphenous vein medially and the small saphenous vein laterally.

II.20Sole (planta) of the foot: muscles, tendons, fasciae, compartments, vessels and nerves.

Core answerThe sole (planta pedis) is covered by thick, keratinised skin and a strong plantar aponeurosis; its deep fascia and intermuscular septa divide the soft tissues into medial, central and lateral compartments. The intrinsic muscles are arranged in four layers from plantar to dorsal — abductor hallucis, flexor digitorum brevis and abductor digiti minimi; quadratus plantae and the four lumbricals; flexor hallucis brevis, adductor hallucis and flexor digiti minimi brevis; and the dorsal and plantar interossei. The long tendons of tibialis posterior, flexor digitorum longus, flexor hallucis longus and fibularis longus enter the sole and are guided by synovial sheaths. The sole is supplied by the medial and lateral plantar arteries (from the posterior tibial) — the lateral plantar artery forming the deep plantar arch with the deep plantar artery of the dorsalis pedis — and by the medial and lateral plantar nerves (from the tibial nerve).

1.Skin, superficial fascia and plantar aponeurosis

  • Skin: very thick, especially over the heel and the metatarsal heads, firmly bound to the underlying aponeurosis by fibrous septa; it is hairless and richly innervated, and the thick stratum corneum protects the weight-bearing surface.
  • Superficial fascia: contains a dense fat pad over the heel and the ball of the foot, divided into loculi by fibrous septa that act as shock absorbers.
  • Plantar aponeurosis (aponeurosis plantaris) — the thickened deep fascia of the sole; it is anchored to the medial process of the calcaneal tuberosity and extends forward as longitudinal fibres that diverge into digital bands for the toes; distally the bands are joined by transverse fibres (superficial transverse metatarsal ligaments). It holds the skin of the sole, protects the vessels and nerves, and supports the longitudinal arch.
  • Medial and lateral plantar septa pass from the margins of the aponeurosis to the bones, dividing the sole into compartments.

4.Long tendons crossing the sole and their sheaths

  • Tibialis posterior — its tendon enters the sole behind the medial malleolus and inserts mainly on the navicular tuberosity and medial cuneiform; it inverts the foot and supports the medial arch.
  • Flexor digitorum longus — divides into four tendons for the lateral four toes; each tendon passes through the split of the corresponding flexor digitorum brevis tendon (the chiasma tendinum) and inserts on the distal phalanx.
  • Flexor hallucis longus — the strongest flexor of the great toe; its tendon runs in the tarsal tunnel, crosses the sole and passes between the two sesamoid bones of the flexor hallucis brevis to the distal phalanx.
  • Fibularis longus — its tendon crosses the sole in a fibrous tunnel deep to the long plantar ligament to reach the medial cuneiform and base of metatarsal I; it everts the foot and supports the transverse arch.
  • Sheaths: the flexor tendons are surrounded by synovial sheaths in the tarsal tunnel and in the digits (digital fibrous sheaths), which are separate from the sheaths of the dorsum — hence a deep-space infection of the sole tends to remain plantar.

5.Vessels of the sole

  • Posterior tibial artery enters the sole through the tarsal tunnel, gives a small medial plantar artery and a much larger lateral plantar artery.
  • Lateral plantar artery passes anterolaterally between the quadratus plantae and flexor digitorum brevis, reaches the base of metatarsal V, and curves medially as the deep plantar arch on the metatarsal bases and interossei.
  • The arch is completed between metatarsals I and II by the deep plantar artery (terminal branch of the dorsalis pedis) and gives four plantar metatarsal arteries, digital branches and three perforating arteries to the dorsum.
  • Medial plantar artery supplies muscles and skin of the medial side of the sole and ends by joining the digital branch to the medial side of the great toe.
  • The pulse of the posterior tibial artery is felt in the tarsal tunnel and, more distally, between the medial malleolus and the heel.

7.Arches of the foot

  • Medial longitudinal arch — calcaneus, talus, navicular, cuneiforms and metatarsals I–III; the higher arch, supported by the plantar aponeurosis, the plantar calcaneonavicular (spring) ligament, and the tendons of tibialis posterior and tibialis anterior.
  • Lateral longitudinal arch — calcaneus, cuboid and metatarsals IV–V; flatter and more rigid, supported by the long and short plantar ligaments and the plantar aponeurosis.
  • Transverse arch — formed by the cuneiforms, cuboid and metatarsal bases; supported by the fibularis longus tendon, which crosses the sole obliquely.
  • The intrinsic muscles act as dynamic supports of the arches during standing and walking, especially the first layer and the flexor hallucis brevis.
Must-memorise
  • Four layers, plantar to dorsal: (1) abductor hallucis, flexor digitorum brevis, abductor digiti minimi; (2) quadratus plantae, four lumbricals; (3) flexor hallucis brevis, adductor hallucis, flexor digiti minimi brevis; (4) dorsal and plantar interossei.
  • Medial plantar nerve supplies four muscles: abductor hallucis, flexor digitorum brevis, flexor hallucis brevis, first lumbrical.
  • Lateral plantar nerve supplies everything else, including all interossei.
  • Deep plantar arch = lateral plantar artery + deep plantar artery of the dorsalis pedis, between metatarsals I and II.
  • Long tendons entering the sole: tibialis posterior, flexor digitorum longus, flexor hallucis longus, fibularis longus.
  • Three compartments: medial, central, lateral; the interossei lie deepest between the metatarsals.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Plantar fasciitis — degeneration of the plantar aponeurosis at its calcaneal insertion; heel pain on first steps in the morning, thickening of the aponeurosis on MRI, and sometimes a calcaneal spur.
  • Flat foot (pes planus) — loss of the medial longitudinal arch, often from dysfunction of the tibialis posterior tendon in adults; the arch fails to recover on toe-standing.
  • Morton's neuroma — enlargement of a common plantar nerve, usually in the third interspace, with pain on walking and a sensation of a pebble under the ball of the foot.
  • Foot compartment syndrome after crush injury produces tense swelling, pain on passive toe movement and sensory loss in the sole; it requires emergency fasciotomy.
  • Diabetic foot — sensory neuropathy removes protective sensation, so pressure at the metatarsal heads and heel produces painless plantar ulcers; the plantar arterial supply is assessed by the posterior tibial and dorsalis pedis pulses.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Which muscles form the first layer of the sole and how are they arranged? — From medial to lateral: abductor hallucis, flexor digitorum brevis, abductor digiti minimi.
  • Which muscle is supplied by the medial plantar nerve but belongs to the second layer? — The first lumbrical.
  • What crosses the sole to reach the medial side of the foot? — The tendon of fibularis longus, which supports the transverse arch.
  • Which arteries form the deep plantar arch and where does it lie? — The lateral plantar artery and the deep plantar branch of the dorsalis pedis; it lies on the bases of the metatarsals and the interossei, deep in the central compartment.
  • Why is the skin of the sole both thick and highly sensitive? — It is a weight-bearing friction surface with a thick keratin layer and fibrous septa binding it to the aponeurosis, but it is also richly innervated for protective pain sensation and position sense.
  • Which ligaments support the medial longitudinal arch? — The plantar calcaneonavicular (spring) ligament, the long and short plantar ligaments and the plantar aponeurosis, assisted by tibialis posterior and tibialis anterior.
