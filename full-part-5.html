V.1Kidney (HE)

Core answerA coronal section of the kidney (ren) stained with haematoxylin and eosin: a compound tubular gland in which the cortex contains renal corpuscles with glomeruli and two kinds of convoluted tubule, while the medulla contains parallel straight tubules, collecting ducts and vasa recta. The round glomerulus inside Bowman's capsule is the structure that identifies the organ at first glance.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Organ level, low-power overview, cut in the coronal (frontal) plane through the hilum so that cortex, medulla and a papilla with its minor calyx are all visible. Set the slide up at low power first: the examiner expects the plane of section and the direction of the layers before any cytology.

2.Low-power architecture

From the surface inwards: a thin fibrous capsule; the cortex (cortex renalis), which looks granular because it is packed with round renal corpuscles (corpuscula renalia); the medulla (medulla renalis), made of parallel straight tubules and ducts running towards the papilla; and at the hilum a papilla opening into a minor calyx lined by urothelium. Conical medullary rays (pars radiata) straddle cortex and medulla: they are bundles of straight tubules that appear to radiate out from the medulla, and the tissue between them is the convoluted part containing the corpuscles. The cortex also dips between pyramids as the renal columns (of Bertini).

3.High-power key features

Renal corpuscle: a tuft of glomerular capillaries inside a clear urinary space, bounded by the thin squamous parietal sheet of Bowman's capsule. The afferent and efferent arterioles enter at the vascular pole; the proximal tubule leaves at the tubular pole. The proximal tubule (tubulus contortus proximalis) is lined by cuboidal cells with strongly eosinophilic cytoplasm and a brush border that narrows the lumen to a star shape. The distal tubule (tubulus contortus distalis) has cuboidal cells of medium eosinophilia, no brush border and a wider, clearly open lumen; its wall beside the vascular pole thickens into the macula densa. Thin limbs are simple squamous and pale. Collecting ducts (ductus colligens) have the widest pale lumina, with principal cells and scattered darker intercalated cells. In the medulla the straight tubules run in parallel, separated by a fine interstitium containing the vasa recta.

4.Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

HE: nuclei are basophilic blue-violet (haematoxylin), cytoplasm and basement membranes eosinophilic pink. The proximal tubules are the most eosinophilic structures on the slide; the glomerular tuft is moderately eosinophilic with many blue nuclei; the medullary interstitium is pale. If further stains are asked for: PAS is positive in the brush border and in the glomerular and tubular basement membranes, silver impregnation blackens the reticular fibres of the interstitium, and immunohistochemistry can mark renin in the juxtaglomerular cells.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Round glomerulus inside a clear space inside a thin capsule — no other organ has this.
  • Side-by-side eosinophilic proximal and paler distal tubules in the cortex.
  • Medulla = parallel straight tubules and wide collecting ducts, no glomeruli.
  • Papilla lined by urothelium gives you the cortex-to-medulla direction.
Differential diagnosis
  • Liver: uniform lobules with sinusoids, no corpuscles, no tubules.
  • Thyroid gland: follicles filled with colloid, not tubular profiles.
  • Pancreas: serous acini plus islets, no glomeruli.
  • Ureter or urinary bladder: urothelium over a thick muscular wall, no cortex.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Renal biopsy is reported on HE together with PAS (basement membranes), silver (interstitium) and immunofluorescence; one slide lets glomeruli, tubules and vessels be judged together.
  • Proximal tubules are the segment most vulnerable to ischaemia and nephrotoxins, hence acute tubular necrosis in shock and drug toxicity.
  • Juxtaglomerular cells of the afferent arteriole secrete renin and the macula densa senses distal sodium chloride; these two structures are the anatomical basis of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system.

V.2Jejunum (HE)

Core answerA transverse section of the jejunum (jejunum) stained with HE: a small intestine in which the mucosa carries villi and crypts lined by simple columnar epithelium with goblet cells, the submucosa contains no glands, and the muscular layer has an inner circular and an outer longitudinal layer. Villi plus crypts together, and the absence of submucosal glands and of large lymphoid aggregates, identify it as jejunum rather than duodenum or ileum.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Organ level, transverse section through the wall of the intestinal tube. Four layers are expected in the answer: mucosa (epithelium, lamina propria, muscularis mucosae), submucosa, tunica muscularis and serosa. Villi fill the lumen and may be cut longitudinally, obliquely or transversely, so at low power they look like an irregular fringe.

2.Low-power architecture

Lumen containing desquamated cells and mucus; then the mucosa, which is dominated by finger-like villi (villi intestinales) whose cores are lamina propria. Below the villi, the epithelium invaginates into the crypts of Lieberkühn (cryptae intestinales). A thin layer of smooth muscle, the muscularis mucosae (lamina muscularis mucosae), separates mucosa from the loose submucosa, which carries vessels but no gland mass. The tunica muscularis shows the inner circular and outer longitudinal layers with the myenteric plexus between them, and the outer surface is covered by serosa.

3.High-power key features

The epithelium is simple columnar: tall enterocytes with an eosinophilic striated border (brush border) and oval basal nuclei, among which sit goblet cells — pale, cup-shaped, with the nucleus squeezed to the base and the mucin granules washed out during processing. At the base of the crypts there are Paneth cells with eosinophilic apical granules, plus stem cells and scattered enteroendocrine cells. The lamina propria is loose connective tissue with lymphocytes, plasma cells, eosinophils, capillaries and a central lacteal in each villus. Each villus core also contains smooth muscle fibres continuous with the muscularis mucosae.

4.Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

HE: enterocyte cytoplasm is eosinophilic, nuclei basophilic; goblet-cell mucin is pale and may look empty. To prove the mucus say PAS stains goblet-cell granules and the brush border magenta/purple-red, and Alcian blue stains acid mucins blue-green. Paneth cell granules are strongly eosinophilic; collagen of the submucosa is eosinophilic and would be blue/green with a trichrome.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Villi and crypts present together — the signature of the small intestine.
  • Simple columnar epithelium with goblet cells; no villus goblet-cell-free sheet of stomach type.
  • No glands in the submucosa (contrast duodenum) and no large Peyer's patches (contrast ileum).
  • Four-layered wall with a clear muscularis mucosae and a serosa.
Differential diagnosis
  • Duodenum: Brunner's mucous glands occupy the submucosa.
  • Ileum: large lymphatic nodules (Peyer's patches) in the lamina propria and submucosa.
  • Colon: crypts but no villi, and far more goblet cells.
  • Stomach: gastric pits and glands, no villi.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Coeliac disease is diagnosed on oriented small-intestinal biopsies: villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia and intraepithelial lymphocytes.
  • The jejunum is the main site of absorption, so resection causes malabsorption and short-bowel syndrome.
  • Goblet cells are the source of the mucus barrier; their number and the mucin staining are used in reporting intestinal metaplasia.

V.3Unicellular gland (PAS + H)

Core answerA PAS–haematoxylin preparation of an epithelium containing unicellular glands: the goblet cells (exocrinocyti caliciformes) appear as goblet-shaped cells whose mucin granules are strongly PAS-positive magenta, while the nuclei are counterstained blue with haematoxylin. A single mucus-secreting cell sitting inside an epithelium is the definition of a unicellular exocrine gland.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Cellular level inside an epithelium — the slide is often a high-power view of intestinal or respiratory mucosa (jejunal villus, colonic crypt, tracheal mucosa). The cut may follow the long axis of the goblet cells (goblet shape visible) or graze the surface (only round magenta caps visible).

2.Low-power architecture

The epithelium is a ribbon of cells with scattered, intensely magenta dots or cups at regular intervals. Beware of three other magenta lines that belong to the method, not to the gland: the basement membrane beneath the epithelium, reticular fibres, and the apical brush border are all PAS-positive. Mucus lying free in the lumen may also be magenta. On the section of intestine you can orient yourself by the villi or crypts; in the respiratory tract by the underlying cartilage or glands.

3.High-power key features

Goblet cell: narrow base resting on the basement membrane, and a swollen apical theca filled with mucinogen granules. The nucleus is oval to triangular, compressed basally by the granules, with dense chromatin. The granules are PAS-positive magenta; in HE they are washed out and the cell looks empty and pale. Secretion is merocrine (exocytosis of granules); after discharge the cell is short and its theca collapses. Neighbouring cells are enterocytes with a striated border, or ciliated and basal cells in the respiratory epithelium. Goblet cells are never grouped into acini and never possess a duct of their own: each cell opens directly onto the surface.

4.Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

The PAS reaction (periodic acid + Schiff reagent) oxidises vicinal diols of polysaccharides to aldehydes, and the Schiff reagent then gives a magenta/purple-red product. It marks glycogen, neutral glycosaminoglycans, mucin, glycoproteins, basement membranes, reticular fibres and the walls of fungi. The diastase (amylase) control is the elegant point: if the staining disappears after diastase, the material is glycogen; if it persists, it is another polysaccharide such as mucin. Haematoxylin counterstains nuclei blue. Acid mucins would also stain with Alcian blue (blue-green).

How to identify it at the exam
  • Single goblet-shaped magenta cells standing in a row of non-staining epithelial cells.
  • The magenta material is inside the apical part of the cell, not in a duct or acinus.
  • Nucleus squeezed to the base — a gland cell that secretes directly to the surface.
  • Diastase control separates mucin from glycogen staining.
Differential diagnosis
  • Multicellular mucous gland (sublingual, submandibular mucous tubules): magenta cells grouped into acini/tubules with ducts and interlobular septa.
  • Gastric surface mucous cells: PAS-positive but form a continuous simple columnar sheet, not individual goblet cells.
  • Basement membrane or brush border: a thin continuous magenta line, not a cell-shaped drop.
  • Paneth cells: eosinophilic apical granules that are not PAS-positive mucin.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Goblet-cell hyperplasia is a response to chronic irritation (chronic bronchitis, asthma) and thickens the mucus layer.
  • Goblet cells normally absent from the oesophagus; their appearance there marks intestinal metaplasia (Barrett oesophagus) and is demonstrated with PAS or Alcian blue.
  • In cystic fibrosis the mucus is abnormally viscid, so the mucociliary and intestinal mucus barriers fail.

V.4Trachea (HE)

Core answerA transverse section of the trachea (trachea) stained with HE: respiratory epithelium (ciliated pseudostratified columnar with goblet cells) resting on a very thick basement membrane, a lamina propria with seromucous glands, and dorsally open horseshoe-shaped pieces of hyaline cartilage. The cartilage is what separates trachea from every other tubular organ in the slide set.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Organ level, transverse section through the wall of the airway; the lumen is round to oval. Three concentric layers are named: mucosa, fibromusculocartilaginous layer and adventitia. Because the cartilage is a series of separate C-shaped pieces, one section may show one large plate or the gap of the membranous wall with the trachealis muscle.

2.Low-power architecture

The epithelium is thrown into longitudinal folds, so the luminal border is wavy. Beneath it lies a pale lamina propria, then the cartilage: glassy, slightly basophilic plates with a perichondrium, occupying roughly the outer third of the wall. Between the mucosa and the cartilage you find seromucous tracheal glands — pale, clustered acini with ducts running towards the lumen. On the dorsal (membranous) side the cartilage is replaced by a band of smooth muscle, the trachealis muscle (musculus trachealis), and elastic fibres. The outer adventitia is loose connective tissue.

3.High-power key features

The epithelium is ciliated pseudostratified columnar: nuclei lie at several levels, giving a false impression of stratification. Cell types: ciliated cells with an apical fringe of cilia, goblet cells with pale mucin, basal cells resting on the basement membrane, and occasional brush and neuroendocrine cells. The basement membrane is strikingly thick (2–4 µm) and eosinophilic. The glands are tubuloalveolar seromucous: mucous tubules capped by serous demilunes, with ducts lined by cuboidal to columnar cells. The cartilage is hyaline: chondrocytes in lacunae, often in isogenous groups, with a perichondrium and no vessels inside the matrix. The trachealis is a compact bundle of smooth muscle with elongated nuclei.

4.Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

HE: cartilage matrix is pale to slightly basophilic, nuclei blue; cytoplasm of ciliated cells eosinophilic; the thick basement membrane and the serous demilunes are strongly eosinophilic; mucous cells are pale. PAS stains the goblet-cell mucin and the basement membrane magenta; Alcian blue marks acid mucin of the glands; orcein (red-brown) shows the elastic fibre layer of the lamina propria; and the cartilage would be metachromatic with toluidine blue.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Pseudostratified ciliated epithelium on a very thick basement membrane.
  • Hyaline cartilage plate in the wall, with a perichondrium.
  • Seromucous glands with serous demilunes between mucosa and cartilage.
  • Dorsal membranous wall = smooth trachealis muscle, no cartilage.
Differential diagnosis
  • Oesophagus: non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, no cartilage, has a muscularis mucosae.
  • Bronchus: cartilage in small irregular plates, a complete ring of smooth muscle between mucosa and cartilage, fewer glands.
  • Larynx: elastic cartilage and stratified squamous epithelium over the vocal fold.
  • Skin: keratinized stratified squamous epithelium with appendages.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Prolonged intubation causes fibrotic narrowing — post-intubation tracheal stenosis with stridor.
  • Primary ciliary dyskinesia (dynein defect) causes failed mucociliary clearance, recurrent infection and often situs inversus (Kartagener syndrome).
  • Smoking and chronic tracheitis reduce cilia, increase goblet cells and enlarge the tracheal glands — the morphological basis of chronic bronchitis.

V.5Oesophagus (HE)

Core answerA transverse section of the oesophagus (oesophagus) stained with HE: a thick non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium over a lamina propria, a well-developed muscularis mucosae, a submucosa with small mucous glands, and a muscular layer whose upper third is skeletal and lower third smooth muscle. The combination of stratified squamous epithelium with a muscularis mucosae and submucosal glands is diagnostic.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Organ level, transverse section of this collapsed muscular tube; the lumen is irregular and star-shaped because of longitudinal mucosal folds. Four layers: mucosa, submucosa, tunica muscularis and adventitia (serosa only in the short abdominal segment).

2.Low-power architecture

The epithelium is pale pink and thick, with connective tissue papillae indenting its base. Beneath it is the lamina propria, then the muscularis mucosae — a distinctly thicker longitudinal smooth muscle layer than elsewhere in the digestive tract, which helps to identify the oesophagus. The submucosa is loose and contains small mucous glands (glandulae submucosae oesophagei) and blood vessels. The tunica muscularis consists of an inner circular and an outer longitudinal layer with Auerbach's myenteric plexus between them; the outer border is adventitia in the thorax.

3.High-power key features

The epithelium is non-keratinized stratified squamous: a basal layer of dividing columnar cells, a thick polygonal (prickle) layer with prominent desmosomes, and flattened superficial cells that retain their nuclei and look clear because they are rich in glycogen. No keratin layer and no goblet cells are present, but lymphocytes and melanocytes may lie between the basal cells. The submucosal mucous glands are small and branched, with pale mucous acini; their ducts open through the mucosa. Near the lower end, oesophageal cardiac glands may appear in the lamina propria. The muscular layer is striated in its upper third (non-cardiac visceral striated muscle), mixed in the middle third, and smooth in the lower third.

4.Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

HE: the epithelium is eosinophilic with basophilic nuclei; the superficial cell layers are pale due to glycogen; the submucosal glands are pale mucinous. With PAS the glycogen of the superficial cells and the mucin of the submucosal glands react, but the pattern is diffuse rather than the crisp magenta cup of a goblet cell. A trichrome makes the collagen of the lamina propria green/blue, separating it from the pink epithelium and muscle. Silver stains the reticular fibre of the lamina propria.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Thick non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium without keratin and without goblet cells.
  • Conspicuous muscularis mucosae and small submucosal mucous glands.
  • Star-shaped lumen with mucosal folds.
  • Two muscle layers of skeletal, mixed or smooth type depending on the level.
Differential diagnosis
  • Trachea: ciliated pseudostratified epithelium, cartilage plates, no muscularis mucosae.
  • Stomach (cardia): simple columnar epithelium with gastric pits; no stratified squamous sheet.
  • Vagina: similar stratified squamous epithelium, but a fibromuscular wall without muscularis mucosae and without glands.
  • Thin skin: keratinized stratified squamous epithelium with appendages.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Gastro-oesophageal reflux produces basal-zone hyperplasia, elongation of the CT papillae and eosinophils; long-standing reflux can cause intestinal metaplasia (Barrett oesophagus).
  • Oesophageal varices form from the submucosal venous plexus in portal hypertension, which is why they bleed so profusely.
  • Squamous cell carcinoma arises from this epithelium, and the loss of myenteric neurons is the basis of achalasia.

V.6Finger pad (HE)

Core answerA vertical section through the pad of a fingertip — thick, glabrous skin stained with HE: a very thick keratinized epidermis with a prominent stratum corneum and a visible stratum lucidum, dermal papillae carrying Meissner corpuscles, eccrine sweat ducts spiralling to the surface, and Pacinian corpuscles deep in the subcutaneous tissue. There are no hair follicles and no sebaceous glands.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Organ level, vertical section through skin plus subcutaneous tissue; identify the free surface and orient the slide with the epidermis upwards. The section runs across the skin crests and grooves (cristae et sulci cutis) of the finger pad, so the surface is wavy — the anatomical basis of fingerprints.

2.Low-power architecture

From the surface down: a massive, homogeneous, strongly eosinophilic stratum corneum; the cellular epidermis; the dermis, divided into a papillary layer (loose connective tissue with the dermal papillae) and a reticular layer (dense irregular connective tissue); and the subcutaneous tissue dominated by adipose cells. Coiled eccrine glands lie in the deep dermis and subcutis, and their ducts run as spirals through the epidermis. Nerves and vessels run in the dermis, with Pacinian corpuscles at the dermis–subcutis junction.

3.High-power key features

Epidermis, from base to surface: stratum basale (a single layer of columnar keratinocytes on a corrugated basement membrane, with melanocytes and Merkel cells), stratum spinosum (several layers of polygonal cells with spiny processes and tonofibrils), stratum granulosum (basophilic keratohyalin granules, lamellar granules), stratum lucidum (a thin, strongly eosinophilic line of dead cells containing eleidin, visible only in thick skin), and the stratum corneum (dead anucleate keratinized scales, much thicker than the whole living epidermis). In each dermal papilla there is a capillary loop and often a Meissner corpuscle — an oval, lamellated tactile corpuscle lying just under the epidermis. The eccrine secretory coil is lined by clear and dark cells with myoepithelial cells; its duct has two layers of cuboidal cells and spirals through the epidermis to a sweat pore. The Pacinian corpuscle is a large onion-like lamellated structure in the deep dermis/subcutis.

4.Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

HE: keratin and the stratum lucidum are strongly eosinophilic; keratohyalin granules are basophilic; nuclei of the living layers are blue; collagen of the dermis is pink and elastic fibres are poorly seen. To show the elastic network use orcein (red-brown) or resorcin-fuchsin (brown-purple to blue-black); PAS outlines the basement membrane and the glycogen of the sweat-gland clear cells; silver impregnation demonstrates the nerve endings and Meissner corpuscles; Sudan staining of a frozen section shows the lipid of the subcutaneous adipocytes.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Thick skin: stratum corneum thicker than the living epidermis, and a visible stratum lucidum.
  • No hair follicles, no sebaceous glands, no apocrine glands — only eccrine sweat glands.
  • Sweat ducts spiralling through the epidermis to pores on the crests.
  • Meissner corpuscles in the dermal papillae; Pacinian corpuscles deep.
Differential diagnosis
  • Thin (hairy) skin: thin stratum corneum, no stratum lucidum, hair follicles and sebaceous glands present.
  • Lip: shows a transition from keratinized skin to non-keratinized mucosa with labial glands.
  • Oesophagus or vagina: non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, no skin appendages.
  • Sole skin: the same thick type in principle, so argue the site from the overall architecture and, if present, the Meissner corpuscle density.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Dermatoglyphs (fingerprints) are formed by the epidermal crests and are individually stable — the basis of forensic identification.
  • Meissner corpuscles detect fine touch and low-frequency vibration; Pacinian corpuscles detect deep pressure and higher-frequency vibration. Their loss is part of diabetic peripheral neuropathy.
  • Blocked eccrine ducts cause miliaria (prickly heat); the thick epidermis regenerates from the stratum basale after superficial burns.

V.7Submandibular gland (HE)

Core answerA section of the submandibular gland (glandula submandibularis) stained with HE: a mixed seromucous salivary gland, predominantly serous, in which basophilic serous acini and pale mucous tubules are capped by serous demilunes, and the secretion drains through intercalated, striated and interlobular ducts. The combination of mixed acini, demilunes and striated ducts is the classic examination picture.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Organ level, section of a lobulated gland: the parenchyma is divided into lobules by connective tissue septa, with interlobular ducts running in those septa. The cut is random, so acini, tubules and ducts of several orders are all visible in one field.

2.Low-power architecture

The gland is tubuloacinar and compound: secretory end pieces of two tinctorial types (dark blue serous acini and pale mucous tubules) are packed into lobules; the pale rounded spaces between acini are adipose cells. In the septa, large interlobular ducts have a visible lumen. Within the lobule, ducts of smaller calibre are seen between the acini. Capsule surrounds the whole gland. There are no islets of Langerhans and no follicular structures.

3.High-power key features

Serous acinus: pyramidal cells arranged around a small central lumen; basal cytoplasm strongly basophilic because of rough endoplasmic reticulum, apical cytoplasm packed with eosinophilic zymogen granules; round nucleus in the basal third. Mucous tubule: pale, foamy cells with flattened basal nuclei and clear apical mucin. Serous demilune: a crescent of serous cells applied to the end of a mucous tubule (largely a fixation artefact of two cell types that are adjacent in life, but a standard examinable feature). Ducts, in order: intercalated duct — small, low cuboidal, pale; striated duct — columnar cells with basal striations produced by mitochondria and basal membrane infoldings; interlobular duct — taller cuboidal to columnar epithelium in the septa. Myoepithelial cells sit between the secretory cells and their basement membrane and squeeze the secretion out.

  • Tracheal glands: the same seromucous design, but inside the wall of the airway with cartilage nearby.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Saliva is modified as it passes along the striated duct by sodium reabsorption and potassium/bicarbonate secretion, which is why striated ducts are the most metabolically active duct segment.
  • Sialolithiasis most often obstructs the submandibular duct (Wharton's duct) because its secretion is the most mucinous and its course runs upwards.
  • Inflammatory and immune sialadenitis (Sjögren syndrome) destroys acini with a lymphocytic infiltrate; mixed tumours (pleomorphic adenoma) arise from duct and myoepithelial cells.

V.8Tendon (HE)

Core answerA longitudinal section of a tendon (tendo) stained with HE: dense regular connective tissue (textus connectivus compactus regularis) made of parallel bundles of strongly eosinophilic type I collagen fibres, with rows of flattened, spindle-shaped fibrocyte nuclei between the bundles and no cells or vessels inside them.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Tissue and organ level; the section is longitudinal, so the bundles run parallel across the field. Some slides include the myotendinous junction (muscle fibres of the free end) or the osteotendinous junction; state which end you see, because the crimp and the branching pattern change there.

2.Low-power architecture

Long, parallel, wavy bundles of collagen fill almost the whole field. Between the bundles run thin septa of loose connective tissue, the endotenon (endotenonium), carrying blood vessels and nerves; the surface of the tendon is wrapped by a thin epitenon and, where present, a paratenon. There is no epithelium, no gland and no cartilage.

3.High-power key features

Collagen bundles: type I collagen fibres, 1–10 µm thick and of indefinite length, grouped into bundles; they run parallel to the line of pull and show a gentle wave (crimp) that straightens under tension. Cells: fibrocytes/tenocytes squeezed between bundles, with elongated, flattened, sharp-pointed (spindle) nuclei arranged in rows; cytoplasm is scanty and the cells never lie in lacunae. Ground substance is rich in dermatan sulfate and contains some elastic fibres. At the point of attachment to bone the tendon fibres continue as Sharpey fibres into the bone matrix.

HE: bundles are intensely eosinophilic and nuclei basophilic; no striation and no sarcoplasm. Ask for a collagen stain to prove the type: picrosirius red stains collagen red, and in polarised light type I collagen appears red to yellow while type III appears green; aniline blue stains collagen blue; orcein would show only the scanty elastic fibres red-brown. Reticulin (silver) is present only around the endotenon vessels, not around the bundles.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Parallel eosinophilic collagen bundles running across the whole field.
  • Thin, sharp-ended fibrocyte nuclei in rows between bundles.
  • No cells inside the bundles, no striation, no lacunae.
  • Endotenon septa with vessels at regular intervals.
Differential diagnosis
  • Smooth muscle (longitudinal): spindle cells with central cigar-shaped nuclei and real cytoplasm; bundles not wavy; no endotenon.
  • Peripheral nerve (longitudinal): wavy fibres with myelin sheaths and perineurium, not homogeneous eosinophilic bundles.
  • Fibrocartilage: chondrocytes in lacunae arranged in rows between coarse collagen bundles.
  • Dense irregular connective tissue (dermis): bundles run in all directions.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Tendons are hypovascular, so healing after rupture is slow and scar-dominated; the Achilles tendon ruptures in its relatively avascular zone.
  • Tendinopathy is a degenerative process with disorganised collagen, not a pure inflammation; the crimp pattern and fibre alignment are the histological markers of normal function.
  • The Golgi tendon organ at the myotendinous junction senses tension and inhibits the muscle through the tendon reflex, protecting the tendon from overload.

V.9Adipose tissue (HE)

Core answerWhite adipose tissue (textus adiposus albus) stained with HE: tightly packed unilocular adipocytes (adipocyti uniloculares) whose single large lipid droplet has been dissolved out during paraffin processing, leaving clear, round spaces limited by a thin eosinophilic rim of cytoplasm with a flattened peripheral nucleus — the "chicken-wire" pattern.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Tissue level, taken from subcutaneous fat, mesentery, renal sinus or epicardium. The section may be a thin sheet (spread preparation of peritoneum) or a block with lobules separated by septa. Conventional HE cannot show the lipid itself, so identify the tissue from the shape of the spaces and the position of the nuclei.

2.Low-power architecture

A mosaic of polygonal to round clear spaces of fairly uniform size, separated by a delicate network of eosinophilic lines (basement membranes and fine collagen of the septa) and by thin connective tissue septa carrying vessels. At the edge of the fat lobule the adipocytes are smaller and merge into loose connective tissue.

3.High-power key features

Unilocular adipocyte: one huge lipid droplet fills the cell; the nucleus is flattened and pushed to the periphery like a signet ring; the cytoplasm is reduced to a thin eosinophilic rim with a few organelles; the cell is surrounded by an external lamina and reticular fibres. Between the cells run capillaries. Brown adipose tissue (textus adiposus fuscus) is the contrast to quote: multilocular adipocytes with many small droplets, a central rounded nucleus, abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm packed with mitochondria (hence brown), rich blood supply and sympathetic innervation; present in the fetus and neonate (interscapular, mediastinal, perirenal).

4.Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

HE: the lipid is not stained at all — it is dissolved by alcohol and xylene, leaving empty spaces; only the thin cytoplasm, basement membrane and nuclei are coloured, with nuclei basophilic and the rims eosinophilic. To demonstrate the fat you must say the magic sentence: a frozen section stained with Sudan III/IV or Oil Red O shows red droplets. Osmium tetroxide blackens the lipid, and a trichrome shows the collagen of the septa.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Empty, round, closely packed vacuoles with a "chicken-wire" pattern.
  • Nuclei flattened at the very periphery of the spaces.
  • No fibres crossing the spaces; delicate capillaries between adipocytes.
  • To prove lipid, ask for a frozen section + Sudan or Oil Red O.
Differential diagnosis
  • Loose connective tissue: abundant fibres and many cell types between the spaces; fat cells are only a minority.
  • Mucous connective tissue (Wharton's jelly): sparse cells, abundant gelatinous matrix, not a uniform vacuole sheet.
  • Processing artefacts: irregular cracks or clefting rather than uniform round spaces with marginal nuclei.
  • Brown adipose tissue: multilocular cells with central nuclei.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Adipose tissue is an endocrine organ (leptin) and contributes to the pro-inflammatory state of obesity.
  • Benign tumours are lipomas (mature unilocular adipocytes); malignant liposarcomas are rare and show lipoblasts.
  • After long-bone fracture, fat droplets may enter torn venules and cause fat embolism; sudden death in a patient with recent trauma should raise this possibility.

V.10Adipose tissue (frozen section, Sudan

Core answerA frozen section of adipose tissue stained with a fat-soluble dye (Sudan III/IV or Oil Red O): because the tissue has never touched alcohol or xylene, the lipid remains in place and stains bright red, proving that the clear spaces of the HE slide are indeed fat and the cells are adipocytes.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Tissue level; the crucial preparation point is the frozen section (sectio congelata). Routine paraffin processing dissolves fat in the clearing agents, so lipid histochemistry needs material snap-frozen and cut on a cryostat, with water-based reagents only.

2.Low-power architecture

The same "chicken-wire" sheet of adipocytes as in HE, but every cell now contains a brightly red globule. The background of connective tissue septa, capillaries and nuclei is counterstained, so the tissue architecture remains readable. Fat lobules, septa and vessels match the HE slide one-to-one.

3.High-power key features

Unilocular adipocyte: a single large spherical red droplet occupying almost the whole cell, with a thin rim of counterstained cytoplasm and a flattened nucleus at the margin; droplet size is fairly uniform. In brown adipose tissue the dye reveals many small red droplets (multilocular pattern) with a central nucleus. Lipid in other cells (steatotic hepatocytes, foam cells, sebaceous gland cells, the lipid of the adrenal cortex) stains the same way, so always confirm which organ you are in.

The chemistry to quote: the dye is more soluble in the lipid droplet than in its own solvent, so it partitions into the fat. Therefore the section must not contact lipid solvents (alcohol, xylene, chloroform) at any step. Nuclei are counterstained blue (haematoxylin) or red (carmine), and this gives the red-on-blue picture. How to identify it at the exam
  • Bright red globules filling cells that look empty on HE.
  • Frozen section, water-based staining — no alcohol or xylene anywhere in the protocol.
  • Blue nuclei at the periphery of the red droplets, matching the adipocyte shape.
  • The next step in the answer: compare with the HE slide of the same tissue.
Differential diagnosis
  • HE adipose tissue: identical architecture but empty spaces; do not call the spaces "fat" on HE alone.
  • Sebaceous gland: lipid also stains red but the cells form alveoli with holocrine secretion.
  • Steatotic liver or foam cells: red droplets inside hepatocytes or macrophages, not one droplet per cell in a fatty sheet.
  • Ice-crystal artefact: irregular clefts that also fail to stain uniformly.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Intraoperative frozen section is used for rapid diagnosis: fat necrosis, lipoma versus liposarcoma, and the detection of fat in lymph nodes.
  • Oil Red O on a renal biopsy demonstrates lipid droplets in tubular cells in nephrotic-range proteinuria.
  • The same cryostat technique preserves enzymes for histochemistry and is the reason frozen sections are kept for research and for lipid storage disorders.

V.11Hyaline cartilage (HE)

Core answerHyaline cartilage (cartilago hyalina) stained with HE: a smooth, glassy, basophilic matrix containing chondrocytes in lacunae, often in isogenous groups, with a perichondrium on the free surfaces (absent from articular surfaces). The matrix protein fibres are "masked", so no fibres can be seen — that is the meaning of hyaline.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Tissue level; the commonest examples are a tracheal ring, a rib cartilage or the articular surface of a long bone. Decide first whether a perichondrium is present: it is present over the trachea and ribs, but absent on articular surfaces, which are nourished by synovial fluid.

2.Low-power architecture

A homogeneous, pale to moderately basophilic matrix with round or oval clear spaces (lacunae) containing cells. Around each lacuna the matrix is darker (capsular matrix), around each isogenous group it is still fairly dark (territorial matrix), and between the groups it is palest (interterritorial matrix). A perichondrium, when present, is a distinct eosinophilic band on the surface with an outer fibrous and an inner chondrogenic layer. There are no vessels and no nerves inside the cartilage.

3.High-power key features

Chondrocytes: round to oval cells with a small, dark nucleus, a pale cytoplasm often containing lipid droplets and glycogen; they sit singly or in isogenous groups (two to four cells derived from one chondroblast division) inside lacunae. At the surface, flattened chondroblasts lie in the inner layer of the perichondrium. Matrix: type II collagen fibrils, aggrecan and hyaluronate give the glassy basophilia; fibrils are invisible because their refractive index matches the matrix. In older cartilage the matrix may calcify and become more deeply basophilic. Growth is by interstitial (division of chondrocytes) and appositional (from the perichondrium) mechanisms.

4.Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

HE: the matrix is basophilic because of the sulfated glycosaminoglycans, and this is the single most useful staining statement. PAS is positive in the matrix and outlines the capsule; Alcian blue stains acid GAGs blue-green; toluidine blue shows metachromasia (violet around the lacunae). The collagen is type II and needs immunohistochemistry to be demonstrated, precisely because it is masked. Calcified areas take haematoxylin more strongly.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Glassy, homogeneous basophilic matrix with no visible fibres.
  • Chondrocytes in lacunae, often in isogenous groups.
  • Perichondrium on a free surface; absent on articular surfaces.
  • No blood vessels inside the tissue.
Differential diagnosis
  • Elastic cartilage: looks similar on HE, but an orcein stain reveals a branching elastic network.
  • Fibrocartilage: coarse eosinophilic collagen bundles with chondrocytes in rows, no perichondrium.
  • Bone: eosinophilic matrix, osteons and canaliculi; lacunae are not round and empty in decalcified bone.
  • Tracheal gland adenoma or thyroid colloid: no lacunae and no chondrocytes.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Articular hyaline cartilage has no perichondrium and no vessels, so it heals poorly; microfissures progress to osteoarthritis.
  • Trauma or degeneration can detach cartilage fragments into the joint cavity (joint mouse), which are kept alive by synovial fluid.
  • Hyaline cartilage calcifies with age (rib and laryngeal cartilages) and is the source of chondrosarcoma; it is the template for endochondral ossification.

V.12Elastic cartilage (orcein + H)

Core answerElastic cartilage (cartilago elastica) stained with orcein and haematoxylin: the same chondrocytes in lacunae and the same perichondrium as hyaline cartilage, but the matrix contains a conspicuous, branching network of elastic fibres stained by orcein, which is the only way to tell it from hyaline cartilage. It is found in the auricle, the epiglottis and the auditory tube.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Tissue level from the auricle, epiglottis or the cartilaginous part of the auditory tube. Perichondrium is present on both surfaces. The orcein preparation is combined with a nuclear counterstain (haematoxylin), so both cells and elastic fibres can be assessed in the same field.

2.Low-power architecture

At low power the cartilage looks similar to hyaline cartilage, except that a dark, branching network runs through the matrix — brown to black with orcein. The perichondrium is visible at the border. In the epiglottis, one side is covered by non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium and the other by respiratory epithelium, so orientation may be needed.

3.High-power key features

Elastic fibres: fine, wavy, branching fibres which anastomose to form a three-dimensional network throughout the interterritorial matrix; they are absent from the immediate capsular region around the lacunae in many areas. Chondrocytes: as in hyaline cartilage — round to oval cells in lacunae, isogenous groups, small dark nuclei, lipid and glycogen in the cytoplasm. Matrix: type II collagen masked as in hyaline cartilage, but with elastin added; the tissue is therefore compression-resistant AND flexible. Perichondrium is present, with the outer fibrous and inner chondrogenic layers.

How to identify it at the exam
  • A brown to black branching network of fibres running through cartilage matrix.
  • Chondrocytes in lacunae and a perichondrium — it is still cartilage.
  • The orcein stain is the discriminator against hyaline cartilage.
  • Site: auricle, epiglottis or auditory tube.
Differential diagnosis
  • Hyaline cartilage: no elastic network on orcein; the commonest confusion if the stain is not considered.
  • Elastic connective tissue (aorta, ligamentum flavum): elastic fibres without lacunae or chondrocytes.
  • Fibrocartilage: coarse eosinophilic collagen bundles and rows of chondrocytes.
  • Auricle skin: contains elastic fibres in the dermis but also epidermis and appendages.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Elastic cartilage combines resilience with flexibility, which is why the auricle and epiglottis spring back into shape; it is the material used for auricular and nasal reconstruction.
  • Relapsing polychondritis is an autoimmune inflammation of cartilage that classically affects the auricle and can destroy its elastic framework.
  • Epiglottic oedema (epiglottitis) rapidly obstructs the airway because the loose connective tissue over elastic cartilage swells freely.

V.13Fibrocartilage (HE)

Core answerFibrocartilage (cartilago fibrosa) stained with HE: coarse, strongly eosinophilic bundles of type I collagen in parallel or crossing arrays, with oval chondrocytes lying in lacunae in rows between the bundles, very little basophilic matrix and no distinct perichondrium. It is the structural transition between dense connective tissue and hyaline cartilage.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Tissue level from the annulus fibrosus of an intervertebral disc (annulus fibrosus disci intervertebralis), the interpubic disc, a meniscus or a fibrocartilaginous articular surface. In the annulus the fibrocartilage lamellae alternate with thin layers of hyaline cartilage and the collagen bundles of successive lamellae cross obliquely.

2.Low-power architecture

The field is dominated by thick, wavy, deeply eosinophilic bundle systems that resemble a tendon but are interrupted by rows of round/oval cells. There is no perichondrium, no capsule and no epithelium; the tissue blends at its edges into dense regular connective tissue or into hyaline cartilage. Blood vessels are confined to the margins.

3.High-power key features

Collagen: bundles of type I collagen predominate and are strongly eosinophilic, running in parallel arrays with a felt-like interweaving; type II collagen is present but scanty. Cells: chondrocytes are oval and lie in lacunae, single or in small isogenous groups, characteristically arranged in axial rows between the bundles; true fibroblasts appear at the margins. Matrix: the proteoglycan versican predominates over aggrecan, so there is little basophilia and only weak Alcian-blue staining. Because collagen dominates the whole volume, a perichondrium cannot be distinguished.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Strongly eosinophilic collagen bundles plus chondrocytes in lacunae arranged in rows.
  • No perichondrium and very little basophilic matrix.
  • Hybrid look: half tendon, half cartilage.
  • Site: intervertebral disc, symphysis, meniscus, tendon insertion.
Differential diagnosis
  • Tendon: parallel collagen bundles but spindle-shaped fibrocyte nuclei with sharp poles, never lacunae or rows of round cells.
  • Hyaline cartilage: glassy basophilic matrix, perichondrium, isogenous groups.
  • Elastic cartilage: elastic network and perichondrium on orcein.
  • Bone: lamellar or trabecular eosinophilic matrix with osteocytes and canaliculi.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Fibrocartilage is built for combined compression and tension, which is why the annulus fibrosus and the menisci are made of it; tears in the annulus let the nucleus pulposus herniate.
  • The peripheral red zone of a meniscus has vessels and some healing capacity, while the inner white zone (pure fibrocartilage) does not — the anatomical basis of meniscal surgery.
  • The interpubic fibrocartilage softens under relaxin in pregnancy to widen the birth canal.

V.14Bone (ground section)

Core answerA ground section of compact lamellar bone (os compactum): an undecalcified slice of the diaphysis ground thin and mounted, showing osteons (osteona) as concentric lamellae around central Haversian canals, interstitial lamellae between them, Volkmann canals and empty osteocyte lacunae with their canaliculi. No cells survive the grinding, so the slide shows the architecture of the matrix only.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Tissue level, transverse section through the compact cortex of a long bone (the tibial diaphysis is the classic block). Hard tissue cannot be cut on a routine microtome, so it is either ground thin without decalcification — the present slide — or decalcified and sectioned as soft tissue for HE.

2.Low-power architecture

A solid, homogeneous matrix perforated by many small circular holes. Around each hole a set of concentric rings: this is an osteon. Between neighbouring osteons lie angular patches, the interstitial lamellae, the remnants of older generations of osteons. At the periosteal and endosteal surfaces, circumferential lamellae run parallel to the surface. Some canals run across the field, linking adjacent osteons — the Volkmann canals.

3.High-power key features

Central (Haversian) canal: contains a capillary, a venule, a nerve and loose connective tissue; the osteon is the cylindrical unit of bone built around it. Concentric lamellae: 3–7 µm thick layers of mineralised matrix in which collagen fibres change direction from lamella to lamella, giving the "plywood" strength. Osteocyte lacunae: small oval spaces between the lamellae, appearing empty or dark in a ground section, with fine canaliculi radiating towards the canal. Volkmann canals run transversely and connect neighbouring osteon canals. Cement lines mark the outer limit of each osteon.

4.Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

A ground section is not decalcified: in the simplest preparation it is mounted unstained and the lacunae and canaliculi appear dark against a translucent matrix, with the canals filled by debris. Many teaching sets stain the ground section with basic fuchsin, which colours the mineralised matrix red-pink and makes the canals and lamellae stand out. Von Kossa or alizarin red demonstrate calcium, and microradiography maps the mineral density. The decalcified HE slide is a different preparation: there the osteocytes are visible in their lacunae.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Concentric lamellae around central canals — osteons and nothing else.
  • Empty osteocyte lacunae and fine canaliculi, no cells with nuclei.
  • Interstitial and circumferential lamellae complete the architecture.
  • Say the technique: undecalcified ground section, calcified matrix retained.
Differential diagnosis
  • Decalcified bone (HE): osteocytes visible in lacunae, eosinophilic matrix, marrow spaces, cutting cones.
  • Calcified hyaline cartilage: chondrocytes in lacunae, no osteons or Haversian canals.
  • Fibrocartilage: coarse collagen bundles and rows of chondrocytes.
  • Tooth ground section: dentinal tubules and enamel, not osteons.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Osteons are constantly renewed: in decalcified bone the remodelling front appears as a cutting cone of osteoclasts followed by a closing cone of osteoblasts.
  • The orientation of lamellae and the density of osteons adapt to load; loss of trabeculae and thinning of the cortex is osteoporosis, while chaotic remodelling with mosaic lamellae is Paget disease.
  • Ground sections are the standard technique for hard tissues in histology and forensic anthropology, and they preserve the mineral phase that ordinary processing dissolves.

V.15Endochondral ossification (HE)

Core answerA longitudinal section through a developing long bone or fetal rib stained with HE: a cartilaginous model is replaced by bone in an ordered sequence, so from the epiphysis towards the diaphysis the epiphyseal growth cartilage shows resting, proliferating, hypertrophic, calcified and eroded zones before the ossification zone, while a periosteal bone collar forms around the shaft and trabeculae are lined by osteoblasts and resorbed by osteoclasts.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Organ level, longitudinal section along the axis of a fetal long bone or a fetal rib; the epiphyseal growth cartilage (cartilago epiphysialis) is the line to follow, and the direction of growth is always from the epiphysis towards the diaphysis. The perichondrium becomes periosteum at the level of the bone collar, and if a rib is used, intercostal muscle and nerve appear beside it.

2.Low-power architecture

At one end there is a cap of hyaline cartilage. Towards the shaft the cells line up in columns, the matrix darkens, and then the cartilage is replaced by a network of eosinophilic trabeculae with marrow spaces between them. On the outside of the shaft, deep to the periosteum, a rim of bone — the periosteal (subperiosteal) collar — has formed by intramembranous ossification. This collar cuts off cartilage nutrition and is the first step of endochondral ossification.

3.High-power key features

The six zones, in order: resting (reserve) cartilage with scattered chondrocytes; proliferating zone with chondroblasts dividing into columns (isogenous lines); hypertrophic zone where chondrocytes enlarge several-fold, become pale and glycogen-rich, and the matrix between them narrows; calcified zone where the matrix becomes deeply basophilic; erosion zone where vessels, chondroclasts and osteoprogenitor cells invade the empty lacunae; and ossification zone where osteoblasts deposit osteoid on the remnants of calcified cartilage, forming primary trabeculae. Osteoclasts sit in Howship lacunae on the trabecular surfaces, and between the trabeculae the marrow space is established. Trabeculae therefore have a blue calcified cartilage core with a pink bone surface — a point examiners love.

  • Osteoblasts lining trabeculae and osteoclasts in Howship lacunae.
Differential diagnosis
  • Intramembranous ossification: bone trabeculae directly in condensed mesenchyme, no cartilage zones (skull vault, maxilla).
  • Adult long bone: only osteons and marrow, no growth cartilage.
  • Pure hyaline cartilage: no vessels, no trabeculae, no marrow spaces.
  • Fracture callus: disorganised mixture of cartilage and woven bone at a fracture site, without the ordered zones.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Rickets and osteomalacia are failures of mineralisation: the growth plate widens and osteoid accumulates unmineralised, so the bone bends.
  • Achondroplasia impairs endochondral ossification (fibroblast growth factor receptor 3), giving short limbs with a normal skull.
  • The hypertrophic-calcified zone is mechanically the weakest, which is why Salter–Harris type I fractures separate the epiphysis through it; closure of the plate ends growth in length.

V.16Smooth muscle (HE)

Core answerSmooth muscle (textus muscularis nonstriatus) stained with HE: bundles or sheets of spindle-shaped leiomyocytes (leiomyocyti) with medium-eosinophilic cytoplasm and a single central, elongated cigar-shaped nucleus; there is no cross-striation, and in cross-section nuclei are seen only in the widest cell profiles.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Tissue level, taken from the wall of a hollow organ (intestine, ureter, uterus) or a blood vessel. Because smooth muscle bundles run in different directions, most slides show both longitudinal and transverse profiles in one field: look for the plane where the cells are long, and for the plane where the cells are round.

2.Low-power architecture

Eosinophilic bundles with no visible striation and no regular parallel arrangement across the whole field. In the gut wall the bundles form layers; in the uterus they interlace. Connective tissue (endomysium) separates the bundles and carries small vessels, but it is far less prominent than the perimysium of skeletal muscle.

3.High-power key features

Longitudinal profile: elongated spindle cells, roughly 5–10 µm wide and 20–50 µm long, whose tapered ends interdigitate with the broad parts of neighbouring cells; the nucleus is central, oval and blunt-ended ("cigar-shaped"), with a pale perinuclear endoplasm at its poles containing mitochondria and Golgi. Cross-section: irregular round or oval profiles of widely varying diameter, closely apposed; a nucleus is visible only in the largest profiles, and most profiles show nucleus-free homogeneous eosinophilic sarcoplasm. Myofilaments do not form myofibrils; they insert into dense bodies, and gap junctions spread the contraction from cell to cell. There are no T-tubules and no striation.

HE: cytoplasm has medium eosinophilia — less intense than skeletal muscle — with basophilic nuclei. How to identify it at the exam
  • Spindle cells with central cigar-shaped nuclei and no cross-striation.
  • Cross-section: variable-diameter round profiles, nuclei only in the widest.
  • Bundles interlacing rather than running in one strict direction.
  • Describe both planes of section — longitudinal and transverse — in the same answer.
Differential diagnosis
  • Skeletal muscle (longitudinal): cross-striation and peripheral nuclei.
  • Cardiac muscle: central nuclei but branching cells and intercalated discs.
  • Tendon: parallel collagen bundles with sharp-ended nuclei and no cytoplasm.
  • Myofibroblasts or dense connective tissue: collagen dominates and the nuclei are more slender and wavy.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • The smooth muscle of vessel media and of hollow organs maintains resting tone, and its contraction is involuntary, slow, weak and tireless.
  • Leiomyoma of the uterus is the commonest benign smooth-muscle tumour; leiomyosarcoma is malignant.
  • Bronchial smooth-muscle contraction narrows the airway in asthma, and uterine leiomyocytes hypertrophy enormously in pregnancy — the same cell type in two very different clinical settings.

V.17Skeletal muscle (cross section, HE)

Core answerA transverse section of skeletal muscle stained with HE: fascicles of polygonal muscle fibres (myofibrae striatae) of varying diameter, each with eosinophilic sarcoplasm and several flattened nuclei displaced to the periphery under the sarcolemma, with endomysium between the fibres and perimysium between the fascicles.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Tissue level, cut across the long axis of the muscle fibres. This is the plane that shows fibre size, nuclei position and connective tissue sheaths best; the same block cut longitudinally gives the companion slide V.18.

2.Low-power architecture

Skeletal muscle is organised hierarchically: epimysium on the surface of the muscle, perimysium separating fascicles and carrying vessels and nerves, and endomysium — loose connective tissue with capillaries — between individual fibres. In cross-section the fascicles look like islands of closely packed polygonal profiles divided by pink septa.

3.High-power key features

Muscle fibres: round to polygonal profiles; diameters vary between fibres (10–100 µm range) because each fibre is an independent syncytium. Sarcoplasm: homogeneously eosinophilic with a fine stippling (myofibrils seen end-on); no cross-striation in this plane. Nuclei: several per fibre, flattened and pushed to the very margin beneath the sarcolemma — the single most important observation, because it excludes cardiac and smooth muscle. Capillaries with erythrocytes lie in the endomysium. An occasional muscle spindle may appear as a small encapsulated group of intrafusal fibres.

HE: sarcoplasm is strongly eosinophilic and nuclei are basophilic; the endomysial collagen is also eosinophilic but paler and less homogeneous. For fibre typing, ATPase or NADH histochemistry separates type I, IIA and IIB fibres, and PAS shows the glycogen of fast fibres.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Polygonal profiles with nuclei at the periphery, never in the centre.
  • Several nuclei per fibre — it is a syncytium.
  • Fascicles divided by perimysium; endomysium between fibres.
  • No striation visible in this plane, so nuclei position is the discriminator.
Differential diagnosis
  • Cardiac muscle (cross-section): central nuclei with perinuclear pale endoplasm, branching, one capillary per cell.
  • Smooth muscle (cross-section): variable-diameter round profiles, no fascicular organisation, nuclei central in the widest profiles.
  • Peripheral nerve (cross-section): rings of myelin around axons, wavy fascicles, no homogeneous sarcoplasm.
  • Dense irregular connective tissue: no polygonal profiles.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Muscle biopsy is read in cross-section to assess fibre types, size variation and capillary density.
  • Denervation produces group atrophy of fibres, and Duchenne dystrophy shows wide variation in fibre size, central nuclei and replacement by fat and fibrous tissue.
  • Each fibre belongs to a single motor unit (one alpha motor neuron), which is why loss of a motor neuron atrophies a scattered group of fibres.

V.18Skeletal muscle (longit. section, HE)

Core answerA longitudinal section of skeletal muscle stained with HE: long, parallel, unbranched muscle fibres with several peripheral nuclei and a distinct cross-striation produced by the sarcomeres, separated by delicate endomysium. Cross-striation plus peripheral nuclei is the definitive identification.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Tissue level, cut parallel to the axis of the fibres. Several fibres run across the field as long eosinophilic ribbons; if the section is slightly oblique, the striation is still visible but the nuclei may appear within rather than at the border — always look at the best-oriented fibres.

2.Low-power architecture

Parallel bundles of fibres with perimysium between the fascicles and endomysium between individual fibres. Near a myotendinous junction the fibres end in tapering tips and the tendon collagen begins; the Golgi tendon organ and the muscle spindle may be found near this region.

3.High-power key features

Cross-striation: alternating dark A bands (myosin thick filaments, anisotropic) and light I bands (actin thin filaments, isotropic), with a thin Z line in the middle of each I band; the repeating unit — the sarcomere (sarcomerum) — is on average about 2.2–2.8 µm long. The A band contains a lighter H zone and a dark M line in its centre. Nuclei: multiple, oval, flattened, immediately under the sarcolemma at the fibre periphery. Sarcoplasm: filled with myofibrils, with rows of mitochondria between them and glycogen and myoglobin. Fibres are never branched and never joined by intercalated discs.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Cross-striation: alternating A and I bands along the fibre.
  • Several nuclei, all peripheral, under the sarcolemma.
  • Fibres long, parallel and unbranched.
  • Iron haematoxylin is the stain that makes striation and nuclei unmistakable.
Differential diagnosis
  • Cardiac muscle: branched cells, central nucleus with perinuclear halo, intercalated discs.
  • Smooth muscle: spindle cells with central cigar nuclei and no striation.
  • Tendon: no cells inside the bundles and no striation.
  • Peripheral nerve: wavy fibres and myelin, not homogeneous sarcoplasm with striation.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • The striation is the morphological expression of the sliding filament mechanism: actin slides over myosin, the I band and H zone shorten and the A band stays constant.
  • Failure of calcium reuptake after death leaves actin and myosin locked — rigor mortis; hypocalcaemia increases excitability and causes tetany.
  • In myasthenia gravis antibodies block the postsynaptic acetylcholine receptor, so the striated fibre is present but cannot be activated; in rhabdomyolysis, myoglobin released from these fibres can cause acute kidney injury.

V.19Cardiac muscle (HE)

Core answerCardiac muscle (myocardium) stained with HE: branching cardiomyocytes (cardiomyocyti) joined end to end by intercalated discs, each with a single central nucleus surrounded by a pale perinuclear endoplasm, cross-striation in the sarcoplasm, and a very rich capillary network in the endomysium.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Tissue level; in the ventricular wall the cardiomyocytes run in a spiral, so one section usually contains fibres cut longitudinally, obliquely and transversely. The longitudinal fibres are needed to see striation and intercalated discs; the transverse ones show the central nucleus best.

2.Low-power architecture

Eosinophilic fibre bundles separated by delicate connective tissue with many capillaries; no thick perimysium comparable with skeletal muscle. In the subendocardium, Purkinje fibres of the conducting system appear as large pale glycogen-rich cells, and epicardial fat and vessels may border the section.

3.High-power key features

Cardiomyocyte: a cylinder about 85–100 µm long and 15 µm wide with one or two terminal cytoplasmic processes, so cells form a Y- or X-shaped network. Nucleus: single (exceptionally two), central, oval, dark, surrounded by a clear perinuclear zone (endoplasm) where organelles, glycogen, lipid droplets and the age pigment lipofuscin accumulate. Sarcoplasm: strongly eosinophilic, filled with myofibrils, showing cross-striation. Intercalated discs (disci intercalares): transverse, straight or step-like dark lines where two cells meet; they contain fascia adherens and desmosomes for mechanical coupling and gap junctions for electrical coupling. Endomysium: loose connective tissue with an extremely dense capillary network — the basis for the heart's dependence on aerobic metabolism.

In a phosphotungstic acid–haematoxylin or semi-thin section, the disc appears as a dark transverse band of stair-like shape.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Branching fibres with a central nucleus and a clear perinuclear halo.
  • Intercalated discs — transverse dark lines between cells.
  • Cross-striation present, so it is striated muscle.
  • Very rich capillary network in the endomysium.
Differential diagnosis
  • Skeletal muscle: unbranched fibres, many peripheral nuclei, no intercalated discs.
  • Smooth muscle: no striation, central cigar-shaped nuclei, spindle cells.
  • Purkinje fibres of the conducting system: much larger, pale, glycogen-rich cells with fewer myofibrils.
  • Artefactual contraction bands: dark transverse bands after death or injury, not true intercalated discs.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Cardiomyocytes are post-mitotic: after myocardial infarction the necrotic focus is replaced by a collagen scar, which cannot contract, so ejection fraction falls.
  • Lipofuscin accumulates with age ("wear-and-tear pigment"); hypertrophy enlarges the nuclei and the fibre diameter.
  • Atrial cardiomyocytes secrete atrial natriuretic peptide, and cardiac troponin released from damaged cells is the standard blood test for myocardial necrosis.

V.20Cardiac muscle (iron hematoxylin)

Core answerA myocardium preparation stained with Heidenhain's iron haematoxylin: the dye blackens the nuclei, the rows of mitochondria and the cross-striation, producing a high-contrast image in which the sarcomeres and the step-like intercalated discs stand out clearly, so cardiac muscle can be proved striated and distinguished from smooth muscle.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Tissue level, high-power cytology; longitudinal and transverse fibres are both present in the same section. The purpose of the slide is contrast, not architecture: the examiner shows it when the question is "what kind of muscle is this and how do you prove it".

2.Low-power architecture

Bundles of fibres with black stippling and black nuclei against a paler background; connective tissue septa and capillaries are much less conspicuous than the muscle itself. The overall arrangement is the same as in the HE slide of myocardium, so orientation is easy.

3.High-power key features

Nuclei are black and central, often with the paler perinuclear endoplasm visible as a halo. Between the myofibrils, rows of mitochondria appear as black dots and dashes, giving the sarcoplasm a stippled, striated look. The cross-striation (A and I bands) is sharper than on HE. Intercalated discs are transverse dark lines, straight or step-like, at the junction of neighbouring cells. Branching of the cells and the rich capillary network remain visible, and the central position of the nuclei excludes skeletal muscle.

Heidenhain's ferric (iron) haematoxylin is a mordant dye complex that stains nuclear structures, the striation of skeletal and cardiac muscle, and mitochondria black; it is used with careful differentiation, so only the structures of interest retain the dye.   • Black, sharply outlined central nuclei with perinuclear halos.
  • Black stippling between myofibrils = mitochondria.
  • Cross-striation and step-like intercalated discs are conspicuous.
  • Branching cells with a dense capillary network — cardiac, not skeletal.
Differential diagnosis
  • Skeletal muscle with iron haematoxylin: peripheral nuclei, long unbranched fibres, no discs.
  • Smooth muscle with iron haematoxylin: longitudinal striations of myofilament bundles and dense bodies, but no cross-striation and central cigar nuclei.
  • HE myocardium: lower contrast; the striation may be missed if the section is thick.
  • Necrotic myocardium: hypereosinophilic fibres with loss of striation and nuclear lysis — a pathological change, not the normal slide.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Early after infarction, cardiomyocytes show contraction bands — intensely eosinophilic transverse bands produced by hypercontraction — that must not be confused with intercalated discs.
  • Because the myocardium is so dependent on oxidative metabolism, mitochondrial stains are used to demonstrate mitochondrial disease, and the density of mitochondria explains the vulnerability to ischaemia.
  • The intercalated disc is the site where gap junctions allow the contraction wave to spread, so its integrity underlies coordinated systolic function.

V.21Sensory ganglion (HE)

Core answerA sensory ganglion (ganglion sensorium), typically a spinal ganglion, stained with HE: clusters of large, round pseudounipolar neurons (neuronum pseudounipolare) with pale vesicular nuclei and prominent nucleoli, each surrounded by a ring of satellite glial cells, with bundles of nerve fibres running between the nerve cell bodies and a connective tissue capsule around the organ.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Organ level: the section cuts through the ganglion along the course of the posterior root, so the fibrous capsule, the clusters of neurons and the entering/leaving nerve fibres are all visible. The neurons are more concentrated towards the periphery of the ganglion, with the fibre bundles running through the centre.

2.Low-power architecture

A nodular organ bounded by a dense connective tissue capsule that continues into the epineurium of the nerve and, towards the central nervous system, into the dura mater. Inside, small round neuron bodies form groups separated by wavy nerve fibres and delicate connective tissue. There is no cortex, no medulla and no ependymal canal — the architecture is a mixture of neurons and fibres, not layers.

3.High-power key features

Pseudounipolar neuron: large, spherical perikaryon with a single process that bifurcates into a peripheral and a central branch (not visible in the section). The nucleus is large, pale and vesicular with a very prominent, dark nucleolus — the "owl-eye" appearance. The cytoplasm contains clumped basophilic Nissl substance, mostly at the periphery, and sometimes lipofuscin. Satellite glial cells form a complete single-layered capsule around each neuron and are separated from it by a basal lamina. Between the neurons, nerve fibres run in bundles with Schwann cells, and no synapses are present inside a sensory ganglion.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Large round neurons with pale nuclei and prominent nucleoli, grouped in clusters.
  • A complete ring of small satellite-cell nuclei around each neuron body.
  • Nerve fibre bundles between the neurons; a connective tissue capsule around the organ.
  • No synapses and no neuropil-rich grey matter with dendrites — this is not CNS.
Differential diagnosis
  • Autonomic (sympathetic or parasympathetic) ganglion: multipolar neurons of irregular shape, several dendrites, satellite cells not forming a complete capsule, and synapses present.
  • Spinal cord grey matter: anterior horn motor neurons are multipolar and embedded in neuropil with a central canal nearby.
  • Peripheral nerve: only fibres and sheaths, no neuron bodies.
  • Adrenal medulla: chromaffin cells in nests, no pseudounipolar neurons.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Varicella-zoster virus becomes latent in sensory ganglia and reactivates as shingles in the corresponding dermatome.
  • Compression or inflammation of a dorsal root ganglion produces radicular pain and sensory loss; the ganglion has no blood–brain barrier, which is why some viruses and drugs reach it easily.
  • Chromatolysis — dispersion of Nissl substance with a pale swollen perikaryon — is the histological response to axon injury, and it is demonstrated with Nissl staining.

V.22Spinal cord (HE)

Core answerA transverse section of the spinal cord (medulla spinalis) stained with HE: a central, H-shaped mass of grey matter (substantia grisea) containing multipolar motor neurons in the anterior horns, surrounded by a peripheral ring of white matter funiculi, with the central canal lined by ependyma in the midline.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Organ level, transverse (horizontal) section through a spinal segment. The shape of the grey matter tells you the approximate level: the anterior horns are largest where the plexuses of the limbs arise (cervical and lumbar enlargements), and the amount of white matter increases cranially. The anterior median fissure and the posterior median sulcus orient the section.

2.Low-power architecture

A butterfly- or H-shaped central region of grey matter surrounding the central canal, and a broad peripheral region of white matter divided into anterior, lateral and posterior funiculi. The anterior horns are broad and rounded, the posterior horns slender and reaching the surface. Pia mater and vessels are seen along the surface, and the anterior median fissure contains loose connective tissue and vessels.

3.High-power key features

Anterior horn: large multipolar alpha and gamma motor neurons — the largest cells in the cord (roughly 80–100 µm) — with abundant Nissl substance, a pale nucleus and a prominent nucleolus; their axons leave through the anterior root. Lateral horn: visceromotor neurons at the segments C8–L3 and S2–S4. Posterior horn: smaller neurons and interneurons with many incoming sensory fibres. Central canal: lined by a single layer of cuboidal-to-columnar ependyma and filled with cerebrospinal fluid. White matter: bundles of myelinated axons with oligodendrocytes; in HE both grey and white matter look pink, differing only in intensity and cellularity.

HE: both grey and white matter appear pink, the grey matter is more cellular and the white matter more homogeneous; this is the point examiners like, because "grey and white" are macroscopic terms, not staining colours. How to identify it at the exam
  • H-shaped or butterfly-shaped central grey matter with a central canal.
  • Large multipolar motor neurons concentrated in the broad anterior horns.
  • Peripheral white matter funiculi; anterior median fissure.
  • No folia, no six-layered cortex — those belong to cerebellum and cerebrum.
Differential diagnosis
  • Medulla oblongata: no regular H-shaped grey matter; instead nuclei such as the inferior olive and decussating tracts.
  • Cerebellum: leaf-like folia with a three-layered cortex.
  • Cerebral cortex: a six-layered ribbon on the surface with white matter beneath, no central canal.
  • Sensory ganglion: clusters of round pseudounipolar neurons, not an H-shaped grey matter.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Loss of anterior horn cells causes lower motor neuron signs — weakness, atrophy and fasciculation — as in poliomyelitis and in the lower motor neuron component of ALS.
  • Expansion of the central canal (syringomyelia) first damages the crossing spinothalamic fibres, producing a cape-like loss of pain and temperature sensation.
  • Lumbar puncture is performed below the termination of the cord, and the level of the lesion can be inferred from the segmental pattern of sensory and motor loss.

V.23Cerebral cortex (Cresyl-violet)

Core answerA Cresyl-violet ("Nissl") preparation of the cerebral cortex (cortex cerebri): the basic dye stains neuronal nuclei, nucleoli and the Nissl substance purple-blue, so the six laminae of the neocortex and the pyramidal cells — including the giant Betz cells of the motor cortex — are clearly displayed, while the white matter beneath stays pale.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Organ level, a strip of cortex cut perpendicular to the pial surface, usually including a gyrus with its overlying pia and the underlying white matter. The grey matter of the cerebral cortex is only 2–5 mm thick, so orientation from pia to white matter is essential and easy: pia at the surface, then the layers, then the paler white matter.

2.Low-power architecture

A band of grey matter with six ill-defined horizontal layers, limited externally by the pia and internally by the white matter. In Cresyl-violet preparations the neuronal perikarya appear as purple dots, most densely packed in the granular layers; the molecular layer at the surface is pale, and the white matter is almost unstained because myelin does not take up the dye.

3.High-power key features

The six laminae, from the surface: I molecular (few cells, many tangential fibres); II external granular (small neurons); III external pyramidal (pyramidal cells, larger towards the depth); IV internal granular (densely packed small granular cells; thickest in sensory areas); V internal pyramidal (large pyramidal cells, including the Betz cells up to about 100 µm in the motor cortex, the origin of the corticospinal tract); VI multiform (mixed fusiform and pyramidal cells merging into the white matter). Pyramidal cells are triangular with an apical dendrite pointing to the surface and basal dendrites; granular cells are small and round. Nissl substance in the perikarya is the purple cytoplasmic material, and the nucleolus is a prominent dark dot inside the pale nucleus.

4.Staining behaviour — how to say it aloud

Cresyl violet is a basic (cationic) dye that binds RNA and DNA, so it stains the nucleus, the nucleolus and the rough endoplasmic reticulum (the Nissl substance) purple-blue; it is one of the standard Nissl stains alongside toluidine blue and thionine. The technique is the logical way to map neuronal cell bodies and their layers, and the pale white matter is the internal control. Chromatolysis after axon injury, with dispersion of Nissl substance, is demonstrated with the same stain.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Six layers of purple neuronal perikarya arranged in horizontal laminae.
  • Triangular pyramidal cells with apical dendrites pointing to the pia.
  • Pale white matter beneath the cortex; pia on the surface.
  • Cresyl violet = nuclei, nucleoli and Nissl substance purple; myelin pale.
Differential diagnosis
  • Cerebellar cortex: three layers, with a single row of huge pear-shaped Purkinje cells and an extremely dense granular layer.
  • Spinal cord: H-shaped grey matter and a central canal, no cortical layers.
  • Hippocampus (allocortex): three or four layers, no six-layered neocortex.
  • Sensory ganglion: clustered large pseudounipolar neurons with satellite capsules.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Cytoarchitecture is the basis of the Brodmann map: the thickness and cellularity of the laminae differ between motor, sensory and association areas, and the motor cortex is identified by its giant Betz cells in layer V.
  • Hypoxic-ischaemic injury produces cortical laminar necrosis in the metabolically most active layers, and Betz cell loss explains the corticospinal signs of motor neuron disease.
  • The cortex depends almost entirely on oxidative metabolism, so blood flow interruption causes irreversible neuronal death within minutes.

V.24Cerebellum (HE)

Core answer Cerebellar cortex (cortex cerebellaris) in HE: a three-layered cortex on leaf-like folia — an outer pale molecular layer, a middle Purkinje cell layer of large pear-shaped neurons in a single row, and an inner, intensely basophilic granular layer packed with tiny granule cells — over a core of branching white matter.

1.Level of organisation and plane of section

Organ level. In a transverse section the folia look like leaves or fingers, each with a white-matter core and a three-layered cortex on both sides; in a sagittal section the branching white matter forms the arbor vitae. The pia follows the surface into the fissures between the folia.

2.Low-power architecture

Alternating leaf-like profiles: pale molecular layer outside, a thin darker line (Purkinje cells) at the interface, an intensely dark granular layer inside, then pale white matter. Because the granular layer is so basophilic, the cortex stands out as a dark ribbon outlining every folium — the easiest low-power recognition feature in the whole nervous system set.

3.High-power key features

Molecular layer: pale, cell-sparse, with the richly branched flattened dendritic trees of Purkinje cells, parallel fibres, and scattered stellate and basket cells near the deeper part. Purkinje cell layer: one row of very large, pear- or bottle-shaped cells — about 50–70 µm by 35 µm — with a pale nucleus and a very prominent nucleolus; their axons are the only output of the cerebellar cortex and project to the cerebellar nuclei. Granular layer: enormously densely packed small granule cells with dark nuclei (the darkest layer in the brain), plus Golgi cells and cerebellar glomeruli. White matter: myelinated axons with oligodendrocytes. Bergmann glia (specialised astrocytes) radiate through the Purkinje layer.

HE: the granular layer is strongly basophilic because of the dense nuclei; the molecular layer is pale and eosinophilic; the white matter is pale. How to identify it at the exam
  • Three cortical layers: pale molecular, one row of large Purkinje cells, dark granular layer.
  • Leaf-like folia with a white-matter core; arbor vitae on sagittal sections.
  • Purkinje cells are the largest and palest neurons in a single line at the layer interface.
  • No six-layered cortex, no central canal, no H-shaped grey matter.
Differential diagnosis
  • Cerebral cortex (neocortex): six layers, pyramidal cells, no dense granular layer of this type.
  • Hippocampus: tightly packed dentate gyrus and a single curved pyramidal layer, not folia.
  • Spinal cord: H-shaped grey matter and a central canal.
  • Cerebellar nuclei: islands of grey matter inside the white matter without cortical lamination.
Clinical / functional correlation
  • Purkinje cells are the most vulnerable neurons of the cerebellum: alcohol, phenytoin and paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration all cause their loss and produce ataxia and dysmetria.
  • Medulloblastoma arises from the granule-cell lineage of the external granular layer, which is why it is a tumour of childhood.
  • The cerebellum coordinates the timing and accuracy of movement and maintains equilibrium — the functional counterpart of its three-layered cortex and arbor vitae.

V.25Vegetative ganglion (Ag)

Core answerThe slide is a whole autonomic (vegetative) ganglion: a peripheral collection of multipolar postganglionic neuron bodies with their satellite glial cells, embedded in bundles of preganglionic and postganglionic nerve fibres and enclosed in a connective tissue capsule. Prepared by silver impregnation (Ag), it is recognised by multipolar neurons, each with a pale nucleus and one prominent dark nucleolus, scattered among nerve fibres with an argyrophilic network and relatively incomplete satellite-cell sheaths.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Organ level: a whole small ganglion — a paravertebral ganglion of the sympathetic trunk, a prevertebral ganglion beside the aorta (coeliac, mesenteric), or an intramural ganglion of the enteric nervous system in the wall of the gut. The section may be cut in any plane. The capsule is continuous with the epineurium of the nerve roots that enter and leave the nodule, so a short piece of nerve trunk is usually attached to one pole. The slide code Ag stands for ganglion autonomicum.

2.Low-power architecture

From outside in there is a capsule of dense irregular collagenous connective tissue, then thin septa of loose connective tissue carrying blood vessels, then the neuronal parenchyma. The neuron bodies are pale, rounded islands of unequal size; between them run broad wavy fascicles of nerve fibres. Compared with a sensory ganglion the stroma is abundant and the neurons look scattered, not nested.

3.High-power key features

  • Neuron body: large, oval to stellate; the nucleus is eccentric (off-centre), pale and vesicular, with one prominent dark nucleolus — the classic "owl's eye".
  • Cytoplasm: pale and finely granular from Nissl (tigroid) substance, which is denser towards the periphery; yellow-brown lipofuscin granules may be stacked at one pole, more with age.
  • Processes: several short dendrites leave the body and radiate into the surrounding tissue; the single axon is thin and cannot be followed. Multipolarity itself is the criterion that excludes a pseudounipolar sensory neuron.
  • Satellite (amphicyte) cells: a thin glial sheath surrounds the perikaryon, but it is less complete than in a sensory ganglion, which is why the autonomic neuron looks relatively bare.
  • Two populations of fibres: preganglionic fibres are myelinated and therefore appear as pale, optically empty rings around a coloured axonal dot; postganglionic fibres are unmyelinated and are seen only as clusters of fine dots in the Schwann cell cytoplasm.

4.Reading the colours

In a silver preparation the neurons, their processes and the neurofibrils are black to dark brown against a pale background: the nucleolus and the argyrophilic network are the darkest points, the neuropil and connective tissue stay pale, and myelin is only weakly argyrophilic. A haematoxylin counterstain, where used, colours nuclei blue. There is no epithelium, no keratin, no cartilage and no muscle except the thin media of intraganglionic vessels — so every large pale cell with a big nucleolus on this slide is a ganglion cell, and every capsule is connective tissue, never a perineurium-delimited fascicle.

How to identify it at the exam
  • A cluster of multipolar neuron bodies with an eccentric nucleus and a single dark nucleolus, lying in abundant nerve-fibre bundles and loose connective tissue.
  • The neurons are not in tight nests; there is plenty of stroma between them — the opposite of a spinal ganglion.
  • A distinct capsule plus attached nerve bundles proves it is a ganglion and not a piece of central grey matter.
  • No lymphoid tissue, no epithelium, no cartilage: the differential list is short.
Differential diagnosis
  • Sensory (spinal) ganglion: neurons are pseudounipolar and round, with a central nucleus and abundant Nissl substance; bodies sit in tight nests separated by fibre bundles, and satellite cells form a complete ring. Autonomic neurons are multipolar, eccentric, scattered.
  • Spinal cord grey matter: multipolar neurons are present, but there is no capsule, no attached nerve fascicles and no perineuronal satellite capsule; neuropil and white matter are visible instead.
  • Peripheral nerve in cross section: contains no neuron bodies at all, only fascicles bounded by perineurium.
  • Adrenal medulla or paraganglion: cords of secretory cells of neural crest origin, without large multipolar neurons; needs special stains to prove.
Clinical and functional note
  • The ganglion is the synaptic relay of the autonomic pathway: the preganglionic (myelinated) axon releases acetylcholine on the postganglionic (mostly unmyelinated) neuron, which then releases noradrenaline (sympathetic) or acetylcholine (parasympathetic).
  • Because transmission here is nicotinic, ganglion-blocking drugs interrupt the whole visceromotor pathway distal to the ganglion.
  • Hirschsprung disease is congenital absence of intramural ganglion cells in a distal segment of colon: the aganglionic segment cannot relax, so the colon above it dilates into a megacolon.

V.26Peripheral nerve (longit. section, HE)

Core answerThe slide is a peripheral nerve cut longitudinally and stained with HE: parallel, slightly wavy fascicles of nerve fibres separated by connective tissue sheaths — epineurium around the whole nerve, perineurium around each fascicle and endoneurium between the individual fibres. In HE the axoplasm is eosinophilic while the lipid myelin is dissolved during preparation, so the nerve reads as bundles of pink wavy ribbons separated by pale clefts and dotted with elongated blue nuclei.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Organ level. A peripheral nerve is a cord of fascicles, each a bundle of nerve fibres (axons with their Schwann sheaths), wrapped in three named connective tissue sheaths. A longitudinal section displays the fibres along their length, so internodes, nodes and the wavy course of the bundles are seen in profile; a peripheral nerve is usually mixed (somatic motor, somatic and visceral sensory, and autonomic fibres), and axons cannot be distinguished from dendrites in a routine section.

2.Low-power architecture

Outside is the epineurium, a dense irregular collagenous envelope that sends septa between the fascicles; between the fascicles lie loose connective tissue, adipose tissue and the vasa nervorum. Each fascicle is bounded by a thin, sharply defined perineurium. The fascicles differ in calibre, interweave and branch, which gives the whole nerve its wavy, rope-like appearance. The nerve is often accompanied by an artery and a vein in a neurovascular bundle.

3.High-power key features

  • Nerve fibres are closely packed, wavy eosinophilic bands. Because the myelin lipids are extracted, the myelin appears as pale, often optically empty clefts between darker axonal ribbons — do not mistake these for oedema.
  • Nodes of Ranvier are transverse constrictions interrupting the myelin at intervals along the fibre; Schmidt–Lantermann incisures are oblique pale notches within a single internode.
  • Nuclei: elongated Schwann cell nuclei lie on the surface of the fibres and are aligned with them; perineurial fibroblast nuclei form one or two rows at the border of each fascicle; endothelial nuclei of capillaries and scattered adipocytes complete the picture.
  • Artefact: after poor fixation the myelin may retract into globes ("myelin ovoids"), which must not be read as degeneration.

4.Reading the colours

HE leaves the myelin unstained, so the colour code is simple: nuclei blue-violet, axoplasm and collagen pink, adipose vacuoles empty. This is precisely why a longitudinal HE nerve cannot show myelin directly — the companion slide with osmium tetroxide is the one on which the myelin is black.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Parallel, wavy bundles of eosinophilic fibres with a perineurium outlining each fascicle — the single most reliable feature.
  • Nuclei are elongated and aligned along the fibres (Schwann cells, perineurial fibroblasts), never central and cigar-shaped as in smooth muscle.
  • No epithelium, no muscle, no glands, no lumen; the only "spaces" are dissolved myelin and adipose.
  • Vasa nervorum in the epineurium confirm that this is a nerve trunk and not a tendon.
Differential diagnosis
  • Smooth muscle, longitudinal section: spindle cells with a single central cigar-shaped nucleus and homogeneous eosinophilic cytoplasm; there is no perineurium and no fascicular architecture, and the cells form one continuous sheet.
  • Tendon: dense regular collagen bundles are strongly eosinophilic, but only rows of fibroblast nuclei lie between them; there are no axons, no endoneurium and no perineurium, and the bundles are straight rather than wavy.
  • Skeletal muscle, longitudinal section: cross-striations and multiple peripheral nuclei; the fibres are much thicker and unbranched.
  • Peripheral nerve, cross section: same organ, but the fascicles are round and the fibres are dots, not ribbons.
Clinical and functional note
  • After transection the distal stump undergoes Wallerian degeneration; surviving Schwann cells form longitudinal cords (Büngner's bands) that guide the regenerating sprouts, whose tangled front is the Perroncito apparatus — both are named in the nervous tissue lectures.
  • Regeneration fails when the endoneurial tubes are not aligned, which is why a sharp cut repairs better than a crush and why a neuroma may form at the proximal stump.
  • Compression neuropathies (carpal tunnel) damage the perineurial blood-nerve barrier and cause endoneurial oedema, so the nerve swells inside an unyielding tunnel.

V.27Peripheral nerve (cross section, HE)

Core answerThe slide is a peripheral nerve in transverse section, HE: a rounded cord divided by epineurial septa into fascicles, and inside each fascicle each myelinated fibre appears as a pale myelin ring around a coloured axonal dot — the classic "honeycomb" or "target" picture — with the fascicle bordered by the concentric lamellae of the perineurium.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Organ level, transverse plane. The plane of section is the reason the same organ looks completely different from V.26: instead of ribbons there are thousands of small circular fibre profiles cut across. The cord is polyfascicular; the number and size of fascicles vary from nerve to nerve and along the same nerve.

2.Low-power architecture

The nerve is round to oval, its surface formed by the epineurium; connective tissue septa radiate inwards and separate fascicles of very different calibre; adipose tissue and vessels lie in the epineurial spaces. Each fascicle has a distinct, thin, eosinophilic rim — the perineurium. A companion artery or vein may lie beside the nerve in the same connective tissue sheath.

  • Perineurium: several concentric lamellae of extremely flattened fibroblasts with elongated nuclei, joined by tight junctions and resting on basal laminae — the "pars epithelioidea" of the perineurium.
  • Endoneurium: delicate collagen between the fibres, with occasional endoneurial capillaries and mast cells.

The blue nuclei belong to Schwann cells, perineurial fibroblasts, endothelial cells and the sparse cells of the endoneurium; the pink is axoplasm and collagen. Nothing is black, because HE does not preserve myelin — the black myelin ring appears only after osmium tetroxide (V.29).

How to identify it at the exam
  • A dot-in-a-ring ("target") pattern repeated over a whole fascicle: that is a myelinated nerve in cross section.
  • A multi-layered cellular perineurium delimiting each fascicle — no other tissue in this series has this structure.
  • No lumen, no epithelium, no cartilage: only connective tissue sheaths and fibre profiles.
  • Compare directly with V.26: the same three sheaths, but the fibres are cut across.
Differential diagnosis
  • Artery: has a true lumen with an endothelial lining and a muscular media; the "rings" are not individual fibres but one elastic membrane.
  • Vein: lumen, thin irregular wall, no fascicular architecture.
  • Adipose tissue: round empty spaces, but each is one large vacuole with a nucleus squeezed at the margin; no perineurium, no axonal dots.
  • Spinal cord white matter: myelinated fibres too, but no perineurium and no epineurium — it belongs to the CNS, and glial nuclei (oligodendrocytes) replace Schwann cells.
Clinical and functional note
  • The perineurium is the anatomical basis of the blood-nerve barrier; its tight junctions keep proteins and circulating antibodies out of the endoneurial space.
  • Inflammatory demyelinating neuropathies (Guillain–Barré syndrome, diphtheria) attack the Schwann cell and myelin; the nerve conduction slows while the axon remains intact.
  • Nerve biopsy is reported on transverse sections, where the density of myelinated fibres per fascicle can be measured.

V.28Peripheral nerve (longit. section, Os)

Core answerThe slide is a peripheral nerve, longitudinal, treated with osmium tetroxide: the unsaturated lipids of the myelin sheath reduce osmium tetroxide to black reduced osmium, so the myelin segments are black, the axon inside them is pale, and the nodes of Ranvier appear as pale gaps between consecutive black internodes. There is no counterstain, so the image is essentially a black-on-yellow image of lipid.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Organ level, longitudinal plane, demonstrated with a lipid (osmium) method rather than a routine stain. Osmium tetroxide is both a fixative and a stain: it is reduced by unsaturated lipids to black osmium dioxide, so myelin and fat droplets become black while protein structures remain pale yellow-brown. It is the classical way to demonstrate myelin sheaths and fat.

2.Low-power architecture

The nerve is a cord of parallel fascicles in which each fibre is not uniformly dark but segmented: black internodes alternate with short pale gaps, so the fascicles look beaded or cross-striped. Between the fibres, connective tissue is pale because it contains little lipid; the epineurium may contain black adipose droplets. Perineurium and epineurium are therefore seen as pale bands framing the dark fascicles.

  • Each fibre is a chain of black cylinders: one cylinder is one internode, i.e. the myelin formed by one Schwann cell.
  • The axon runs as a pale line in the centre of the black cylinder; at the node the black sheath stops on both sides of a narrow pale band.
  • Schmidt–Lantermann clefts are oblique pale lines crossing the black internode: they are normal channels of residual Schwann-cell cytoplasm between the myelin lamellae, not a fixation artefact, and they are diagnostically useful.

4.Reading the colours

Only lipid is black. That single rule explains the whole slide: myelin black, axon pale, connective tissue pale, fat black. It also explains why the nuclei are almost invisible — osmium alone does not stain chromatin, so the slide is read by shape, not by nuclear detail, which is the opposite of what you do on the HE slides.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Black segmented bands with pale nodes of Ranvier: myelin stained with osmium.
  • Pale axonal core inside each black cylinder, and pale connective tissue between cylinders.
  • No blue nuclei at all — a monochrome, lipid-based preparation, not HE.
  • If only black droplets are seen without cylinders and nodes, the field is adipose tissue, not nerve.
Differential diagnosis
  • Peripheral nerve, HE (V.26): same shape of tissue, but myelin is pale, not black, and blue nuclei are everywhere.
  • Elastic fibres stained with orcein or resorcin-fuchsin: also dark, but they form a wavy network in a vessel wall or a cartilage, never segmented cylinders with nodes.
  • Adipose tissue (osmium or frozen section): black droplets of very different size with no internal pale core and no nodes.
  • Melanin or haemosiderin pigment: brown, granular and intracellular, not a smooth black sheath.
Clinical and functional note
  • Myelin is the electrical insulator of the axon; the gaps at the nodes allow saltatory conduction, which is why loss of myelin slows conduction even when the axon survives.
  • Myelin stains are the classical way to show demyelination and remyelination — the internodes become shorter and thinner after repair.
  • Osmium tetroxide is a dangerous vapour and is now used mainly for small blocks and for fat demonstration; paraffin sections of osmicated tissue must be cut with care.

V.29Peripheral nerve (cross section, Os)

Core answerThe slide is a nerve in transverse section treated with osmium: each myelinated fibre is a black myelin ring with a pale axonal centre, so a fascicle looks punctate or "bicycle-spoke" patterned; unmyelinated fibres show no black ring, and the perineurium stays pale, which is what outlines each fascicle.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Organ level, transverse plane, lipid (osmium) method. Here the myelin is seen as a ring rather than a segment, so the section asks a different question from V.28: not "where are the nodes" but "how many fibres are myelinated and how thick is their sheath".

2.Low-power architecture

A round cord whose fascicles stand out as fields of black rings on a pale background. The perineurium appears as a pale band around each fascicle — it contains no myelin — and the epineurium is a broad pale zone with black adipose droplets and vessel walls. Because the black rings are distributed evenly inside each fascicle, low power alone identifies the organ.

  • Myelin rings: concentric black annulus; the larger the fibre, the thicker the black ring relative to the pale axonal centre, and the larger the axon itself.
  • Axon: a pale dot in the middle of each ring; on tangentially cut fibres it becomes eccentric or even tangential, so rings must be judged in the plane of section.
  • Unmyelinated fibres: not individually ringed; they appear as small groups of pale dots, the whole group being enclosed by one Schwann cell (Remak bundle).

How to identify it at the exam
  • Black rings with pale centres, all of the same construction, filling a fascicle: osmium-stained nerve in cross section.
  • Pale band separating fascicles = perineurium; pale space between rings = endoneurium.
  • No nuclei visible and no eosinophilia — a monochrome lipid preparation.
  • Glycogen, collagen and cartilage stay pale, so a black structure here is always lipid-rich.
Differential diagnosis
  • Peripheral nerve, HE (V.27): the same dot-in-ring picture, but the rings are pale (dissolved myelin) and blue nuclei are visible.
  • Adipose tissue with osmium: black droplets of irregular size without a pale core and without a perineurium.
  • Artery with an elastic stain: concentric dark lamellae, but they surround one large lumen, not thousands of small axons.
  • White matter of the spinal cord or optic nerve: histologically central nervous tissue with oligodendrocyte myelin, no perineurium, no epineurium.
Clinical and functional note
  • Counting myelinated fibres and measuring their myelin thickness is the classical way to separate axonal from demyelinating neuropathy.
  • In demyelinating disease the axon survives but the black ring is thin or absent over segments; in axonal loss whole fibres disappear and the fascicle looks empty.
  • The pale perineurial band is not a passive wrapper: it is the diffusion barrier that keeps the endoneurial environment stable, and it hypertrophies in chronic compression.

V.30Astrocyte (GFAP IHC)

Core answerThe slide is an immunohistochemical preparation of central nervous tissue reacted for GFAP (glial fibrillary acidic protein), the intermediate filament characteristic of astrocytes: the astrocytes and all their processes are covered by the brown diaminobenzidine reaction product, while the background and nuclei carry only a pale counterstain. It demonstrates one cell type, not an organ's architecture.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Cellular level, demonstrated by a marker rather than by a histological stain. GFAP is an intermediate filament of neuroglial cells, especially astrocytes; because it is present in the cell body and throughout the processes, an anti-GFAP antibody with a peroxidase detection system makes the whole astrocyte visible. The tissue is brain — fibrous astrocytes of the white matter are the easiest to demonstrate, and vessels and the subpial surface are included in favourable fields.

2.Low-power architecture

There is no capsule, no epithelium and no layered wall: the field is a more or less uniform network of brown lines on a pale background. The network is denser in white matter (long straight processes of fibrous astrocytes) and finer in grey matter (protoplasmic astrocytes); around blood vessels the brown processes converge to form a ring, and at the surface of the tissue they form a continuous brown limiting layer. This perivascular and subpial accentuation is the first orientation point at low power.

  • Cell shape: a small brown body with several long radiating processes — a star or spider outline. The processes branch irregularly and end in flattened expansions.
  • Reaction product: the brown precipitate fills the cytoplasm and the processes, so the nucleus appears as a negative pale area inside the brown cell body. Read the shape, not the nucleus.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Brown, star-shaped cells with radiating processes on a pale background: an immunohistochemical preparation, not a routine stain.
  • The marker is GFAP, an intermediate filament of neuroglia — the astrocyte's own cytoskeleton.
  • Perivascular and subpial brown end-feet prove the processes belong to astrocytes, not to neurons.
  • The reaction product is cytoplasmic and follows the processes; a purely nuclear signal would mean a different marker.
Differential diagnosis
  • Nissl or cresyl violet preparation: neurons are purple because of their Nissl substance, but no cell processes are stained and astrocytes show only their nuclei — the opposite of this slide.
  • Golgi silver impregnation: one whole neuron is black (perikaryon, dendrites, axon), not a population of small star cells.
  • Oligodendrocytes: small, round, dense nuclei with no GFAP and no radiating processes; they form myelin in the CNS.
  • Microglia: of monocyte origin, ramified but GFAP-negative; they are demonstrated with macrophage markers such as CD68.
  • Ependyma: a cuboidal-to-columnar lining of the ventricles and central canal, not scattered star cells.
Clinical and functional note
  • Astrocytes induce the tight junctions of CNS capillaries and cover them with end-feet, so they are part of the blood-brain barrier; they also buffer extracellular potassium and take up neurotransmitters.
  • After injury astrocytes proliferate and become GFAP-rich (reactive astrogliosis), forming the glial scar that walls off a lesion but also blocks axonal regeneration.
  • GFAP is used in diagnostic pathology to identify astrocytic tumours, and GFAP mutations cause Alexander disease — which is why the same stain serves the histology class and the tumour board.

V.31Aorta (HE)

Core answerThe slide is the wall of the aorta — the prototype elastic (conducting) artery — cut transversely and stained with HE. Its dominating feature is an extremely thick tunica media built of concentric fenestrated elastic membranes with smooth muscle cells between them; on HE these membranes read as wavy, glassy eosinophilic bands, so the media looks striped. The lumen stays round and open, the intima is thin and the adventitia is thin but carries vasa vasorum.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Organ level: the full thickness of a large vessel wall in cross section. The aorta is the type specimen of an elastic artery (arteria elastotypica), the group that also contains its largest branches — subclavian, common carotid, renal and common iliac arteries. The plane of section is transverse, so every coat is seen as a concentric ring around the lumen.

2.Low-power architecture

Three coats are visible at once, and their proportions are the diagnosis. The lumen is large, round and not collapsed, because the elastic wall retains its shape after the pressure falls. The intima is only a thin pale line; the media occupies most of the wall and is conspicuously striped; the adventitia is thin, paler and blends into the periaortic fat, with small vessels (vasa vasorum) inside it. In proportion to the very wide lumen the wall looks thin, even though in absolute terms it is a massive structure.

  • Vasa vasorum: small arteries, capillaries and veins in the adventitia and outer media — the inner part of the aortic wall is nourished by diffusion from the lumen.
  • No valves, no clear intimo-medial boundary: unlike a muscular artery, the aorta does not single out one internal elastic membrane; the whole media is elastic.

4.Reading the colours

On HE both elastic membranes and collagen are eosinophilic, so colour alone cannot separate them; the discriminator is the regular wavy periodicity of the media. This is exactly why the companion slide is stained with an elastic method: HE shows the architecture, resorcin-fuchsin proves the elastin.

How to identify it at the exam
  • A very thick media full of regular wavy eosinophilic bands alternating with smooth muscle: elastic artery.
  • Round, wide, uncollapsed lumen with a thin intima and a thin adventitia containing vasa vasorum.
  • No single internal elastic membrane standing out, and no valves — those belong to other vessels.
  • Many elastic lamellae = aorta or its large branches; a single elastic membrane = muscular artery.
Differential diagnosis
  • Muscular (distributing) artery: media of many layers of pure smooth muscle with one thick continuous internal elastic membrane and a prominent external elastic membrane; adventitia relatively thicker; lumen much smaller.
  • Large vein: thin media, thickest adventitia with longitudinal collagen and smooth muscle bundles, irregular collapsed lumen, valves possible.
  • Pulmonary trunk: also elastic, but its media is thinner because pulmonary arterial pressure is far lower than aortic pressure.
  • Umbilical artery: a muscular artery whose media contains no elastic membranes at all, although it is a large vessel.
Clinical and functional note
  • The elastic recoil of the media converts the intermittent ejection of the left ventricle into continuous flow during diastole — the "elastic spring" or Windkessel effect.
  • Atherosclerosis begins in the intima as lipid accumulation after endothelial injury; dissection is a longitudinal intimal tear that lets blood split the media; an aneurysm is a bulge caused by loss of the load-bearing elastic and muscular elements.
  • Because the inner wall is avascular and fed by diffusion, the vasa vasorum of the outer wall become clinically important in inflammatory aortitis and in the ischaemic outer media.

V.32Aorta (resorcin-fuchsin)

Core answerThe same aortic wall stained with resorcin-fuchsin, a selective elastic stain that colours elastin brown-purple to blue-black. The outcome is a media of dozens of continuous wavy dark lamellae running around the lumen, with unstained smooth muscle cells between them: this is the definitive histological proof that the vessel is an elastic artery.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Organ level, transverse section, demonstrated with a histochemical method for a single matrix component. Resorcin-fuchsin is one of the classical elastic stains; it is applied to show exactly the element that HE cannot resolve, and it is therefore the natural companion to the HE section of the same vessel.

2.Low-power architecture

The whole media turns into a regular series of parallel wavy dark lines, evenly spaced around the lumen; the intima carries a thin dark line at its base, and the adventitia is almost free of dark lines except for the walls of its small vessels. Because the lines are continuous and evenly repeated, low power alone is enough to call the vessel an elastic artery.

  • The internal elastic membrane is the first dark lamella of the series and the external elastic membrane the last; there is no sharp change of structure across either, which is typical of elastic arteries.
  • The adventitia shows scattered dark elastic fibres and the elastic laminae of its small vessels; the intima may show a thin dark line.

Dark = elastin. Pale = smooth muscle, collagen and ground substance. The stain is therefore a "negative" picture of the muscle: the muscle fibres are seen only as the unstained gaps between the lamellae, and their number gives an idea of how much contractile tissue is packed between the elastic sheets.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Dozens of parallel, evenly spaced, wavy dark lamellae filling a thick media: elastic stain of an elastic artery, i.e. aorta or its large branches.
  • The number of membranes, not their presence, is the discriminator: many = elastic artery, one or two = muscular artery.
  • The dark bands are continuous around the lumen and give the wall its corrugated ("accordion") profile.
  • If the dark material is a fine network in the adventitia only, you are looking at a vein or a small vessel.
Differential diagnosis
  • Muscular artery with the same stain: only the internal elastic membrane (and sometimes an external one) is strongly coloured, so the media shows 1–2 lines instead of dozens.
  • Orcein instead of resorcin-fuchsin: the same target, but red-brown rather than blue-black; never read the colour as a different structure.
  • Verhoeff's haematoxylin: elastin is black, but nuclei are black too, which mimics numerous fine lamellae if the section is over-stained.
  • HE section of the same aorta: architecture is identical, but the elastic membranes are merely eosinophilic and cannot be counted reliably.
Clinical and functional note
  • Elastin is the element that stores the energy of systole and returns it in diastole; when it fragments with age, systolic pressure rises and diastolic pressure falls.
  • In Marfan syndrome and in cystic medial degeneration the elastic membranes are fragmented and the media is weakened, which predisposes to aortic dilatation and dissection.
  • Elastic stains are used in diagnostic histopathology to demonstrate the integrity of the internal elastic membrane, for example in inflammatory disease of arteries.

V.33Artery, vein (HE)

Core answerThe slide shows an artery and its companion vein side by side in transverse section, HE. The artery has a thick wall, a small round uncollapsed lumen and a muscular media, so its wall-to-lumen ratio is high; the vein has a thin wall, a large irregular collapsed lumen, a thin media and an adventitia that is its thickest coat. Both are paired because they share one connective tissue sheath in a neurovascular bundle.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Organ level, two vessels in one transverse section. The pair is deliberately chosen: comparing two organs with the same three coats side by side is the fastest way to learn what each coat does, because every difference in the field is a function of pressure.

2.Low-power architecture

The two lumens may be of comparable size, but the walls are not. The artery is round, its wall is thick and its lumen relatively small; a wavy eosinophilic internal elastic membrane is often visible inside the media. The vein is flattened or stellate, its lumen often contains blood or a clot, its wall is thin and merges gradually with the surrounding connective tissue, so no sharp outer border exists.

  • Vein: a very thin intima, a media of only a few muscle layers, and a broad adventitia of collagen bundles running longitudinally; the boundary between media and adventitia is blurred.

  • Valves, when present, are paired folds of intima projecting into the venous lumen.

4.Reading the colours

Both walls are pink, so colour is useless here: the diagnosis is made by shape and proportion — round versus collapsed, thick wall versus thin wall, media-thick versus adventitia-thick. That is exactly the order in which the examiner expects the comparison to be spoken.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Three-point rule: lumen shape (round vs collapsed), wall-to-lumen ratio (high vs low), which coat is thickest (media vs adventitia).
  • The wavy internal elastic membrane is the artery's signature; the vein has nothing comparable.
  • The vein's wall fades into the surrounding connective tissue, the artery's adventitia is a distinct boundary.
  • Both vessels have three coats — the slide is a comparison, so name both sets, not just the artery.
Differential diagnosis
  • Elastic artery (aorta): no single internal elastic membrane; instead the media is striped by dozens of wavy elastic bands (see V.31 and V.32).
  • Large vein: adventitia is enormously thickened and contains longitudinal smooth muscle bundles; medium veins have valves.
  • Lymphatic vessel: even thinner wall, no erythrocytes in the lumen, numerous valves, irregular calibre.
  • Peripheral nerve: no lumen at all, only fascicles and sheaths.
Clinical and functional note
  • The artery's thick media resists arterial pressure and regulates peripheral resistance; the vein's thin wall and large lumen make it a low-pressure capacitance vessel holding most of the blood volume.
  • When venous valves become incompetent and the wall dilates, varicose veins result; inflammation of a superficial vein is thrombophlebitis.
  • Arterial occlusion (thrombus, embolus, atherosclerosis) causes ischaemia of the territory with pain and pallor, whereas venous occlusion causes swelling and oedema — the two clinical pictures mirror the two wall structures.

V.34Artery, vein (Orcein)

Core answerThe same artery and vein stained with orcein, which colours elastic fibres and elastic membranes red-brown. The artery is identified at once by a sharply defined, wavy red-brown internal elastic membrane at the intimo-medial junction (with an external elastic membrane in larger muscular arteries), whereas the vein contains only scanty, fine elastic fibres in its intima and adventitia.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Organ level, transverse section, selective elastic stain. Orcein is the elastic stain that preserves the best morphological detail of the vessel wall, so it is the ideal slide on which to point at the internal elastic membrane and name it.

2.Low-power architecture

On the arterial side a single bright, continuous, scalloped red-brown line traces the lumen just outside the endothelium: this is the internal elastic membrane, and it immediately marks the vessel as an artery. On the venous side there is no such line — only faint, interrupted red-brown fibres in the intima and in the adventitia. The arterial media also shows a fine red-brown mesh of elastic fibres between the muscle cells.

How to identify it at the exam
  • One crisp wavy red-brown line at the intimo-medial junction = internal elastic membrane = muscular artery.
  • Almost no elastic material in the wall = vein.
  • Dozens of parallel red-brown lamellae = elastic artery (aorta, large branches).
  • The stain is selective for elastin, so everything red-brown on the slide is elastic tissue — arteries and veins cannot be confused by colour, only by pattern.
Differential diagnosis
  • Resorcin-fuchsin on the same vessels: identical pattern, blue-black instead of red-brown; the colour difference must not be read as a structural difference.
  • HE of the same vessels: the internal elastic membrane is present but only weakly eosinophilic and easily missed.
  • Elastic cartilage: also orcein-positive, but the elastic fibres are in a chondromucoid matrix with chondrocytes in lacunae, not in a vessel wall.
  • Nerve: the wavy fascicles of a nerve may catch the eye, but there is no lumen and no elastic membrane.
Clinical and functional note
  • The internal elastic membrane is the landmark used to measure intimal thickening in arteriosclerosis: the distance from endothelium to membrane is the intima, and it grows with disease.
  • Loss and fragmentation of elastic fibres reduce the resilience of the vessel and contribute to dilatation and to the formation of varices in veins.
  • Because orcein also stains the elastic fibres of skin and cartilage, the method is used beyond the vascular system, but in this course it is the vascular pattern that matters.

V.35Spermatic cord (HE)

Core answerThe slide is a transverse section of the spermatic cord (funiculus spermaticus), HE: a neurovascular bundle running from the deep inguinal ring to the testis. Two structures make the diagnosis — the ductus deferens, a small-lumen tube with an exceptionally thick three-layered smooth muscle wall and a pseudostratified stereociliated epithelium, and the pampiniform plexus, a cluster of thick-walled veins — both wrapped in loose connective tissue and in the coverings of the abdominal wall, including the striated cremaster muscle.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Organ level, transverse section through the cord. The cord is not a viscus with a lumen: it is a bundle of ducts, vessels, nerves and lymphatics held together by connective tissue and covered by the layers it dragged with it during the descent of the testis — external spermatic fascia, cremaster muscle with its cremasteric fascia, and internal spermatic fascia. The plane of section is transverse, so every content is cut across and counted.

2.Low-power architecture

Outside, if the scrotal skin has been included, there is skin and dartos; otherwise the outermost layer is the thin external spermatic fascia, followed by bundles of cremaster muscle (striated, in cross section) and then the internal spermatic fascia. The interior is loose connective tissue with fat, and within it the contents are seen: one ductus deferens with a tiny, star-shaped lumen and a very thick pink wall; a crowd of veins of different calibre with thick muscular walls; one or two small arteries; nerve bundles; thin-walled lymphatic vessels.

  • Ductus deferens: tiny stellate lumen + extremely thick three-layered muscle coat + pseudostratified columnar stereociliated epithelium — nothing else in the body looks like this.
  • Pampiniform plexus: many thick-walled veins of different sizes packed together.
  • Cremaster: striated muscle bundles around the whole bundle prove you are in the spermatic cord.
  • No glands, no cartilage, no respiratory epithelium: the differential list is short.
Differential diagnosis
  • Umbilical cord (V.46): amniotic epithelium, abundant Wharton's jelly, two arteries and one vein, no muscular duct with a narrow lumen and no striated cremaster.
  • Epididymis: one highly coiled duct with regular, tall pseudostratified epithelium and long stereocilia, surrounded by a thin smooth muscle coat; there is no thick three-layer muscularis and no pampiniform cluster.
  • A single thick-walled duct without a cord: the ductus deferens alone, without the vessels, nerves and coverings, is not the spermatic cord — always name the pampiniform plexus and the cremaster alongside it.
  • Oesophagus (the other "thick muscular tube"): wide lumen, non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, muscularis mucosae and submucosa, two muscle layers with striated muscle in its upper third, and never a pampiniform plexus.
Clinical and functional note
  • The pampiniform plexus cools arterial blood on its way to the testis by countercurrent exchange; dilatation of the plexus (varicocele) raises testicular temperature and is a recognised cause of male subfertility.
  • Because the cord passes through the inguinal canal, it is the pathway of an indirect inguinal (funicular) hernia, and the persistence of the processus vaginalis explains hydrocele and, in part, cryptorchidism.
  • Torsion of the cord compromises first the thin-walled veins and then the artery, so the testis becomes congested and ischaemic — a surgical emergency.

V.36Blood smear (MGG)

Core answerThe slide is a peripheral blood smear stained with May–Grünwald–Giemsa (MGG), a Romanovsky-type stain: the acidic dye eosin colours basic structures (haemoglobin, granules) pink-red, while the basic methylene-blue/azure dyes colour acidic structures (nuclear chromatin, RNA) purple-blue. Erythrocytes dominate the field; leucocytes and platelets are the minority to be identified one by one.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Cellular level: this is not a section but a smear — a drop of blood spread into a monolayer, air-dried, fixed in methanol and then stained. Morphology is best read in the thin tail ("feather edge") of the smear, where the cells lie flat and do not overlap; in the thick body of the smear erythrocytes are piled up and distorted.

2.Low-power architecture

The field is a pink sea of erythrocytes with a few purple nucleated cells and tiny lilac platelets scattered between them. Because 60–70 % of leucocytes are neutrophils, the first nucleated cell found is likely to be a neutrophil. The background should be clean; precipitated stain or dirt is an artefact, not a cell.

  • Agranulocytes: no specific granules; lymphocyte has a round dense nucleus with a thin cytoplasmic rim, monocyte has an indented nucleus and generous grey-blue cytoplasm.

4.Reading the colours

MGG gives a fixed colour code: erythrocytes pink-red, nuclear chromatin purple, myeloid cytoplasm pale pink, lymphocyte cytoplasm pale blue, eosinophilic granules brick-red, basophilic granules dark blue, azurophil (lysosomal) granules purple-red. Learning these seven colours is enough to classify every cell on the slide.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Erythrocytes have no nucleus; every nucleated cell in the field is a leucocyte.
  • Classify each leucocyte in two steps: nucleus shape first, then granule colour.
  • Neutrophil = lobed nucleus + neutral pink granules; eosinophil = bilobed + brick-red granules; basophil = obscured nucleus + dark blue-black granules.
  • Lymphocyte = round dense nucleus with a thin rim; monocyte = largest cell, indented nucleus, abundant grey-blue cytoplasm.
  • Platelets are anucleate fragments, not cells, and must not be counted as white cells.
Differential diagnosis
  • Monocyte versus large lymphocyte: the monocyte has a folded or kidney-shaped nucleus, more cytoplasm and vacuoles; the large lymphocyte still has a round nucleus occupying most of the cell.
  • Eosinophil versus neutrophil with toxic granulation: eosinophil granules are larger, uniform and brick-red, and the nucleus has two lobes.
  • Basophil versus stain precipitate: precipitate lies on top of cells and is irregular and structureless, whereas basophil granules belong to one cell and surround its nucleus.
  • Crenated erythrocytes (spiculated artefact of drying) must not be reported as pathological poikilocytosis on a poor smear.
Clinical and functional note
  • The differential count is a daily clinical test: neutrophilia points to bacterial infection, eosinophilia to allergy or parasites, lymphocytosis to viral infection, and the appearance of blast cells suggests leukaemia.
  • Erythrocyte morphology reports anaemia: hypochromia and microcytosis in iron deficiency, macrocytes in B12/folate deficiency, sickle cells in haemoglobinopathy.
  • Intra-erythrocytic parasites (malaria, babesia) are diagnosed on exactly this stain, and MGG is also used for bone marrow aspirates, where the same colour rules apply.

V.37Red bone marrow (HE)

Core answerThe slide is red (haematopoietically active) bone marrow in HE: a highly cellular tissue filling the intertrabecular spaces of spongy bone, supported by reticular connective tissue and penetrated by wide sinusoids, in which the various blood-cell lineages mature side by side with megakaryocytes. Its hallmark is the mixture — several lineages at several stages of maturation in a single field.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Tissue and organ level at once: the slide usually contains fragments of spongy bone with the marrow in the spaces between the trabeculae, obtained by aspiration or biopsy, or taken from a long bone. Red marrow is the active form; yellow marrow is the same stroma loaded with adipocytes and haematopoietically silent, and it can be reactivated. In the adult, red marrow persists in the axial skeleton and in the proximal humerus and femur.

2.Low-power architecture

Pink bony trabeculae border dark purple cellular fields. The marrow is dark because nuclei are densely packed; the empty round spaces are adipocytes, more numerous with age; the pale channels are sinusoids. Megakaryocytes stand out even at this magnification as the largest single cells of the field, and this mixture of many cell types is itself the diagnostic low-power feature.

  • Bone trabeculae plus a densely cellular space filled with several lineages at once = bone marrow.
  • Megakaryocytes are the single most useful confirmatory cell: large, multilobated, sinusoidal.
  • Erythroid islands with dense nuclei and myeloid cells with paler indented nuclei sit together — no lymphoid organ shows this mixture.
  • Adipocytes of all sizes and sinusoids complete the picture; absence of capsule and of epithelium excludes lymphoid organs.
Differential diagnosis
  • Yellow bone marrow: dominated by adipocytes with only sparse cells; no erythroid islands, no megakaryocytes.
  • Spleen red pulp: also blood-rich and sinusoidal, but there are cords of Billroth and no bony trabeculae containing a mixed haematopoietic population with megakaryocytes.
  • Bone ground section: shows osteons and lacunae but no cells inside the lacunae and no marrow at all.
  • Lymph node or thymus: encapsulated, lymphoid only, no bone and no megakaryocytes.
Clinical and functional note
  • Marrow is sampled from the posterior iliac crest; the aspirate is smeared (MGG) and the biopsy is decalcified and stained with HE, as on this slide.
  • Marrow cellularity and lineage distribution diagnose aplasia (empty, fatty marrow), leukaemia (sheets of blast cells), myelofibrosis and metastatic carcinoma.
  • When the body needs more blood cells (haemolysis, chronic hypoxia), yellow marrow can reconvert to red marrow, which is why red marrow reappears in the diaphyses of long bones in adults.

V.38Thymus (HE)

Core answerThe slide is the thymus, HE: a lobulated lymphoid organ in which each lobule has a dark cortex packed with lymphocytes and a pale medulla containing Hassall's corpuscles. The medulla of neighbouring lobules is continuous and there are no germinal centres, because the thymus is a primary lymphoid organ where T cells are generated, not a filter for lymph.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Organ level, cut so that capsule, septa, cortex and medulla are all visible. The thymus lies in the anterior mediastinum behind the sternum; it is largest in childhood and is gradually replaced by adipose tissue with age, although it never disappears completely. Its stroma is unique: reticular epithelium, an endodermal derivative, replaces the usual reticular connective tissue.

2.Low-power architecture

A capsule of dense connective tissue sends septa inwards, but the septa do not traverse the whole organ, so the "lobules" are false lobules. Each lobule is basophilic outside (cortex) and eosinophilic inside (medulla); because the septa are incomplete, the pale medulla of adjacent lobules joins across the organ. In the medulla, pink whorled Hassall's corpuscles are visible even at low power.

4.Reading the colours

Blue means lymphocytes: the cortex is blue because it is lymphocyte-rich. Pink means epithelium: the medulla is pink because epithelial reticular cells and Hassall's corpuscles predominate. Reading a thymus is therefore reading a blue-outside/pink-inside pattern, and that polarity is the exact opposite of a lymph node, where the cortex is nodular and the medulla is pale for a different reason.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Dark cortex, pale medulla, and a medulla that is continuous between lobules.
  • Hassall's corpuscles — concentric pink whorls in the medulla — are the single most specific structure.
  • No germinal centres and no subcapsular sinus: this is a primary lymphoid organ.
  • The stroma is epithelial (cytokeratin-positive), not reticular connective tissue, so no reticular fibre network is seen.
Differential diagnosis
  • Lymph node: cortex with nodules and germinal centres, a subcapsular sinus under the capsule, and a medulla of cords and sinuses; no Hassall's corpuscles.
  • Spleen: no cortex and medulla at all; white pulp arranged around central arterioles, red pulp with cords and sinusoids.
  • Palatine tonsil: covered by stratified squamous epithelium with deep crypts on the luminal side.
  • Parathyroid gland: nests and cords of chief cells with no lymphocytes and no lobulation of this kind.
Clinical and functional note
  • The thymus is largest in childhood and undergoes involution after puberty, being replaced by fat — which is why an adult mediastinal scan shows mostly adipose tissue.
  • Thymoma is associated with myasthenia gravis, and removal of a hyperplastic or neoplastic thymus can improve the disease.
  • Failure of the third and fourth pharyngeal pouch derivatives (DiGeorge syndrome) causes thymic aplasia with T-cell deficiency, while the blood-thymus barrier explains why circulating antigens do not normally reach developing T cells.

V.39Spleen (HE)

Core answerThe slide is the spleen, HE: an encapsulated organ with a dense capsule and trabeculae, whose parenchyma is white pulp — periarteriolar lymphoid sheath and lymphoid nodules around a central arteriole — embedded in red pulp made of splenic cords of Billroth and wide sinusoids. There is no cortex and no medulla, because the spleen filters blood, not lymph.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Organ level, with capsule, trabeculae and both pulps visible. The spleen lies in the peritoneal cavity and is the largest accumulation of lymphoid tissue in the body. Its stroma is reticular connective tissue, its framework comes from the capsule, and its vascular arrangement — open circulation in humans — explains the structure of the red pulp.

2.Low-power architecture

A thick capsule of dense collagenous connective tissue with a mesothelial surface sends trabeculae into the organ; trabecular arteries and veins run inside them. Between the trabeculae the parenchyma is a mosaic of basophilic white pulp islands and eosinophilic, blood-filled red pulp. This two-pulp pattern is recognisable at low power and is the first thing to say aloud.

  • Red pulp: cords of Billroth are packed with erythrocytes and macrophages; the sinusoids between them are wider than ordinary capillaries and are lined by stave-like endothelial cells whose gaps let red cells squeeze through.

  • Trabecular vessels: arteries and veins within the trabeculae are surrounded by dense collagen, which distinguishes them from vessels in the pulp.
  • No subcapsular sinus and no afferent lymphatics: the spleen has only efferent lymphatics at the hilum.

4.Reading the colours

Blue = lymphoid tissue (white pulp). Red-pink = blood, sinusoids and cords (red pulp). Deep pink solid bundles = capsule and trabeculae. The contrast between the two pulps is the whole slide: once you can point to white pulp and red pulp, the organ is identified.

How to identify it at the exam
  • White pulp and red pulp side by side, with no cortex/medulla arrangement.
  • A central arteriole running eccentrically through a lymphoid nodule — pathognomonic for the spleen.
  • Dense capsule with trabeculae containing trabecular arteries and veins.
  • Red pulp sinusoids lined by stave-like cells and packed with erythrocytes, plus macrophages with ingested red cells.
Differential diagnosis
  • Lymph node: cortex with nodules and a subcapsular sinus, paracortex, medullary cords and sinuses; no white/red pulp and no central arteriole in a nodule.
  • Thymus: lobules with dark cortex and pale medulla, Hassall's corpuscles, no germinal centres, no erythrocyte-filled pulp.
  • Palatine tonsil: epithelium with crypts on the surface — epithelium is the giveaway.
  • Haemorrhagic or infarcted tissue: blood everywhere but no organised white pulp around an arteriole.
Clinical and functional note
  • The spleen filters blood: it removes aged erythrocytes (about 120 days old), recycles iron, and mounts immune responses against blood-borne antigens.
  • Splenomegaly accompanies portal hypertension, haemolytic and haematological disease; the enlarged organ is fragile and may rupture after relatively minor trauma.
  • After splenectomy the loss of filtering and of antibody response to encapsulated bacteria increases susceptibility to overwhelming sepsis, which is why vaccination is required.

V.40Lymph node (HE)

Core answerThe slide is a lymph node, HE: a bean-shaped, encapsulated organ with afferent lymphatics entering the convex surface, a subcapsular sinus, a cortex containing lymphoid nodules with germinal centres, a paracortex, and a medulla of cords and sinuses, with efferent vessels leaving at the hilum. It is the filter interposed in the course of lymphatic vessels.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Organ level, cut through the hilum when possible. The node is embedded along a lymphatic vessel, so lymph enters through several afferent vessels on the convex side, percolates through the sinuses and leaves through efferent vessels at the hilum, where the blood vessels also enter and leave.

2.Low-power architecture

The organ is bean-shaped. Under the capsule a pale subcapsular (marginal) sinus runs as a clear band. The outer cortex is studded with basophilic nodules, some with pale germinal centres; the deeper paracortex is diffuse and paler; the medulla at the hilum is pale and irregular, made of cords and sinuses. Trabeculae from the capsule divide the cortex, and the hilum is the indented side where vessels converge.

  • Germinal centre: pale, with centroblasts, centrocytes and scattered tingible-body macrophages phagocytosing apoptotic debris (the "starry sky"); the dark ring around it is the mantle zone of small resting B cells.
  • Sinuses: crossed by reticular cells and fibres, which slow the lymph and give macrophages time to phagocytose antigen — the node's filter mesh.
  • High endothelial venules in the paracortex: cuboidal endothelium that allows lymphocytes to leave the blood and enter the node.
  • Medullary cords are rich in plasma cells, whose eccentric nucleus with a "clock-face" chromatin and basophilic cytoplasm marks antibody production.

4.Reading the colours

Cortex is blue where lymphocytes are dense; germinal centres are paler because the cells are larger with more cytoplasm; sinuses are pale because they contain lymph and only a loose mesh; the medulla is pale overall. The colour code therefore maps directly onto the path of lymph from the subcapsular sinus to the hilum.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Bean shape with a capsule, a subcapsular sinus and a hilum.
  • Cortex outside, medulla inside — the same peripheral-dark, central-pale plan as a thymic lobule (so not a discriminating feature by itself); here the cortex carries lymphoid follicles and the medulla is discontinuous.
  • Lymphoid nodules with germinal centres in the cortex and medullary cords and sinuses centrally.
  • Both afferent and efferent lymphatics (the thymus has only efferent vessels, the spleen none afferent).
Differential diagnosis
  • Thymus: lobules with dark cortex and pale medulla, Hassall's corpuscles, no germinal centres and no subcapsular sinus.
  • Spleen: white pulp around a central arteriole inside a red pulp; no cortex/medulla, no subcapsular sinus.
  • Palatine tonsil: surface epithelium with deep crypts; lymphoid tissue directly under the epithelium, capsule only at the base.
  • Appendix or Peyer's patch: lymphoid nodules in the gut wall under an intestinal epithelium, without a capsule.
Clinical and functional note
  • The lymph node is an immunological filter: about 99 % of arriving antigen is phagocytosed by macrophages in the sinuses, and the remainder is presented to lymphocytes in the nodules.
  • Sentinel lymph node is the first node draining a tumour and the first to receive metastases; its biopsy is part of cancer staging.
  • Lymphadenopathy in infection is due to germinal-centre formation, and removal of regional nodes (axillary clearance) causes lymphoedema because the drainage route is interrupted.

V.41Palatine tonsil (HE)

Core answerThe slide is the palatine tonsil, HE: a mass of lymphoid tissue covered by stratified squamous non-keratinized epithelium that invaginates into deep crypts, with numerous lymphoid nodules and a fibrous capsule only on its deep aspect, resting on the pharyngeal muscle. It is a partially encapsulated lymphoid organ guarding the entrance of the digestive tract.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Organ level, cut so that both the mucosal surface and the deep capsular side are present. The tonsil is part of Waldeyer's tonsillar ring and lies in the tonsillar fossa between the palatal arches; it is a peripheral lymphoid organ but partially encapsulated and has only efferent lymphatics.

2.Low-power architecture

One side of the slide is epithelium, folded into deep crypts whose lumens contain debris; beneath it lies a broad field of lymphoid tissue with nodules, many of them with pale germinal centres. At the base a fibrous capsule separates the tonsil from the deeper connective tissue, where skeletal muscle of the pharynx and seromucous glands are seen. There is no subcapsular sinus and no cortex/medulla distinction.

  • Crypt content: amorphous detritus, desquamated epithelial cells, bacteria and neutrophils — the reason tonsillar crypts are a site of repeated infection.

The epithelium is pink with blue nuclei and is easy to follow into the crypts; the lymphoid tissue is blue because of its dense nuclei; germinal centres are pale; detritus in the crypt is a mixture of blue and pink debris. The rule to remember: epithelium plus crypts means tonsil, because no other lymphoid organ on the list has an epithelial surface.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Stratified squamous non-keratinized epithelium with deep crypts over lymphoid tissue: palatine tonsil.
  • Capsule only on the deep side, so the luminal surface is free — the definition of a partially encapsulated organ.
  • Lymphoid nodules with germinal centres but no subcapsular sinus and no afferent lymphatics.
  • Skeletal muscle deep to the capsule confirms the pharyngeal location.
Differential diagnosis
  • Pharyngeal and tubal tonsils: the surface is respiratory — ciliated pseudostratified columnar epithelium — not stratified squamous; the pharyngeal tonsil also lacks a thick capsule.
  • Lingual tonsil: a single crypt and no capsule; lymphoid nodules over the root of the tongue.
  • Lymph node: no epithelium and no crypts; it has a subcapsular sinus and both afferent and efferent lymphatics.
  • Thymus: lobules with cortex and medulla and Hassall's corpuscles, no crypts and no epithelium on the surface.
  • Oesophagus: also lined by stratified squamous non-keratinized epithelium, but it has muscularis mucosae, submucosa and a two-layered muscularis externa, not lymphoid nodules in crypts.
Clinical and functional note
  • Acute inflammation of the palatine tonsil (correctly amygdalitis, though "tonsillitis" is used clinically) fills the crypts with pus, seen as white spots; a peritonsillar abscess is a feared complication.
  • Enlarged pharyngeal tonsil ("adenoid vegetation") obstructs the nasal airway in children and causes mouth breathing, snoring and recurrent middle-ear disease.
  • The tonsil produces mainly IgA, which is stabilised in secretions by the secretory component — an effective mucosal defence at the entrance of the digestive tract.

V.42Larynx (HE)

Core answerThe slide is a section through the larynx, HE: a cavitated organ whose mucosa forms two kinds of folds — the vestibular (false) fold with loose connective tissue and glands, and the vocal (true) fold with stratified squamous epithelium, Reinke's space, the elastic vocal ligament and the vocalis muscle — all supported by a skeleton of hyaline and elastic cartilage and moved by striated laryngeal muscles.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Organ level, cut frontally or coronally through the larynx, or sagittally through the epiglottis. The lumen has an hourglass shape: vestibule above, glottis in the middle, infraglottic cavity below. The larynx is the first part of the lower respiratory tract and the organ of voice production.

2.Low-power architecture

An air-filled lumen is bounded by mucosa thrown into folds; pieces of cartilage occupy a large part of the section, and bundles of striated skeletal muscle and seromucous glands lie between them. The vocal fold is the wedge-shaped projection with a very loose core, and the vestibular fold lies above it. The mixture of cartilage, striated muscle and glandular mucosa in one slide is what makes the larynx unique in this series.

Hyaline cartilage is basophilic with chondrocytes in groups; elastic cartilage has a similar matrix but elastic fibres in it (visible only with an elastic stain); skeletal muscle is strongly eosinophilic with peripheral nuclei; glands are pale and lobulated. Then ask where the epithelium changes: that transition marks the vocal fold.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Cartilage plus striated muscle plus a cavitated mucosa in one section: larynx.
  • Two epithelia — respiratory and stratified squamous — meeting at the vocal fold.
  • Two cartilages — hyaline and elastic — in the same organ.
  • The vocal fold is identified by its stratified squamous epithelium, loose Reinke's space, elastic vocal ligament and vocalis muscle, in that order from the surface.
Differential diagnosis
  • Trachea: a single type of epithelium (respiratory) throughout, C-shaped hyaline cartilage, no stratified squamous lining and no mucosal folds of this kind.
  • Oesophagus: stratified squamous non-keratinized epithelium everywhere, no cartilage, muscularis mucosae, submucosa and two layers of muscularis externa with skeletal muscle in the upper third.
  • Bronchus: cartilage present only as irregular plates around the whole circumference, respiratory epithelium throughout, no striated muscle.
  • Epiglottis alone: elastic cartilage with two epithelial surfaces (lingual and laryngeal) and taste buds on the lingual side.
Clinical and functional note
  • Because Reinke's space is extremely loose, the vocal folds swell readily — vocal nodules and polyps (singer's nodes) arise there, and the resulting mass prevents complete closure of the glottis, producing hoarseness.
  • Most laryngeal carcinomas arise from the stratified squamous epithelium of the vocal fold, which is one reason the epithelial transition zone matters clinically.
  • The vocal folds are moved by the laryngeal muscles supplied by the recurrent laryngeal nerve; its damage (thyroid surgery, tumour) causes hoarseness, and bilateral damage can obstruct the airway.

V.43Trachea (HE)

Core answerThe slide is the trachea in transverse section, HE: a hollow airway lined by respiratory epithelium resting on a conspicuously thick basement membrane, with seromucous glands in the submucosa and a series of C-shaped hyaline cartilage rings that are open posteriorly and closed there by the trachealis muscle. The C-shape is visible only because the section is transverse.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Organ level, transverse plane. The trachea is a 10–12 cm tube with a lumen about 2 cm wide, continuous with the larynx above and dividing into the two main bronchi at the level of the fourth thoracic vertebra. Its wall contains 15–20 pieces of horseshoe-shaped hyaline cartilage, and the plane of section is what allows the horseshoe to be seen as a C.

2.Low-power architecture

A lumen lined by folded mucosa; a pale, thick layer of glands; and then the cartilage, seen as a basophilic C-shaped plate with its opening directed posteriorly. Across the opening lies the membranous wall, in which smooth muscle and elastic fibres replace the cartilage. Outside, the adventitia blends with the surrounding connective tissue.

  • Because the right main bronchus is wider and less angled, inhaled foreign bodies and aspirated material enter it more often than the left.
  • The mucociliary apparatus (cilia plus mucus from goblet cells and glands) is the tracheal defence; smoking and repeated inflammation reduce the cilia and increase goblet cells, which is the histological basis of chronic bronchitis.

V.44Lung (HE)

Core answerThe slide is lung, HE: a spongy organ of alveoli — thin-walled air spaces separated by capillary-rich interalveolar septa — together with bronchioles, respiratory bronchioles and alveolar ducts, and a pleural surface covered by mesothelium. In the fields where alveoli dominate there is no cartilage and no gland, which is what separates lung from trachea and bronchus.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Organ level, a section through the parenchyma that should include at least one airway and, in favourable slides, the pleura. The conducting portion ends at the bronchiole; the respiratory portion begins at the respiratory bronchiole, where gas exchange starts, and continues through alveolar ducts and saccules to the alveoli.

2.Low-power architecture

The field is a "sponge": empty spaces of very different size. The largest tubes with folded, basophilic walls are bronchioles; the smallest round spaces are alveoli; between them run thin pink septa with capillaries. Where a vessel is cut, erythrocytes mark it. At the edge of the section the pleura appears as a thin line of mesothelium over a narrow layer of connective tissue.

3.High-power key features

  • Type I pneumocyte: extremely flattened cell; its nucleus is flat and its cytoplasm thins to a film over the capillary, covering most of the alveolar surface.
  • Type II pneumocyte: cuboidal cell bulging into the alveolar lumen, with vacuolated (lamellar) cytoplasm; it secretes surfactant and is the progenitor of type I cells.
  • Alveolar macrophage (dust cell): large, free or attached cell with phagocytosed particles; it is the lung's scavenger.
  • Interalveolar septum: a continuous capillary sandwiched between type I cells, with reticular and elastic fibres, fibroblasts and Kohn's pores that connect neighbouring alveoli.
  • Pleura: a single mesothelial layer with a thin submesothelial connective tissue, which contains elastic fibres — seen clearly on the orcein slide that follows.

4.The blood-gas barrier

Gas exchange happens where the septum is thinnest. The barrier, from air to blood, consists of surfactant, the cytoplasm of the type I pneumocyte, the fused basal laminae of pneumocyte and endothelium, and the endothelial cell of the continuous capillary. Its extreme thinness is the structural reason why oxygen and carbon dioxide equilibrate in a fraction of a second.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Empty alveolar spaces of different sizes separated by thin capillary-rich septa: lung parenchyma.
  • An airway with no cartilage and no glands = bronchiole, i.e. inside the lung.
  • Type II pneumocytes (cuboidal, bulging) and alveolar macrophages identify the alveolar wall at high power.
  • Pleura at the edge confirms the organ; anthracotic pigment is a normal finding in adults.
Differential diagnosis
  • Trachea or bronchus: cartilage is present — as C-shaped rings in the trachea, as irregular plates in a bronchus — so their presence excludes the purely alveolar part of the lung.
  • Oesophagus: a muscular tube with stratified squamous epithelium; no air spaces at all.
  • Adipose tissue: also "empty spaces", but they are round, of uniform size, each with a nucleus at the margin, and there are no septa with capillaries.
  • Emphysema: enlarged air spaces with destroyed septa — a pathological version of the same architecture, not a different organ.
  • Other stains of the same lung: HE shows architecture only; orcein and resorcin-fuchsin show the elastic skeleton (see V.45).
Clinical and functional note
  • Surfactant from type II pneumocytes lowers surface tension and keeps alveoli open; its deficiency in premature infants causes infant respiratory distress syndrome, and its destruction in ARDS damages the blood-gas barrier.
  • Emphysema destroys the elastic fibres of the septa, so the alveoli merge and lose their recoil, producing air trapping and a barrel chest.
  • Because the pulmonary circulation receives the whole cardiac output at low pressure, the alveolar capillaries are the site of gas exchange and also the first filter for emboli from the systemic veins.

V.45Lung (orcein + H)

Core answerThe same lung stained with orcein, which colours elastic fibres red-brown, and haematoxylin, which colours nuclei blue. The point of the slide is the elastic skeleton of the lung: elastic fibres in the interalveolar septa, at the mouths of the alveoli, along the bronchioles, around the vessels and, most obviously, in the pleura, whose continuous elastic layer is a reliable landmark.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Tissue level with a selective histochemical stain and a nuclear counterstain. It is the same organ as V.44, but the question it answers is different: not "what structures are here" but "where is the elastic tissue and how is it arranged".

How to identify it at the exam
  • Red-brown fibres forming a net around air spaces: an elastic stain of lung, not HE.
  • The continuous red-brown pleural band proves the organ is the lung and gives the surface for orientation.
  • Elastic fibres are densest at the alveolar mouths and in the bronchiole wall — exactly where mechanical stress is greatest.
  • Blue nuclei are still visible, so the stain can be orcein + H rather than a pure elastic preparation.
Differential diagnosis
  • HE lung (V.44): same architecture, but no red-brown fibres; the septa are simply pink.
  • Elastic aorta (V.32): elastic fibres also demonstrated, but they form dozens of parallel lamellae in a vessel wall, not a mesh around air spaces.
  • Elastic cartilage: orcein-positive too, but the elastic fibres lie in a chondromucoid matrix with chondrocytes in lacunae.
  • Collagen stain or trichrome: colours collagen, not elastin — the pattern would be a blue/green fibrous background instead of a discrete fibre net.
Clinical and functional note
  • Elastic fibres are the physical basis of the lung's elastic recoil: they store energy during inspiration and empty the lung passively during expiration.
  • In emphysema the elastic fibres of the septa are destroyed (proteases, smoking, alpha-1-antitrypsin deficiency), so the alveoli merge and the lung loses its recoil.
  • Ageing and chronic inflammation fragment pulmonary elastin, which is why the elastic stain is used in research and in the histological assessment of emphysema.

V.46Umbilical cord (HE)

Core answerThe slide is the umbilical cord (funiculus umbilicalis) in transverse section, HE: two umbilical arteries and one umbilical vein embedded in Wharton's jelly, a mucous connective tissue, and covered by a simple squamous-to-cuboidal amniotic epithelium. There are no nerves and no other structures in a normal cord.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Organ level, transverse section. The cord runs from the umbilicus of the fetus through the amniotic cavity to the fetal surface of the placenta; it develops as the body folds push the proximal yolk sac and allantois to its base. A mature cord is about 2 cm wide and about 50 cm long, roughly the length of the fetus.

2.Low-power architecture

A thin surface layer of amniotic epithelium encloses a large field of pale, myxoid Wharton's jelly. Inside it, three vessels stand out: two arteries with thick, deeply eosinophilic walls and small irregular lumens, and one vein with a much thinner wall and a large, often collapsed, oval lumen. No cartilage, no muscle bundles and no glands are present. Small remnants of the omphaloenteric or allantoic duct may be seen in some cords.

3.High-power key features

  • Umbilical artery: a very thick media of smooth muscle, but the wall is remarkable for a negative feature — it contains no elastic membranes, which is unusual for an artery of this size and is a useful discriminator in favour of the umbilical cord.
  • Umbilical vein: thin media, wide lumen, frequently folded on itself; no valves.
  • Wharton's jelly: pale, myxoid, almost empty-looking matrix with scattered spindle-shaped fibroblasts and macrophages; there are no adipocytes and no dense collagen bundles.
  • Amniotic epithelium: one layer of flat to cuboidal cells; no cilia, no goblet cells, no keratin.

4.Reading the colours

Wharton's jelly is pale blue-grey because its ground substance is basophilic and its cells are sparse; the smooth muscle of the vessels is strongly eosinophilic; the amniotic epithelium is a thin pink line with blue nuclei. The contrast between a mucoid, cell-poor stroma and two thick muscular arteries is the picture to memorise.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Two arteries and one vein in a myxoid stroma, covered by a single epithelial layer: umbilical cord.
  • Wharton's jelly — mucous connective tissue with almost no cells — replaces ordinary connective tissue.
  • No nerves, no skeletal muscle, no duct with a thick muscle coat: the cord is only vessels plus jelly.
  • The umbilical arteries lack elastic membranes, which distinguishes them from systemic muscular arteries.
Differential diagnosis
  • Spermatic cord (V.35): contains the ductus deferens (tiny lumen, very thick three-layered muscle coat, pseudostratified epithelium), a pampiniform venous plexus, striated cremaster muscle and fascial coverings — none of which is present here.
  • Placenta (V.47): chorionic villi with trophoblast and intervillous maternal blood; there is no villus here.
  • Artery and vein slide (V.33): two systemic vessels with internal elastic membranes and a connective tissue stroma, not three vessels in Wharton's jelly.
  • Mucoid connective tissue elsewhere (e.g. vitreous): has no amniotic epithelium and no paired arteries.
Clinical and functional note
  • A single umbilical artery (two-vessel cord) is the commonest congenital anomaly of the cord and is associated with other fetal anomalies, so it prompts further investigation.
  • Wharton's jelly cushions the vessels against compression, which is why cord entanglement and even true knots often cause no harm; when compression does occur, the thinner-walled vein is compressed before the arteries.
  • The cord is the route for cord blood collection (a source of stem cells) and for fetal blood sampling; cord prolapse is an obstetric emergency because it compresses the vessels.

V.47Placenta (HE)

Core answerThe slide is the placenta, HE: a fetal part — the chorionic plate with its chorionic villi — and a maternal part — the decidual plate — with intervillous spaces between the villi filled with maternal blood. Each villus has a core of fetal connective tissue with capillaries and Hofbauer cells and is covered by syncytiotrophoblast, beneath which a cytotrophoblast layer persists during the first half of pregnancy.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Organ level, a section through both parts. The human placenta is haemochorionic: maternal blood in the intervillous space is in direct contact with the syncytiotrophoblast, so only the fetal tissues of the villus separate the two circulations. The section should include the chorionic plate, villi and the decidual plate, which together show the whole architecture.

2.Low-power architecture

On one side lies the chorionic plate — connective tissue with large fetal vessels, covered by amnion towards the amniotic cavity. From it, branching villi project into the intervillous space, which is filled with maternal blood. On the other side is the decidual plate, from which placental septa grow towards the fetal side but do not reach it, so that they divide the placenta into lobes (cotyledons) without completely separating them.

3.High-power key features

  • Syncytiotrophoblast: a continuous multinucleated eosinophilic layer with a brush border on the villus surface; it is the interface with maternal blood and the source of hCG and other placental hormones.
  • Cytotrophoblast (Langhans cells): a single cuboidal layer beneath the syncytium, continuous only in the first half of pregnancy and important for regeneration.
  • Villus core: loose, jelly-like connective tissue with fetal capillaries, fibroblasts, myofibroblasts and Hofbauer cells — specialised macrophages that regulate local immune reactions and vascular growth.
  • Fibrinoid: amorphous, strongly eosinophilic material on and around villi (Langhans', Rohr's and Nitabuch's fibrinoid), which supports the villi and seals damaged surfaces.
  • Maturation: early villi are short and wide with few vessels; at term they are long, thin and richly branched, with capillaries lying directly under the syncytiotrophoblast.

4.The placental barrier

From fetal blood to maternal blood the barrier consists, in order, of the endothelium of the fetal capillary, its basal lamina, the connective tissue core of the villus, the basal lamina of the trophoblast, the cytotrophoblast and the syncytiotrophoblast. In the second half of pregnancy the cytotrophoblast becomes discontinuous and the basal laminae fuse, so the barrier becomes thinner and exchange more efficient — at the price of easier transplacental passage of drugs, viruses and antibodies.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Villi with a double trophoblast cover, a cellular core and fetal capillaries, floating in maternal blood: placenta.
  • Hofbauer cells in the villus core and fibrinoid on the villus surface are the two specific details.
  • Two parts must be named: the fetal chorionic plate with villi and the maternal decidual plate with septa.
  • The intervillous space has no endothelial lining — that is what "haemochorionic" means.
Differential diagnosis
  • Umbilical cord (V.46): three vessels in Wharton's jelly with an amniotic epithelial surface; no villi and no intervillous space.
  • Uterine endometrium: glands, spiral arteries and stroma; no trophoblast and no villi.
  • Amnion and chorion laeve: membranes without villi; the amnion is a simple epithelium on connective tissue.
  • Hydatidiform mole: oedematous, avascular villi with trophoblast proliferation — a pathological exaggeration of the normal villous architecture.
Clinical and functional note
  • The placenta performs the functions of lung, gut, kidney and endocrine gland for the fetus; its syncytiotrophoblast secretes hCG, progesterone, oestrogens and chorionic somatomammotropin.
  • The barrier is selective, not absolute: IgG crosses by receptor-mediated endocytosis (the basis of passive immunity and of Rh sensitisation), and many drugs, viruses and bacteria cross it too.
  • Defective remodelling of the maternal spiral arteries and placental insufficiency underlie pre-eclampsia and intrauterine growth restriction; an abnormally adherent or deeply invasive placenta (praevia, accreta) causes severe obstetric haemorrhage.

V.48Chicken embryo (HE)

Core answerThe slide is a transverse section through the body of a chicken embryo at the somite stage, HE: a whole-body cross-section in which the three germ layers are present at once and the axial organs lie in a fixed order — neural tube dorsally in the midline, notochord immediately ventral to it, somites flanking them, the dorsal aortae and the endodermal gut tube below, and the intraembryonic coelom between the somatic and splanchnic layers of the lateral plate mesoderm.

1.Level of organisation and plane

Whole-embryo level, transverse plane. The chick embryo is the classical model because it develops as a flat disc on the surface of the yolk, so a single cross-section contains the complete body wall, the neural tube and the gut at the same time. The plane cuts across the body axis, which is why the organs are arranged symmetrically around the midline.

2.Low-power architecture: find the axis first

Orientate by finding the neural tube at the midline dorsally; below it lies the notochord, and lateral to these the paired somites. Further ventrally are the paired dorsal aortae and the gut tube lined by endoderm. The coelom is the clear space between the somatic (body-wall) and splanchnic (gut-associated) layers of the lateral plate mesoderm. The surface is covered by a single layer of ectoderm.

3.High-power key features

  • Neural tube: a thick wall of pseudostratified neuroepithelium around a narrow neural canal. The sulcus limitans divides its wall into a dorsal alar plate (sensory) and a ventral basal plate (motor).
  • Notochord: a solid rod of cells immediately ventral to the neural tube, enclosed in a sheath; it is the embryonic inducer of the neural plate and the axial skeleton.
  • Somite: a compact, rounded mass of paraxial mesoderm lateral to the neural tube, with an epithelium-like surface; it is the segmental unit of the body.
  • Dorsal aortae: small paired vessels lined by endothelium, ventral to the somites and lateral to the gut; blood cells may be visible inside the lumen.
  • Gut tube: a tube lined by endoderm, at first a flat sheet that has folded into a tube; it lies ventral to the notochord.

4.Reading the colours

Neuroepithelium and other densely cellular tissues are strongly basophilic because nuclei are packed; mesenchyme is pale and loosely cellular; endoderm forms a neat cuboidal-to-columnar lining; erythrocytes and blood islands may be eosinophilic. The key is not colour but topography: the structures are always in the same order from dorsal to ventral, so the answer is an ordered list, not a search.

How to identify it at the exam
  • Find the neural tube at the midline; the notochord is immediately ventral to it and the somites are lateral to both.
  • All three germ layers must be identifiable: ectoderm on the surface, mesoderm between, endoderm lining the gut tube.
  • The presence of a notochord excludes adult central nervous tissue, and the presence of a gut tube plus somites excludes a placental or cord section.
  • The section is whole-body: skin and body wall, gut and neural axis are present in one field.
Differential diagnosis
  • Adult spinal cord section: a neural tube-like structure, but there is no notochord, no somites, no surface ectoderm, and the cord is surrounded by meninges, bone and muscle.
  • Umbilical cord or placenta (V.46, V.47): extraembryonic structures; no neural tube, no notochord, no somites.
  • Another level of the same embryo: more cranial sections show the pharyngeal arches and heart, more caudal ones the tail bud; the axial organs are the constant features.
  • Teratoma: contains all three germ layers but disorganised, without an axis, a neural tube or a notochord.
Clinical and functional note
  • Failure of neural tube closure produces anencephaly (cranial) or spina bifida (caudal); folic acid supplementation reduces the risk.
  • Notochord remnants can give rise to chordoma, a tumour of the clivus and sacrum — the notochord itself normally persists only as the nucleus pulposus.
  • Because each somite forms a vertebra, a muscle block and a dermal segment, errors in segmentation produce vertebral and muscular anomalies, and the segmental origin explains the arrangement of spinal nerves, muscles and dermatomes in the adult.
