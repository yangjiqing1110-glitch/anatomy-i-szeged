III.1The vertebral column: gross anatomy, syndesmology and X-ray anatomy.

Core answerThe vertebral column is the segmented axial skeleton of the trunk: about 33 vertebrae — 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 fused sacral and 3–4 coccygeal — united by intervertebral symphyses (discs), zygapophysial synovial joints and a continuous system of ligaments, and arranged in four curvatures (cervical lordosis, thoracic kyphosis, lumbar lordosis, sacral kyphosis). A typical vertebra is a weight-bearing body plus an arch that encloses the vertebral foramen. The syndesmology — discs, zygapophysial joints, anterior and posterior longitudinal, flaval, interspinous, supraspinous and intertransverse ligaments — explains why the column is stiff in the thorax and mobile in the cervical and lumbar regions, and the same elements form the checklist of its X-ray anatomy: alignment, body height, disc spaces, pedicles, intervertebral foramina and facet joints.

1.Number, regions and curvatures

The column extends from the skull to the coccyx and is the axis of support of the trunk: it transmits body weight through the sacro-iliac joints to the pelvis and lower limbs, carries and positions the head, and gives attachment to the muscles that move the trunk and limbs. The vertebrae are segmental, and each region carries the imprint of its function — mobility in the neck, a rib-bearing stability in the thorax, weight transmission in the lumbar region.

Seen from the side the column shows four curvatures. The cervical lordosis and lumbar lordosis are secondary curvatures (concave posteriorly): they appear after birth when the infant lifts the head and begins to stand. The thoracic kyphosis and sacral kyphosis are primary curvatures (concave anteriorly) and are retained from the C-shaped embryonic spine. The secondary curvatures bring the line of gravity into a vertical line, so the upright posture is maintained with the least muscular effort, and the alternating curves increase elasticity and spread the load. Abnormal curvatures are hyperlordosis, hyperkyphosis, scoliosis (a lateral curvature in the frontal plane, usually with rotation of the vertebrae) and gibbus (an angular kyphotic prominence).

3.Syndesmology: the joints of the column

A typical vertebra has six joints with its neighbours: four synovial (two above, two below) and two symphyses (one above, one below), each symphysis containing an intervertebral disc (discus intervertebralis). Movement between two vertebrae is small, but the effects are additive along the column.

The intervertebral disc fills the gap between the hyaline cartilage plates covering the vertebral bodies. Its outer part, the annulus fibrosus, is fibrocartilage arranged in concentric lamellae and is continuous with the anterior and posterior longitudinal ligaments; its centre, the nucleus pulposus, is mucoid in the young, rich in water and proteoglycans, and becomes more fibrous with age. Discs extend from C2 to the sacrum (including L5–S1), are thinnest in the thoracic and thickest in the lumbar region, and are avascular — they are nourished by diffusion and carry nerve fibres only peripherally.

For trauma the column is divided into three clinical columns: anterior (vertebral bodies and anterior longitudinal ligament), middle (vertebral bodies and posterior longitudinal ligament) and posterior (ligamenta flava, interspinous and supraspinous ligaments, plus the ligamentum nuchae in the neck). Injury of one column is usually stable; injury of two or more is usually unstable and needs fixation. At the craniocervical junction the ligaments are so critical that disruption risks brainstem and cord injury.

5.Movements and their regional distribution

The column flexes, extends, laterally flexes, rotates and circumducts. The range in each region follows the orientation of the zygapophysial facets:

  • Cervical: facets almost horizontal — maximum mobility, and the first two vertebrae are specialised (III.2) so that nodding occurs at the atlanto-occipital and rotation at the atlanto-axial joints.
  • Thoracic: facets approximately in the coronal plane and the ribs splint the region — flexion and extension are limited, rotation is relatively free.
  • Lumbar: facets approximately sagittal and interlocking — flexion and extension are the main movements, rotation is restricted.

Extension is limited by the anterior longitudinal ligament and by the approximation of spinous processes; flexion by the posterior longitudinal ligament, ligamenta flava, interspinous and supraspinous ligaments and the capsules. Rotation is checked by the alar ligaments at the top of the column and by the orientation of the facets below.

6.X-ray anatomy of the vertebral column

Plain radiographs remain the first examination, and the examiner expects a systematic reading: alignment, bone (body height, endplates, pedicles, processes), cartilage (disc spaces), and soft tissue. CT best shows fractures and bone destruction; MRI shows the cord, dural sac, subarachnoid space, discs and nuclei pulposi, the ligamenta flava and the extradural space.

Must-memorise
  • Counts: 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral, 3–4 coccygeal; 31 pairs of spinal nerves (8 C, 12 T, 5 L, 5 S, 1 Co).
  • A typical vertebra has six joints with its neighbours: 4 zygapophysial (synovial) + 2 symphyses (discs).

Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Disc herniation: an annular tear allows nuclear material to protrude, usually posterolaterally, into the intervertebral foramen, compressing the spinal nerve root; in the cervical region protrusions often ossify into disc-osteophyte bars. Diagnosis is by MRI, and the level must be identified before discectomy/laminectomy.
  • Vertebral fractures: the three-column concept decides stability. Wedge (crush) fractures of osteoporotic vertebrae occur in the thoracolumbar region and are treated by vertebroplasty or kyphoplasty — cement is injected through the pedicle under X-ray guidance.
  • Spondylolysis and spondylolisthesis: a stress fracture of the pars interarticularis (most often at LIV–LV) lets the body slip forwards.
  • Spina bifida: failure of fusion of the vertebral arches. Occulta (defect of LV or SI, asymptomatic, sometimes a tuft of hair) and the severe form with meningocele or myelomeningocele and neurological deficit.
  • Metastases are common in vertebral bodies and may cause collapse or extrude into the canal; sacralisation of LV and lumbarisation of SI alter the counting of levels; fused cervical vertebrae occur in Klippel–Feil syndrome.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Why does the vertebral body increase in size from CII to LV? — The load increases caudally, and the trabeculae and body adapt to it.
  • Why do discs herniate posterolaterally? — The posterior longitudinal ligament is narrow and weak laterally, and the annulus is thinnest behind.
  • How do you identify LV on an MRI? — By the iliolumbar ligament running from its transverse process to the iliac crest.
  • Where is a lumbar puncture performed and what does the needle traverse? — Between the spinous processes of the lumbar vertebrae (the arches do not overlap here): supraspinous and interspinous ligaments, ligamentum flavum, extradural space, dura and arachnoid, into the subarachnoid space.
  • What is the pars interarticularis? — The segment of the lamina between the superior and inferior zygapophysial processes; its fracture is seen on the oblique film as a break in the neck of the “Scottie dog”.

III.2Functional anatomy of the atlantooccipital and atlantoaxial joints.

Core answerTwo joint complexes connect the skull to the cervical spine. The atlanto-occipital joints are paired biaxial ellipsoid (condyloid) synovial joints between the convex occipital condyles and the slightly concave superior articular facets of the atlas; they produce nodding (flexion and extension). The atlanto-axial joints are the median atlanto-axial joint — a pivot between the dens and the anterior arch of the atlas, held by the transverse ligament — plus two lateral plane joints; together they act as a single pivot for rotation of the head. Stability depends on the transverse, alar, apical and cruciform ligaments and on the tectorial membrane, which is why their injury can be fatal.

1.Osteology of the atlas and the axis

The first two cervical vertebrae are so modified for the head that they are examined as a separate topic. The atlas (CI) is a ring with no body — the body of CI fuses with that of CII during development and becomes the dens — so there is no intervertebral disc between CI and CII. The axis (CII) carries the tooth-like dens upwards from its body.

2.The atlanto-occipital joints

The two atlanto-occipital joints (articulatio atlantooccipitalis) are formed by the convex occipital condyles and the slightly concave superior articular facets of the atlas. Each is a biaxial ellipsoid (condyloid) synovial joint with its own capsule. They are reinforced by the anterior and posterior atlanto-occipital membranes, which are the upward continuations of the anterior longitudinal ligament and of the ligamentum flavum respectively.

Function. Because the condyles are convex and the facets concave, movement is essentially flexion and extension — the nodding movement of the head — with a small amount of lateral flexion; rotation is negligible here because the capsule and the alar ligaments are tight. The two joints move together and share the movement with the cervical intervertebral joints, so nodding looks like a movement of the whole neck even though its specific joint is the atlanto-occipital.

3.The atlanto-axial joints: three joints, one pivot

The atlanto-axial complex consists of one median and two lateral joints. In the median atlanto-axial joint (articulatio atlantoaxialis mediana) the dens articulates anteriorly with the dental fovea of the anterior arch of the atlas and posteriorly with the transverse ligament of the atlas; two synovial cavities are therefore present. The lateral atlanto-axial joints are plane synovial joints between the inferior articular facets of the atlas and the superior articular facets of the axis.

Together the three joints behave as a pivot: the atlas, carrying the skull, rotates around the vertical axis of the dens. The dens is the pivot and the transverse ligament is the restraint that keeps it in place against the anterior arch.

5.Functional anatomy: how the movement is shared

The functional division of labour is the point of the topic: the atlanto-occipital joints are for nodding, the atlanto-axial joints for turning. Flexion and extension of the head occur largely at the atlanto-occipital joints, and rotation of the head occurs as the atlas and the attached skull rotate on the axis. In practice the two complexes work together — turning the head is accompanied by slight extension and lateral flexion, and nodding is accompanied by small rotations — and they are assisted by the suboccipital muscles (rectus capitis posterior major and minor, obliquus capitis superior and inferior), which extend and rotate the head and are all supplied by the posterior ramus of C1.

Stability is therefore bought by bone shape plus ligaments, not by muscles: the transverse ligament resists anterior translation of the atlas, the alar ligaments limit rotation, the capsules and membranes limit the extremes of flexion and extension, and the tectorial membrane lies between these ligaments and the spinal cord. This is why a ligamentous injury here is far more serious than a sprain elsewhere in the spine.

6.Radiology and clinical anatomy

The routine film is the open-mouth anteroposterior (odontoid) view, which shows the dens between the lateral masses of the atlas and the symmetry of the two lateral atlanto-axial joints, together with the lateral view, on which the relationship between the anterior arch of the atlas and the dens (the atlantodental interval) is measured — widening indicates failure of the transverse ligament. CT defines fractures; MRI shows the ligaments, the tectorial membrane and the spinal cord; flexion–extension films demonstrate instability.

Must-memorise
  • Atlanto-occipital = biaxial ellipsoid synovial joint, paired, between occipital condyles and the superior facets of the atlas; nodding.
  • Atlanto-axial = median pivot (dens + anterior arch + transverse ligament) + two lateral plane joints; rotation.
  • The transverse ligament holds the dens; the alar ligaments limit rotation; the apical ligament is minor; the tectorial membrane is the upper posterior longitudinal ligament.
  • No intervertebral disc between CI and CII; the body of CI becomes the dens of CII.
  • Suboccipital muscles = posterior ramus of C1.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Jefferson (burst) fracture of the atlas: axial loading (diving into shallow water, head hitting the roof of a car) breaks the closed ring at more than one site — seen on CT.
  • Hangman's fracture: hyperextension with distraction fractures the pedicles of CII with spondylolisthesis of C2 on C3.
  • Dens fracture: hyperextension or flexion may fracture the tip or base of the dens or the body of the axis; the dens fragment can move with the atlas and compress the cord.
  • Transverse ligament failure (trauma, rheumatoid arthritis, laxity syndromes) allows atlanto-axial subluxation with a widened atlantodental interval; the cord and brainstem are at risk.
  • Craniocervical instability carries a high risk of cord injury: quadriplegia, respiratory compromise from phrenic paralysis (C3–C5) and hypotension from interruption of the sympathetic outflow.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Why is there no disc between CI and CII? — The body of CI has fused with that of CII to form the dens.
  • Which ligament would you test in a patient with suspected atlanto-axial instability? — The transverse ligament, indirectly by the atlantodental interval on the lateral film; MRI shows it directly.
  • Which joint produces “yes” and which produces “no”? — Atlanto-occipital: nodding; atlanto-axial: rotation.
  • What are the contents of the suboccipital triangle and its boundaries? — Rectus capitis posterior major and obliquus capitis superior and inferior bound it; it contains the vertebral artery and the posterior ramus of C1.
  • Why is the open-mouth view needed? — The dens lies behind the anterior arch of the atlas and is obscured by the mandible and maxilla on the standard AP film.

III.3The bones and joints of the thorax. The movements of respiration.

Core answerThe bony thorax is built of 12 thoracic vertebrae, 12 pairs of ribs (costae) with costal cartilages (cartilagines costales) and the sternum (sternum). The superior thoracic aperture (TI, rib I, manubrium) is open to the neck; the inferior thoracic aperture (TXII, ribs XI and XII, costal margin, xiphoid process) is closed by the diaphragm. Ribs articulate with the vertebrae at the costovertebral joints (joint of the head of the rib and costotransverse joint), with the sternum at the sternocostal joints, and with each other at the interchondral joints. Respiration changes thoracic volume in three dimensions: vertically by descent of the diaphragm, anteroposteriorly by the pump-handle movement of the ribs and sternum, and transversely by the bucket-handle movement of the rib shafts.

1.The bones of the thorax: the ribs

There are 12 pairs of ribs, each ending anteriorly in a costal cartilage. The upper seven are true ribs because their cartilages reach the sternum; ribs VIII–X are false ribs whose cartilages join the cartilage of the rib above; ribs XI and XII are floating ribs with no anterior connection. The costal cartilages give the wall its mobility and elasticity.

A typical rib has a curved shaft with an anterior and a posterior end. The posterior end carries a head (with two articular facets separated by a crest), a neck and a tubercle; the tubercle has an articular part with an oval facet for the transverse process and a rough non-articular part for ligaments. The shaft bends at the angle and bears the costal groove on its inferior, internal surface, which protects the intercostal vessels and nerve.

2.The sternum and the thoracic apertures

The adult sternum has three parts. The manubrium bears the jugular (suprasternal) notch, the clavicular fossae, a facet for the first costal cartilage and a demifacet for the upper half of the second. The body has a demifacet for the lower half of the second cartilage, facets for the third to sixth cartilages and a demifacet for the seventh; its anterior surface shows transverse ridges, the fused sternebrae. The xiphoid process is variable in shape, begins as cartilage, ossifies in the adult and carries demifacets for the seventh costal cartilages.

The junction of the manubrium and the body is the manubriosternal symphysis, felt as the sternal angle. It lies at the level of the intervertebral disc between TIV and TV and is the most useful landmark of the thorax: the second costal cartilage articulates here, the plane separates the superior from the inferior mediastinum, marks the superior border of the pericardium, the end of the ascending aorta and beginning of the arch, the end of the arch and beginning of the thoracic aorta, and the bifurcation of the trachea. Because rib I is hidden below the clavicle, ribs are counted from rib II at the sternal angle.

The superior thoracic aperture is bounded by vertebra TI, the first ribs and the manubrium and is open to the neck. The inferior thoracic aperture is bounded by vertebra TXII, ribs XII and XI, the costal margin and the xiphoid process, and is closed by the diaphragm. The angle between the two costal margins at the xiphoid is the infrasternal (subcostal) angle.

3.The joints of the thoracic cage

Each rib is joined at its two ends and its cartilage is joined to its neighbours, so the cage is a deformable but continuous unit. Two synovial joints make up the costovertebral articulation of a typical rib: the joint of the head and the costotransverse joint.

4.Movements of respiration: pump-handle and bucket-handle

Breathing changes three diameters of the thorax. The vertical diameter increases when the diaphragm contracts and its domes flatten. The anteroposterior and transverse diameters change when the ribs are elevated, by two different mechanisms that the examiner always asks for by name.

Pump-handle movement. The anterior ends of the ribs are lower than their posterior ends. When the ribs are elevated, their anterior ends and the sternum move upwards and forwards, the angle between the manubrium and the body of the sternum may open slightly, and the anteroposterior diameter of the thorax increases — the ribs work like the handle of a pump. Depression reverses the movement, carrying the sternum downwards and backwards.

Bucket-handle movement. The shafts of the ribs are curved so that their middles lie lower than their two ends. When the shafts are elevated, their middles move laterally, increasing the transverse diameter as the handles of a bucket move outwards when lifted.

Quiet inspiration: diaphragm contracts and domes descend → external (and intercartilaginous internal) intercostals elevate the ribs → thoracic volume rises → intrathoracic pressure falls → air enters and venous return improves. Quiet expiration is passive: elastic recoil of lung and chest wall.

5.The muscles of respiration

  • Inspiration. The diaphragm is the chief muscle of quiet inspiration, assisted by the external intercostals and the intercartilaginous part of the internal intercostals, which elevate the ribs. In forced inspiration the scalene muscles fix and elevate the upper ribs, with sternocleidomastoid, levatores costarum, serratus posterior superior, pectoralis major and minor and serratus anterior.
  • Expiration. Quiet expiration needs no muscle. Forced expiration uses the internal intercostals (interosseous part), transversus thoracis, subcostales, serratus posterior inferior and the abdominal wall muscles, which raise intra-abdominal pressure and push the relaxed diaphragm back into the thorax.
  • Fixation. Muscles of the neck and abdomen can fix the upper or lower ribs, so that the action of the intercostals produces movement instead of a futile pull on a mobile cage.

In deep inspiration the transverse and anteroposterior diameters, and the infrasternal angle, all increase; the downward movement of the diaphragm adds the vertical component. The ribs are also the site of attachment of muscles of the upper limb, which is why a shoulder girdle fixed by its muscles can assist forced inspiration.

6.Radiology and clinical anatomy of the thoracic cage

  • Rib fracture. A single fracture is painful but of little consequence; fractures of several ribs in two places produce a flail segment, which moves paradoxically (inwards during inspiration) and may require assisted ventilation.
  • Cervical rib. An accessory rib articulating with CVII, present in about 1 % of people; its fibrous band may compress the lower brachial plexus and produce thoracic outlet syndrome.
  • Sternal puncture reaches the marrow cavity of the body of the sternum for bone marrow aspiration; median sternotomy and lateral thoracotomy are the standard surgical approaches to the chest.
  • Chest drainage is placed in the fourth or fifth intercostal space between the anterior and midaxillary lines, immediately above the superior border of the rib below, because the neurovascular bundle lies against the inferior border of the rib above (III.5).
  • Chest radiograph. Ribs are counted from rib II at the sternal angle; costal cartilage calcification increases with age; the sternal angle marks the level of the aortic arch and tracheal bifurcation.

  • What are the attachments of the intra-articular ligament of the head of the rib? — From the crest of the head to the intervertebral disc, dividing the joint into two synovial cavities.
  • Which ribs move mainly with pump-handle and which with bucket-handle? — The upper ribs and sternum dominate the pump-handle (AP) movement, the lower ribs the bucket-handle (transverse) movement.
  • Which muscle is the chief muscle of quiet inspiration? — The diaphragm; the external intercostals assist.
  • What happens to the infrasternal angle in deep inspiration? — It increases as the costal margins rise and the thorax widens.

III.4The diaphragm.

Core answerThe diaphragm (diaphragma) is a thin musculotendinous sheet that fills the inferior thoracic aperture and separates the thoracic from the abdominal cavity. It has a central central tendon and three groups of muscular fibres — sternal, costal and lumbar — the lumbar part consisting of the two crura and the median, medial and lateral arcuate ligaments. It has three large openings: the caval opening at TVIII, the oesophageal hiatus at TX and the aortic hiatus at TXII. It is the chief muscle of inspiration, is supplied almost entirely by the phrenic nerves (C3–C5), and its paralysis or herniation is of major clinical importance.

1.Form, position and domes

The diaphragm has two asymmetric domes. On the right the dome is produced by the liver, with contributions from the right kidney and suprarenal gland; on the left by the fundus of the stomach and spleen, with contributions from the left kidney and suprarenal gland. In normal expiration a reasonable estimate places the right dome at the level of rib V and the left dome at the fifth intercostal space — a useful guide when percussing the thorax. The domes are flattened centrally where the fibrous pericardium is attached to the central tendon, and they ascend and descend with breathing.

In the median plane the diaphragm slopes inferiorly from its anterior attachment to the xiphoid (approximately TVIII/IX) to its posterior attachment at the median arcuate ligament, crossing anteriorly to the aorta at about TXII. The peripheral attachments of the diaphragm are the xiphoid process, the costal margin, the ends of ribs XI and XII, the ligaments across the posterior abdominal wall and the lumbar vertebrae.

4.Innervation

The phrenic nerves (C3, C4, C5) provide the entire motor supply and sensory fibres to the central part of the diaphragm, including the pericardium and the mediastinal and diaphragmatic pleura. They descend through the thorax between the mediastinal pleura and the pericardium, pierce the diaphragm and branch on its abdominal surface. The right phrenic nerve accompanies the inferior vena cava through the caval opening; the left passes through the muscle on its own.

The peripheral part of the diaphragm receives additional sensory fibres from the lower intercostal nerves. This double sensory supply explains the two patterns of referred pain: irritation of the central diaphragm is felt in the shoulder region (C3–C5 dermatomes), whereas irritation of the peripheral diaphragm is felt over the lower thoracic or abdominal wall.

6.Function, radiology and clinical anatomy

Contraction of the muscle fibres flattens the domes and draws the central tendon inferiorly. This increases the vertical diameter of the thorax, raises thoracic volume and lowers intrathoracic pressure, so that air enters the lungs and venous return to the heart is enhanced. The same contraction raises intra-abdominal pressure and thus assists coughing, micturition, defecation and parturition — the abdominal press. On a chest radiograph the domes are seen as curved shadows; the right is normally higher than the left because of the liver, and the costophrenic angles should be sharp. On coronal CT the crura, the arcuate ligaments and the three openings are identifiable.

Clinical / radiological correlation

  • Congenital hernias occur where the embryonic components failed to fuse: Morgagni hernia between the xiphoid and the right costal margin, and Bochdalek hernia through the left posterolateral defect. Bowel in the thorax compresses the lungs and may cause pulmonary hypoplasia.

  • Referred pain: central diaphragmatic irritation (for example blood from a ruptured spleen) is referred to the shoulder tip through the phrenic nerve (C3–C5).

  • What are the boundaries of the lumbocostal triangle? — Between the costal and lumbar parts, over the upper part of quadratus lumborum; a site of congenital hernia.

III.5The muscles and layers of the thoracic wall. The intercostal space.

Core answerFrom the surface inwards the thoracic wall consists of skin, superficial fascia (with the breast), deep pectoral fascia, a muscular layer, endothoracic fascia and parietal pleura. The muscular layer contains the three intercostal muscles (external, internal, innermost), the subcostales and transversus thoracis, reinforced by the levatores costarum, serratus posterior and the pectoral and serratus anterior muscles. Each intercostal space contains the three muscle layers and, in the costal groove between the internal and innermost layers, the intercostal vein, artery and nerve in that order from above downwards — the nerve, lying most inferiorly, is the structure most at risk. The intercostal nerves are the anterior rami of T1–T11, with the subcostal nerve (T12) below rib XII.

1.The layers of the thoracic wall

The wall is built in layers, and surgical planes and spread of infection follow them. The superficial pectoral fascia surrounds the breast, separated from the pectoralis major by the retromammary space. The deep pectoral fascia has three parts: the fascia over pectoralis major, the clavipectoral fascia enclosing pectoralis minor and subclavius, and the thoracic fascia covering the external intercostals. Between the muscle layers lie tissue spaces — the subpectoral space (which contains the interpectoral lymph nodes) and the retromammary space — and laterally the pectoral and clavipectoral fasciae merge into the axillary fascia.

3.The intercostal space: contents and the neurovascular order

There are eleven intercostal spaces (spatia intercostalia). Each contains the three muscle layers and, in the costal groove of the rib above, the intercostal vessels and nerve. Three points are examined every year:

  • The neurovascular bundle lies in the plane between the internal and innermost intercostal muscles — the neurovascular plane.
  • In the groove the order from above downwards is vein, artery, nerve (VAN); the vein is highest, the nerve lowest.

4.Vessels of the thoracic wall

Blood supply forms a basket around the chest: posterior and anterior intercostal arteries anastomose within each space.

  • Posterior intercostal arteries. The upper two on each side arise from the supreme intercostal artery (a branch of the costocervical trunk of the subclavian artery); the remaining nine arise from the posterior surface of the thoracic aorta. Because the aorta lies on the left, the right-sided vessels cross the midline anterior to the vertebral bodies and are longer than the left.
  • Anterior intercostal arteries. Those of the upper six spaces arise from the internal thoracic artery, those of the lower spaces from its terminal musculophrenic branch. Each space usually receives two branches — one below the upper rib and one above the lower rib, where it meets a collateral branch of the posterior artery.
  • Internal thoracic artery. Arises from the subclavian artery, descends behind the costal cartilages of the upper six ribs about 1 cm lateral to the sternum, and ends at about the sixth intercostal space by dividing into the superior epigastric and musculophrenic arteries. Its perforating branches pass forwards with the anterior cutaneous nerves to supply the skin and breast.
  • Veins. Posterior intercostal veins drain to the azygos and hemi-azygos systems; anterior intercostal veins drain to the internal thoracic veins. The upper posterior intercostal veins on the left may join as the left superior intercostal vein (to the left brachiocephalic vein); on the right as the right superior intercostal vein (to the azygos vein).
  • Lymphatics. Drain to parasternal nodes along the internal thoracic vessels, to intercostal nodes near the heads and necks of the ribs, and to diaphragmatic nodes; parasternal nodes drain to the bronchomediastinal trunks, upper intercostal nodes also to the bronchomediastinal trunks, and lower intercostal nodes to the thoracic duct.

5.Nerves of the thoracic wall

The intercostal nerves are the anterior rami of spinal nerves T1–T11 and lie in the intercostal spaces; the anterior ramus of T12 is the subcostal nerve and runs below rib XII. Each typical intercostal nerve gives a large lateral cutaneous branch (which divides into anterior and posterior branches for the overlying skin), a collateral branch near the lower rib, and ends as an anterior cutaneous branch that emerges parasternally or on the anterior abdominal wall. In the thorax the intercostal nerves carry somatic motor fibres to the muscles of the wall, somatic sensory fibres from the skin and parietal pleura, and postganglionic sympathetic fibres to the periphery; they communicate with the sympathetic trunk through rami communicantes.

Atypical nerves must be named: T1 contributes to the brachial plexus; the lateral cutaneous branch of T2 is the intercostobrachial nerve supplying the medial surface of the upper arm; the lower intercostal nerves supply the muscles, skin and peritoneum of the abdominal wall; and the skin of the upper chest is supplied by supraclavicular nerves from the cervical plexus.

6.Clinical and radiological notes

  • Chest drain (thoracostomy) insertion: in the fourth or fifth intercostal space between the anterior and midaxillary lines, entering just above the superior border of the rib below the space, to avoid the neurovascular bundle; complications include pneumothorax and bleeding.
  • Intercostal nerve block: local anaesthetic placed near the subcostal groove produces analgesia for chest trauma, thoracotomy, mastectomy or upper abdominal surgery; the pleura and intercostal vessels may be punctured.
  • Surgical access: median sternotomy gives access to the heart, great vessels and anterior mediastinum; lateral thoracotomy and video-assisted thoracoscopy enter through intercostal spaces and divide thoracic wall muscles.
  • Internal thoracic artery is used as a graft in coronary artery bypass surgery because of its rich anastomoses and resistance to atherosclerosis.
  • Rib fracture and flail chest (III.3): the intercostal muscles normally splint each space, and their loss of continuity allows paradoxical movement.

III.6The muscles of the posterior abdominal wall and the deep muscles of the back.

Core answerThe posterior abdominal wall is built on the lumbar vertebrae, the sacrum, ribs XI and XII and the iliac fossae, and is filled by the psoas major, psoas minor, iliacus and quadratus lumborum muscles, with the diaphragm above and the thoracolumbar fascia behind them. The deep (intrinsic) muscles of the back are arranged in layers — spinotransversales (splenius), erector spinae (iliocostalis, longissimus, spinalis), transversospinales (semispinalis, multifidus, rotatores), segmental muscles (interspinales, intertransversarii, levatores costarum) and the suboccipital group — and every one of them is supplied by the posterior rami of spinal nerves. They extend, laterally flex and rotate the vertebral column and head and maintain posture.

1.Bony and fascial framework

The midline of the posterior abdominal wall is formed by the bodies of the five lumbar vertebrae and their intervertebral discs; the discs complete the boundary, and the prominence of the region is due to the lumbar lordosis. Below them the upper margin of the sacrum continues the midline, and laterally the ilia join the sacrum at the sacro-iliac joints, their iliac fossae giving origin to iliacus. Superiorly, ribs XI and XII complete the bony frame; rib XII gives attachment to many muscles and ligaments. The diaphragm closes the superior boundary of the region.

Two fascial sheets dominate the region. The psoas fascia forms a sheath around the psoas major and is attached to the lumbar vertebrae and discs; the thoracolumbar fascia covers the deep muscles of the back and has three layers in the lumbar region.

3.The thoracolumbar fascia

In the lumbar region the thoracolumbar fascia (fascia thoracolumbalis) consists of three layers. The posterior layer is thick, attached to the spinous processes of the lumbar and sacral vertebrae and to the supraspinous ligament, and extends laterally to cover the erector spinae. The middle layer is attached medially to the tips of the lumbar transverse processes and intertransverse ligaments, inferiorly to the iliac crest and superiorly to the lower border of rib XII. The anterior layer covers the anterior surface of quadratus lumborum, is attached medially to the lumbar transverse processes, inferiorly to the iliac crest, and superiorly forms the lateral arcuate ligament for the diaphragm.

The posterior and middle layers unite at the lateral margin of the erector spinae; at the lateral border of quadratus lumborum the anterior layer joins them and forms the aponeurotic origin of transversus abdominis. In the thoracic region the same fascia covers the deep muscles and separates them from the superficial and intermediate groups, attaching medially to the thoracic spinous processes and laterally to the angles of the ribs. The intrinsic back muscles therefore occupy an osseofibrous compartment bounded by this fascia, the vertebral arches and the spinous and transverse processes.

4.Classification of the deep (intrinsic) muscles of the back

The back muscles are divided into extrinsic and intrinsic groups. The extrinsic muscles (trapezius, latissimus dorsi, rhomboids, levator scapulae — and the intermediate serratus posterior superior and inferior) move the upper limb or the ribs and are innervated by anterior rami of spinal nerves, except trapezius (accessory nerve). 6.Clinical and functional notes

  • Posterior rami lesions weaken the intrinsic muscles on one side and produce localised back pain; the posterior rami are also the route for facet-joint pain.
  • Lumbar triangle (of Petit) between the lateral border of latissimus dorsi, the posterior border of external oblique and the iliac crest is a weak point and a site for auscultation, percussion and renal examination.
  • Psoas abscess may present as a mass below the inguinal ligament; the psoas sheath is also a route for the spread of disc infection.
  • Scoliosis and posture depend on balanced action of the erector spinae and transversospinales; muscle imbalance (for example in muscular dystrophy) produces a structural curve.
  • Renal and lumbar examination uses the same muscles and fascia as landmarks: the lumbar region is examined for tenderness and percussion tenderness over the kidneys, and the deep muscles must be relaxed for the examination.

  • Lumbar disc herniation compresses the descending root in the intervertebral foramen; the deep muscles and fascia are divided in the surgical approach, which is why postoperative muscle weakness and pain are common.

  • Quadratus lumborum spasm produces deep loin pain and is a common cause of “low back pain” after prolonged sitting; it also stabilises the twelfth rib in inspiration.

Likely follow-up questions
  • Why is the psoas sheath clinically important? — It extends from the lumbar vertebrae and discs to the thigh, so disc infection can spread along it and present below the inguinal ligament.

  • What separates the intrinsic from the extrinsic back muscles? — The posterior layer of the thoracolumbar fascia; the intrinsic group is innervated by posterior rami.
  • Which is the deepest muscle of the back? — Rotatores (and the segmental intertransversarii/interspinales), lying deepest of the transversospinales.
  • Where does quadratus lumborum attach and what does it do? — From the iliac crest, iliolumbar ligament and transverse process of LV to rib XII and the transverse processes of LI–LIV; it depresses rib XII, stabilises the trunk and produces lateral bending.

III.7The surface projections of thoracic organs. Topography of the thoracic cavity, the divisions of the mediastinum.

Core answerThe thoracic cavity is divided into a right and a left pleural cavity and the mediastinum (mediastinum) between them. A transverse plane through the sternal angle and the TIV/TV intervertebral disc divides the mediastinum into a superior and an inferior mediastinum, and the inferior part is subdivided by the pericardial sac into anterior, middle and posterior mediastinum. The same palpable landmarks — jugular notch, sternal angle, costal margin, ribs, midclavicular and midaxillary lines — allow the heart (apex in the fifth intercostal space in the midclavicular line), the valves, the lungs and pleura and the mediastinal contents to be projected onto the chest wall, which is the basis of percussion, auscultation and safe chest drainage.

1.Topography of the thoracic cavity

The thorax is an irregular cylinder with a narrow superior thoracic aperture open to the neck and a large inferior thoracic aperture closed by the diaphragm. Inside, the wall encloses three compartments: the two pleural cavities, each around a lung, and the mediastinum. The pleural cavities are completely separated from each other by the mediastinum, so disease on one side does not automatically cross to the other, and the mediastinum can be entered surgically without opening the pleura. Both pleural cavities extend above rib I into the root of the neck, because the apices of the lungs project there.

The mediastinum is a thick, flexible, longitudinally oriented partition in the median sagittal plane. It contains the heart in its pericardial sac, the great vessels, the trachea and oesophagus, the thymus, nerves and lymphatics. It also works as a conduit: the oesophagus, vagus nerves and thoracic duct pass through it between neck and abdomen, the phrenic nerves pass through it to the diaphragm, and the trachea, thoracic aorta and superior vena cava run within it to and from thoracic organs. The lungs occupy the pleural cavities; each lung has an apex, three surfaces (costal, mediastinal and diaphragmatic) and a hilum where the root enters, with the mediastinal pleura reflecting onto the organ there.

2.Surface landmarks and reference lines

Projection of thoracic organs is only possible because a few bony points are palpable. The jugular notch lies at the top of the manubrium; the sternal angle is felt as a transverse ridge at the level of the TIV/TV disc; the xiphoid process, the costal margin and the infrasternal angle are palpable below; the second costal cartilage is identified immediately lateral to the sternal angle and is the starting point for counting ribs; the midclavicular line is drawn vertically through the middle of the clavicle, and the midaxillary line vertically through the middle of the axilla. The vertical reference lines used in the thorax are the anterior median, sternal, parasternal, midclavicular, anterior axillary, midaxillary and posterior axillary lines (plus the scapular and posterior median lines behind).

The sternal angle is the single most tested plane. At the level of the TIV/TV disc it marks: the articulation of the second costal cartilage with the sternum; the boundary between the superior and inferior mediastinum; the superior border of the pericardium; the end of the ascending aorta and beginning of the arch; the end of the arch and beginning of the thoracic aorta; and the bifurcation of the trachea, just superior to the pulmonary trunk.

6.Clinical use of the projections

  • Auscultation sites are not the anatomical projections: heart sounds are carried downstream with the blood, so each valve is heard where its jet strikes the chest wall — aortic right second intercostal space, pulmonary left second, tricuspid left lower sternal border, mitral at the apex.
  • Pleural effusion collects in the costodiaphragmatic recess and blunts the costophrenic angle; it is aspirated or drained posteriorly and inferiorly where the recess is deepest.
  • Pneumothorax removes the lung from the chest wall; the apex and the lateral chest wall are the first to show it because the lung does not normally fill these extremes.
  • Mediastinal widening on the chest film suggests lymphadenopathy, aortic disease or tumour; the divisions of the mediastinum localise the lesion.
  • Pericardial and cardiac borders are used to assess chamber enlargement, and the position of the apex indicates the cardiac silhouette in the fifth intercostal space.

Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Thoracocentesis and chest drainage are performed in the lower intercostal spaces (above the rib below) so as to enter the costodiaphragmatic recess without injuring the liver, spleen or diaphragm.
  • Auscultation is the practical test of the surface projections: the valve heard best in each point is downstream of the valve itself, which is why the pulmonary valve is heard at the left second space.
  • Mediastinal masses are localised by compartment: anterior (thymoma, thyroid, teratoma), middle (lymphadenopathy, aneurysm), posterior (neurogenic tumour, oesophageal lesion).
  • Pleural tapping should never be performed below the eighth rib in the midclavicular line or below the tenth rib in the midaxillary line, because the pleural cavity ends there and the needle would enter the abdomen.
  • Cardiac enlargement on the PA film changes the left and lower borders; the apex is displaced laterally and downwards in left ventricular enlargement.

III.8The anatomy, blood supply and lymphatic drainage of the female breast.

Core answerThe female breast (mamma) is a modified sweat gland lying in the superficial fascia over the pectoralis major, with its base extending from rib II to rib VI and from the sternum to the midaxillary line; an axillary tail projects into the axilla. It consists of 15–20 lobes drained by lactiferous ducts that open on the nipple, supported by the suspensory (Cooper's) ligaments and separated from the deep fascia by the retromammary space. Its blood supply is from the internal thoracic (medial), the axillary branches (lateral) and the second to fourth posterior intercostal arteries; its lymph drains mainly to the axillary nodes (about 75 %), the parasternal nodes for the medial part, and to intercostal and interpectoral nodes. The terminal duct lobular unit is the functional unit and the commonest site of origin of carcinoma.

1.Extent, position and form

The breast lies in the pectoral region, in the superficial fascia anterior to the pectoralis major and the anterior thoracic wall, resting also on serratus anterior and the external oblique. Its base extends vertically from rib II to rib VI and transversely from the sternum to as far laterally as the midaxillary line. A layer of loose connective tissue, the retromammary space, separates it from the deep fascia and gives it a degree of movement over the underlying muscles — a plane the surgeon uses. The axillary process (axillary tail) extends superolaterally around the lower margin of pectoralis major into the axilla, may perforate the deep fascia, and can reach as high as the apex of the axilla; it can be mistaken for an axillary mass.

The size and shape of the breast depend mainly on fat and on the state of the gland. In non-lactating women fat predominates; during lactation glandular tissue becomes abundant. The nipple (papilla mammaria) is surrounded by the pigmented areola, which bears the openings of the areolar glands (Montgomery). In men the breast is rudimentary, consisting only of small ducts that normally do not extend beyond the areola, but carcinoma can still occur.

2.Structure of the gland

The mammary gland is a branching tubulo-alveolar gland. Its lobes and lobules are embedded in a firm fibrofatty stroma, and the stroma condenses in places into the suspensory ligaments of the breast (Cooper's ligaments), which run from the gland to the dermis and support the breast.

6.Clinical and radiological anatomy

  • Carcinoma of the breast arises most often in the terminal duct lobular unit. Tumour traction on Cooper's ligaments produces skin retraction and pitting; obstruction of subcutaneous lymphatics produces the orange-peel (peau d'orange) appearance; extensive dermal infiltration may give the hard, woody skin of cancer en cuirasse.
  • Spread is by lymphatics, by veins and by direct invasion. Staging requires the size and site of the primary tumour, the number and sites of involved nodes and the organs of spread; CT, bone scanning and PET-CT are used to look for lung, liver and bone metastases.
  • Mastectomy removes breast tissue from the medial axillary wall, where the long thoracic nerve lies; injury paralyses serratus anterior and produces a winged scapula. The nerve to latissimus dorsi may also be damaged.
  • Mammography shows the Cooper's ligaments as a fibrous network; invasive ductal carcinoma appears as an irregular radiodense mass that distorts the architecture of the surrounding fat.
  • Male breast cancer is rare but occurs, because the rudimentary ducts are the same tissue; accessory nipples occur along the mammary ridge.

III.9The definition and structures of the superior mediastinum. Topography, developmental stages and histology of the thymus.

Core answerThe mediastinum is the median region of the thorax between the two pleural cavities; the transverse plane through the sternal angle and the intervertebral disc TIV/V divides it into a superior and an inferior mediastinum. The superior mediastinum contains the thymus, the right and left brachiocephalic veins, the superior vena cava, the arch of the aorta with its three branches, the trachea, the oesophagus, the thoracic duct and the phrenic and vagus nerves. The thymus (thymus) is the most anterior structure of this compartment – a bilobed, endodermally derived, encapsulated primary lymphoid organ in which T lymphocytes are educated; histologically it has a dark cortex and a pale medulla containing Hassall's corpuscles, and it undergoes fatty involution after puberty.

1.The mediastinum and the definition of its superior part

The mediastinum (mediastinum) extends from the posterior surface of the sternum anteriorly to the thoracic vertebrae posteriorly, and from the superior thoracic aperture to the diaphragm. A horizontal plane through the sternal angle and the disc between vertebrae TIV and TV separates the superior from the inferior mediastinum; the inferior part is further subdivided by the pericardium into the anterior, middle (pericardium and heart) and posterior mediastina. The same plane is a landmark for rib II, the superior limit of the pericardium, the beginning and the end of the arch of the aorta, the bifurcation of the trachea and the entry of the superior vena cava into the pericardial sac.

3.Topography of the thymus

In the adult the thymus is a bilobed, asymmetrical organ lying in the superior mediastinum immediately posterior to the manubrium of the sternum, in front of the left brachiocephalic vein, the arch of the aorta and the pericardium. Its upper poles may reach into the neck as high as the thyroid gland, while its lower part typically extends into the anterior mediastinum over the pericardial sac. Laterally it is related to the mediastinal pleura and to the phrenic nerves. It is a large organ in the child, begins to atrophy after puberty and in the elderly is barely identifiable, being represented by lobulated fatty tissue in which small islands of thymic tissue persist.

  • Arteries: small branches from the internal thoracic arteries; smaller twigs from the pericardiacophrenic and inferior thyroid vessels may contribute.
  • Veins: drain chiefly into the left brachiocephalic vein, sometimes into the internal thoracic veins.
  • Lymphatics: to the parasternal, tracheobronchial and root-of-neck lymph nodes; the thymus has only efferent lymphatic vessels.
  • Nerves: derived from the vagus and sympathetic plexuses accompanying the vessels.

4.Development of the thymus

The thymus is an endodermal organ. Its epithelial primordium arises from the third pharyngeal pouch on each side; the parathyroid glands develop from the same pharyngeal pouch region, which explains why ectopic parathyroid tissue is a common finding inside the thymus. The developmental stages are:

  1. Epithelial primordium: endodermal cells of the third pouch proliferate and form a paired, initially hollow epithelial cord that soon becomes solid and loses its connection with the pharyngeal wall.
  2. Descent: the primordia migrate caudally and medially with the developing heart and great vessels into the superior mediastinum, where the two lobes come to lie behind the manubrium of the sternum; the thymus thus has a cervical origin and a thoracic position.
  3. Colonisation by lymphocytes: lymphoid progenitor cells from the bone marrow enter the epithelial primordium and proliferate, converting it into a lymphoid organ; the epithelial cells become the epithelial reticular cells (epithelioreticulocyti) which form the stroma and present self-antigens.
  4. Compartmentalisation: the organ is divided into an outer dark cortex and an inner pale medulla; a capsule and connective-tissue septa grow in from the surrounding mesenchyme and divide the cortex into incomplete lobules (pseudolobules) that share a common medulla.
  5. Hassall's corpuscles (corpuscula thymica) differentiate in the medulla from concentric layers of epithelial reticular cells; their number and size increase with age.

The thymic stroma is unique: it is a reticular epithelium and not ordinary reticular connective tissue – apart from the developing tooth, no other organ has this type of stroma. A small population of myoid cells expressing skeletal-muscle proteins is also present; their function is unknown.

5.Histology of the thymus

5.1 General architecture

  • Capsule (capsula thymi): dense irregular collagenous connective tissue; septa (septa corticalia) penetrate the parenchyma and produce incomplete lobules, each with its own cortex but a medulla continuous with the neighbouring lobules.
  • Cortex (cortex thymi): dark on H&E because of densely packed thymocytes with large nuclei and scanty cytoplasm; mitoses are numerous in the subcapsular zone, and the cells mature as they move inwards. There are no lymphoid nodules in the thymus – the cortex is uniformly dark.
  • Corticomedullary junction: blurred boundary containing numerous vessels and high endothelial venules; this is where progenitors enter and mature T cells leave.
  • Medulla (medulla thymi): pale, because lymphocytes are fewer and epithelial reticular cells more abundant; contains mature T cells, dendritic cells, macrophages and Hassall's corpuscles.
  • Larger vessels and the only (efferent) lymphatic vessels lie in the septa; there are no afferent lymphatics – antigens do not reach the thymic parenchyma through lymph.

6.Blood-thymus barrier, T-cell education and involution

The blood-thymus barrier exists only in the cortex. It prevents circulating macromolecules and antigens from reaching developing T cells, so that the thymocytes meet only the self-antigens presented by epithelial reticular cells. It has five layers: (1) capillary endothelium with tight junctions, (2) capillary basal lamina, (3) a thin perivascular connective-tissue space containing macrophages, (4) the basal lamina of the reticular epithelium, and (5) the processes of type I epithelial reticular cells joined by occluding junctions. The medulla has no such barrier – T cells there have already been selected.

Education of T cells begins when bone-marrow progenitors enter the cortex as double-negative thymocytes (CD2 positive, CD4 and CD8 negative). They express a pre-T-cell receptor, proliferate and become double-positive (CD4+ CD8+). They are then tested in the cortex by epithelial reticular cells (positive selection): those that fail to recognise self-MHC–self-peptide complexes – about 90% – undergo apoptosis and are phagocytosed by cortical macrophages. Survivors become single-positive (CD4+ or CD8+) and move to the medulla, where dendritic cells and medullary epithelial cells challenge them with self-antigens; autoreactive cells are deleted (negative selection). In total well over 90% of thymocytes die; the survivors leave as naive T cells through the high endothelial venules and efferent lymphatics and seed the secondary lymphoid organs.

  • Involution: the thymus is relatively largest in the neonate and child; after puberty rising sex-hormone levels cause progressive atrophy, with adipose replacement of the cortex. It never disappears completely – islands of thymic tissue and epithelial reticular cells persist, and the organ can be reactivated in youth.
  • Accelerated involution follows chronic illness, infection, radiation and stress.

III.10Histology of the red bone marrow. Embryonic/foetal haematopoiesis. Haematopoietic stem cells and their regulation. The structure and development of erythrocytes and thrombocytes.

Core answerRed bone marrow (medulla ossium rubra) is a highly vascular, cell-rich connective tissue occupying the marrow cavities of long bones and the intertrabecular spaces of spongy bone; after birth it is the only normal site of haematopoiesis. Prenatal blood formation passes through mesoblastic (yolk sac), hepatic, splenic and myeloid (bone-marrow) phases. Every blood cell descends from a pluripotential haematopoietic stem cell, whose proliferation and differentiation are controlled by colony-stimulating factors, interleukins, erythropoietin and thrombopoietin. Erythrocytes develop through the erythroblastic series with progressive haemoglobinisation and extrusion of the nucleus; platelets are fragments of the cytoplasm of megakaryocytes, released into the marrow sinusoids as proplatelets.

1.Histology of the red bone marrow

Bone marrow is a gelatinous, extensively vascular, cell-rich tissue filling the spaces between bone trabeculae and the medullary cavity of long bones. In infants and young individuals all marrow is haematopoietically active and therefore red; as a person approaches 20 years of age much of the diaphyseal marrow accumulates fat and becomes inactive yellow marrow (medulla ossium flava).

  • Vascular compartment: nutrient arteries enter through nutrient canals and break up into a network of wide sinusoids; these drain into a central longitudinal vein, which empties into veins leaving through the nutrient canals. The marrow veins are smaller than their arteries, and the high hydrostatic pressure keeps the thin-walled sinusoids patent.
  • Haematopoietic compartment: between the sinusoids lie the haematopoietic cords (islands), usually composed of a single cell lineage at various stages of maturation. Red-cell precursors and megakaryocytes lie mainly in the intertrabecular spaces, white-cell precursors mainly in the paratrabecular spaces.
  • Sinusoid wall: a discontinuous endothelium resting on a basal lamina, a fine mesh of reticular fibres (type III collagen) and adventitial reticular cells, whose processes wrap the sinusoid and enclose the haematopoietic cords.
  • Adventitial reticular cells regulate how much marrow volume is available for haematopoiesis: when they accumulate lipid they enlarge and convert red into yellow marrow. Reticular cells also secrete growth factors (e.g. stem cell factor).
  • Macrophages in the cords phagocytose extruded nuclei and discarded cytoplasm and supply iron to the erythroid line (they become siderophages when loaded with haemosiderin).

Mature blood cells cross the sinusoidal endothelium into the bloodstream. In the adult, active red marrow persists in the axial skeleton (vertebrae, sternum, ribs, skull, pelvis) and in the proximal ends of the humerus and femur.

6.Thrombopoiesis and the structure of platelets

Platelets arise from CFU-Meg, which produce megakaryoblasts. These enlarge by endomitosis – repeated nuclear division without cytoplasmic division – and become giant, polyploid megakaryocytes (up to 64 N; among the largest cells of the body, about 35–150 μm), regulated by thrombopoietin. Megakaryocytes lie next to the marrow sinusoids and push their cytoplasm into the sinusoidal lumen; the cytoplasm fragments along demarcation channels and releases proplatelets, which disperse into individual platelets in the circulation.

  • Platelets are disc-shaped cell fragments 2–4 μm in diameter, without a nucleus; they survive about two weeks.
  • On Romanovsky staining they show a pale periphery – the hyalomere – and a darker central granulomere.
  • Hyalomere: a ring of microtubules plus actin and myosin, and two channel systems – the surface-opening tubular system (increases surface area) and the dense tubular system (calcium sequestration).
  • Granulomere: mitochondria, glycogen, peroxisomes and three granule types – alpha granules (fibrinogen, platelet-derived growth factor, thromboplastin, coagulation factors), delta granules (ADP, ATP, calcium, serotonin, histamine) and gamma granules (lysosomes).
  • Function: adhesion to exposed collagen, aggregation and formation of the platelet plug, then participation in the coagulation cascade.

III.11White blood cells: types, and their morphological properties and development. The mononuclear phagocytic system.

Core answerWhite blood cells (leukocytes, leucocyti) are the nucleated formed elements of blood; they use the circulation only to reach the tissues. They are classified into granulocytes – neutrophils, eosinophils and basophils, which have specific granules and lobed nuclei – and agranulocytes – lymphocytes and monocytes, which lack specific granules. All descend from the pluripotential haematopoietic stem cell: the granulocytes pass through myeloblast → promyelocyte → myelocyte → metamyelocyte → band cell → mature cell, while monocytes pass through promonocyte and enter the tissues as macrophages, the cells of the mononuclear phagocytic system.

1.Classification and general properties

Leukocytes number about 6,500–10,000 per mm³ of blood. They have no function within the bloodstream: they leave the capillaries and venules by diapedesis (migration between endothelial cells), enter the connective tissue compartment and act there. Two large groups are distinguished:

  • Granulocytes (granulocyti, polymorphonuclear leukocytes): contain specific granules and a segmented nucleus; examples are neutrophils, eosinophils and basophils. They are terminally differentiated and cannot divide.
  • Agranulocytes (agranulocyti, mononuclear cells): no specific granules; lymphocytes and monocytes.

The granules are of two kinds: azurophilic granules, which are lysosomes present in all granulocytes, and specific granules, whose staining defines the cell type. On Romanovsky-type stains (Wright, Giemsa) neutrophils are pink, eosinophils bright orange-red and basophils dark blue-black.

4.Monocytopoiesis and the mononuclear phagocytic system

Monocytopoiesis starts from the bipotential CFU-GM, which gives the unipotent CFU-M; its precursor cells are the promonocyte, which matures into the monocyte that enters the circulation. A few days after release the monocyte leaves the bloodstream, enters the connective tissue and differentiates into a macrophage.

The mononuclear phagocytic system (monocyte–macrophage system, MPS) comprises all cells derived from these marrow precursors: blood monocytes, connective-tissue macrophages and the specialised macrophages of individual organs. It is a system of phagocytes and antigen-presenting cells.

  • Resident (fixed) macrophages: Kupffer cells of the liver, dust cells of the lung, microglia of the central nervous system, and the dendritic (Langerhans) cells of the epidermis; splenic and lymph-node macrophages; bone-marrow macrophages.
  • Transient (free, elicited) macrophages: monocytes that enter an area of inflammation, act and then die or migrate away.
  • Fused macrophages: foreign-body giant cells (when the material is too large for one cell) and osteoclasts of bone.

Functions of macrophages:

  1. Phagocytosis of invading microorganisms, dead and disrupted cells and debris.
  2. Secretion of signalling molecules such as TNF-α and IL-1, which initiate inflammation and immune responses.
  3. Antigen presentation: antigens are broken down into epitopes, loaded on MHC II molecules and presented to T helper cells.
  4. Iron recycling after erythrophagocytosis (haemosiderin in siderophages) and removal of extruded nuclei in the bone marrow.

5.Lymphocytes and lymphopoiesis

Lymphopoiesis begins with CFU-Ly, giving CFU-LyT (T cells) and CFU-LyB (B cells). B cells acquire immunocompetence in the bone marrow and then seed the B-dependent zones of secondary lymphoid organs; T-cell progenitors leave the marrow, enter the thymic cortex and become immunocompetent there (see III.9). Mature lymphocytes recirculate between blood, lymph and connective tissue; only about 2% of the body's lymphocytes are in the peripheral blood.

Must-memorise
  • Five leukocytes: neutrophil, eosinophil, basophil (granulocytes); lymphocyte, monocyte (agranulocytes).
  • Differential count: neutrophils 60–70%, lymphocytes 20–25%, monocytes 3–8%, eosinophils 2–4%, basophils <1%.
  • Nuclei: neutrophil multilobed, eosinophil bilobed, basophil S-shaped, lymphocyte round and acentric, monocyte kidney-shaped.
  • Granulocyte sequence: myeloblast → promyelocyte → myelocyte (specific granules appear) → metamyelocyte → band → mature.
  • MPS = monocytes + macrophages + specialised organ macrophages; derived from a common bone-marrow precursor.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Leukocyte adhesion deficiency I: defective selectin ligands or β2-integrins prevent margination and diapedesis, so leukocytes cannot reach inflamed tissue; recurrent bacterial infections occur despite a normal white-cell count.
  • Hereditary NADPH oxidase deficiency: neutrophils phagocytose bacteria but cannot mount a respiratory burst, so they cannot kill them – recurrent bacterial infections in childhood.
  • Pus consists of dead neutrophils, killed bacteria and extracellular fluid; the type of inflammatory exudate (purulent vs. chronic with lymphocytes, plasma cells and fibroblasts) reflects the stage of inflammation.
  • Autoimmune disease may follow failure of selection; HIV destroys CD4+ T helper cells and thus cripples cell-mediated and most humoral responses.
Likely follow-up questions
  • What distinguishes a myeloblast from a promyelocyte? Both lack specific granules and are mitotic; the promyelocyte is larger and has azurophilic granules, coarser chromatin and fewer nucleoli.
  • Which cell type is the largest leukocyte in the circulation? The monocyte – kidney-shaped nucleus, grey-blue cytoplasm; it becomes a macrophage in the tissues.
  • Name four resident macrophage populations. Kupffer cells (liver), dust cells (lung), microglia (CNS), splenic/lymph-node macrophages (and epidermal dendritic cells).

III.12Histology of the lymphatic follicles and lymph nodes.

Core answerLymphoid (lymphatic) follicles (noduli lymphoidei) are nodular accumulations of B lymphocytes supported by reticular tissue. A primary follicle is a resting nodule without a germinal centre; a secondary follicle has a pale germinal centre (dark zone of proliferating centroblasts, light zone of centrocytes), a dense mantle of naive B cells and a peripheral marginal zone. Lymph nodes (nodi lymphoidei) are small, encapsulated, bean-shaped organs interposed in the course of lymphatic vessels: lymph enters through afferent vessels at the convex surface, percolates through the subcapsular, trabecular and medullary sinuses, and leaves through efferent vessels at the hilum, while lymphocytes enter the node through high endothelial venules of the paracortex.

1.Organisation of lymphoid tissue

Lymphoid tissue is classified by its arrangement and by its capsule. By arrangement there is diffuse lymphoid tissue (mainly T lymphocytes scattered among the stroma) and nodular (follicular) lymphoid tissue (mainly B lymphocytes in discrete nodules). By encapsulation the organs are encapsulated (lymph nodes, spleen), partially encapsulated (tonsils) and non-encapsulated (MALT – mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue, e.g. Peyer's patches, BALT). The framework everywhere is reticular connective tissue – type III collagen fibres with reticular cells – except in the thymus, where the stroma is a reticular epithelium.

Lymph nodes are small, usually bean-shaped organs (a few millimetres up to about 3 cm; a healthy node is soft and often not palpable) with a convex surface pierced by afferent vessels and a concave hilum where vessels enter and leave. 4.Lymph flow through the node

afferent lymph vessels → subcapsular (marginal) sinus → trabecular (paratrabecular) sinuses → medullary sinuses → efferent lymph vessels at the hilum

  • Afferent vessels pierce the capsule of the convex surface and open into the subcapsular sinus; their valves prevent lymph from flowing back out of the node.
  • Efferent vessels at the hilum also possess valves, preventing lymph from re-entering the node.
  • The sinuses are lined by simple squamous endothelium and spanned by a network of stellate reticular cells (littoral cells) that slow the flow, giving macrophages a scaffold on which to catch particulate matter.
  • Macrophages in the sinuses remove approximately 99% of the antigen reaching the node by phagocytosis; the remainder reaches the follicles and is presented to B cells.
  • Lymph flow is driven by the contraction of surrounding skeletal muscle and by vessel-wall smooth muscle, since it has no pump of its own.

5.Blood supply and lymphocyte traffic

  • Arteries enter at the hilum and reach the parenchyma along the trabeculae; they break up into capillary beds in the cortex and pass through the paracortex as post-capillary venules.
  • In the paracortex these venules acquire a cuboidal endothelium and become high endothelial venules: B and T lymphocytes leave the blood here, B cells migrate to the cortex and T cells remain in the paracortex.
  • Lymphocytes leave the node with lymph through the efferent vessels or return to the blood, and recirculate between blood, lymph and lymphoid tissue throughout life.
  • Plasma cells formed in the cortex enter the medullary cords; most of them leave through the efferent lymph and settle in the bone marrow, while some secrete antibody locally until they die.
Must-memorise
  • Primary follicle = no germinal centre; secondary follicle = germinal centre + mantle + marginal zone.
  • Dark zone = centroblasts + mutation; light zone = centrocytes + follicular dendritic cells + positive selection.
  • Lymph node: cortex = B cells; paracortex = T cells + HEV; medulla = cords and sinuses.
  • Lymph route: afferent → subcapsular → trabecular → medullary sinuses → efferent at the hilum.
  • FDC are not bone-marrow derived; all other lymph-node macrophages/APCs are.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Lymphadenopathy: inflammation makes a node enlarge, become tender and hard; a node enlarged by tumour is usually painless. Enlargement is caused by germinal-centre formation and lymphocyte proliferation.
  • Sentinel node: the first node draining a region or organ; it is examined surgically (biopsy/blue dye/isotope) because it is the first to receive metastatic cells.
  • Lymphogenic metastasis is typical of carcinomas; the sinuses act as a "sieve" that traps released tumour cells.
  • Chains of connected nodes explain how infection or tumour spreads from one region to another; obstruction of lymphatics produces lymphoedema.
Likely follow-up questions
  • How do you distinguish a lymph node from a tonsil on a slide? The node is completely encapsulated with a hilum, afferent and efferent vessels, and a cortex–paracortex–medulla arrangement; the tonsil is partly covered by epithelium with crypts and has no afferent lymphatics.
  • Where are T and B cells in the node? B cells in cortical follicles, T cells in the paracortex (around the HEV).
  • What are the functions of the node? Filtration of lymph, phagocytosis of particulate antigen, and initiation of humoral and cell-mediated immune responses.

III.13The functional anatomy and histology of the tonsils.

Core answerThe tonsils (tonsillae) are partially encapsulated collections of lymphoid tissue lying beneath the epithelium at the entrance of the pharynx, where – together with the pharyngeal, tubal, palatine and lingual tonsils – they form Waldeyer's tonsillar ring. Their function is to sample inhaled and ingested antigens; the crypts trap debris and microorganisms, while the lymphoid nodules behind the epithelium mount the immune response. The palatine tonsil lies between the palatoglossal and palatopharyngeal arches, is covered by stratified squamous non-keratinised epithelium and possesses only efferent lymphatics, so it does not filter lymph.

1.Definition and classification

Tonsils are partially encapsulated lymphoid organs: their free surface is covered by epithelium continuous with that of the pharynx, while only the deep surface is separated from the underlying tissues by a fibrous capsule. They consist of a stroma of reticular connective tissue containing numerous lymphoid nodules, many with germinal centres, with T lymphocytes scattered in the interfollicular tissue.

2.Waldeyer's tonsillar ring – functional anatomy

Waldeyer's tonsillar ring (anulus lymphoideus pharyngis) is a nearly complete ring of lymphoid tissue surrounding the openings of the nasal and oral cavities into the pharynx. It guards the entrance of both the respiratory and the digestive tracts, so that antigens in air and food are sampled before they pass further. The ring is formed by the pharyngeal tonsil above, the palatine and tubal tonsils laterally, the lingual tonsil below and small lymphoid nodules in the mucosa of the soft palate and pharyngeal wall.

  • Pharyngeal tonsil: on the roof of the nasopharynx; covered by respiratory epithelium (pseudostratified ciliated columnar) with pleats rather than true crypts and a thin capsule; its enlargement (adenoid) blocks the nasal airway.
  • Palatine tonsils: the largest and most frequently examined; wedged between the two palatal arches, resting on the superior constrictor of the pharynx, with the glossopharyngeal nerve passing close to its deep surface (hence the gag reflex).
  • Lingual tonsils: a collection of nodules at the root of the tongue; the ducts of the posterior mucous lingual glands open into the floor of its crypts.
  • Tubal tonsil: a small nodular mass at the pharyngeal opening of the auditory tube; part of the defence of the middle ear.

Tonsils develop from the endoderm of the pharyngeal wall, as does the thymus, and they combine features of the thymus (endodermal origin, only draining lymphatics) with those of a lymph node (numerous B-cell nodules). They diminish with age and are at their most prominent in childhood. Their mucosal defence is mediated mainly by IgA, which is secreted onto the epithelial surface as a dimer stabilised by the secretory component produced by the epithelial cells.

3.Histology of the palatine tonsil

  • Surface epithelium: stratified squamous non-keratinised epithelium, continuous with the pharyngeal mucosa; it is permeated by lymphocytes ("fibrillated" or reticulated appearance) and contains antigen-presenting cells.
  • Crypts (cryptae tonsillae): about a dozen deep, branching pits lined by the same epithelium; their lumina contain desquamated epithelium, food debris, cell detritus and colonies of microorganisms. The entrance to a crypt is the fossula.
  • Parenchyma: numerous lymphoid nodules, many with pale germinal centres, lying immediately beneath the epithelium; T lymphocytes occupy the interfollicular tissue. Each nodule is structurally identical to a lymph-node follicle.
  • Capsule and trabeculae: the deep aspect is separated from the surrounding muscle by a dense fibrous capsule that sends trabeculae into the organ; this capsule is the plane of surgical cleavage in tonsillectomy and a barrier against the spread of infection.
  • Lymphatic vessels: the tonsil contains only efferent lymphatic vessels; it has no afferent lymphatics and therefore does not act as a lymph filter.

III.14The spleen: anatomy, blood supply and histology.

Core answerThe spleen (splen, lien) is the largest lymphoid organ, lying in the left hypochondrium against the diaphragm at the level of ribs IX–X. It is covered by peritoneum except at the hilum and is suspended by the gastrosplenic and splenorenal ligaments. Its blood supply is the splenic artery from the coeliac trunk; its parenchyma consists of white pulp (T cells in the periarterial lymphatic sheath and B cells in lymphoid nodules), a marginal zone and red pulp (splenic cords of Billroth and sinusoids). It filters the blood, destroys aged erythrocytes and mounts immune responses against blood-borne antigens.

1.Gross anatomy

The spleen is a soft, vascular, wedge-shaped organ in the left upper quadrant (left hypochondrium) of the abdomen, lying against the diaphragm at the level of ribs IX to X. It is surrounded by viscera and is normally not palpable. It has:

  • a convex diaphragmatic surface facing posterosuperiorly;
  • a concave visceral surface facing anteroinferiorly, bearing impressions for the stomach (gastric impression), the left kidney (renal impression) and the left colic flexure (colic impression);
  • an upper pole and a lower pole;
  • a medial hilum, the only area not covered by visceral peritoneum, where the splenic vessels enter;
  • a dense, thin capsule of collagenous connective tissue covered by peritoneum.

The spleen is connected to the greater curvature of the stomach by the gastrosplenic ligament, which contains the short gastric and gastro-omental vessels, and to the left kidney by the splenorenal ligament, which contains the splenic vessels; both ligaments are parts of the greater omentum. Occasionally the tail of the pancreas reaches the hilum. In an adult the organ weighs roughly 50–250 g.

2.Development and functions

The spleen develops as part of the vascular system in the part of the dorsal mesentery that suspends the developing stomach from the body wall; it therefore belongs to the foregut region and is supplied by the coeliac trunk. It is a site of haematopoiesis in the foetus, and in the adult it can resume blood formation if the marrow fails (extramedullary haematopoiesis). Its functions are:

  1. Filtration of blood and destruction of senescent erythrocytes (about 120 days old) and old platelets by macrophages of the red pulp.
  2. Immune surveillance: T and B cells are activated in the white pulp against blood-borne antigens, and plasma cells and antibodies are produced.
  3. Haematopoiesis in the foetus and in marrow failure.
  4. Blood reservoir: the human spleen has only isolated myofibroblasts in its capsule and cannot contract to expel blood as the spleen of some animals does.

3.Blood supply and venous drainage

The splenic artery is the largest branch of the coeliac trunk. It runs a tortuous course to the left along the superior border of the pancreas, travels in the splenorenal ligament and divides into branches that enter the hilum. Along the way it gives:

  • short gastric arteries, which pass through the gastrosplenic ligament to the fundus of the stomach;
  • the left gastro-omental artery, which runs along the greater curvature of the stomach;
  • numerous small branches to the neck, body and tail of the pancreas.

At the hilum the artery divides into segmental branches; inside the organ the vessels continue as trabecular arteries within the connective-tissue trabeculae, then as sheathed arteries in the white pulp, central arterioles and penicillar arteries. Venous blood is collected by red-pulp veins into trabecular veins; these unite at the hilum to form the splenic vein, which runs behind the body of the pancreas and joins the superior mesenteric vein to form the portal vein. Only efferent lymphatics leave the spleen.

4.Histology

  • Capsule and trabeculae: dense collagenous connective tissue with elastic fibres, covered by mesothelium (peritoneum); trabeculae carry the trabecular arteries and veins into the parenchyma. The stroma is reticular connective tissue (type III collagen).
  • White pulp (pulpa alba): lymphoid tissue arranged around the arteries, in two parts. The periarterial lymphatic sheath (PALS) is a cylinder of T lymphocytes around a central arteriole; within or beside it a lymphoid nodule of B lymphocytes may develop, sometimes with a pale germinal centre (if the nodule is active the central arteriole appears eccentric).
  • Marginal zone: the interface between white and red pulp; contains macrophages, interdigitating dendritic cells, plasma cells, T and B cells and small marginal sinuses. It is the first site where arriving blood contacts the splenic parenchyma and where antigens are captured.
  • Red pulp (pulpa rubra): composed of splenic cords of Billroth – reticular fibres covered by stellated reticular cells, containing erythrocytes, macrophages, plasma cells, granulocytes, monocytes and platelets – separated by wide splenic sinusoids with a special endothelium (fusiform "stave" cells arranged along the long axis), discontinuous basal lamina and annular reticular fibres.

Must-memorise

Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Splenomegaly: the spleen enlarges in infection, portal hypertension, haemolytic anaemias, leukaemia and lymphoma; it is detected by deep palpation of the left hypochondrium and confirmed by ultrasound or CT.
  • Splenic rupture: the capsule is thin and the organ highly vascular, so blunt trauma to the left lower ribs – often with rib fractures – causes profuse intraperitoneal bleeding; rupture must always be suspected after blunt abdominal injury.
  • Splenectomy may be required after rupture; the organ is removed with its vessels at the hilum, and the patient must thereafter be protected against blood-borne infection.
  • Hypersplenism – an overactive spleen – removes excessive numbers of blood cells, producing cytopenias that improve after splenectomy.
Likely follow-up questions
  • What are the differences between the spleen and a lymph node? The spleen filters blood (not lymph), has no cortex/paracortex/medulla but white pulp, marginal zone and red pulp, has only efferent lymphatics, and its T zone is the PALS around a central arteriole.
  • Where are old red blood cells destroyed? In the red pulp cords of the spleen, by macrophages; the sinusoid wall lets only deformable young cells through.
  • Why is the spleen vulnerable in trauma? It has a thin capsule, is highly vascular and lies under the left lower ribs.

III.15Upper airways: functional anatomy of the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses. (without structures of the skull)

Core answerThe two nasal cavities (cavitas nasi) are the uppermost part of the respiratory tract: wedge-shaped spaces held open by bone and cartilage, divided by the nasal septum and opening posteriorly through the choanae into the nasopharynx. Their lateral wall carries the three conchae, which create the inferior, middle and superior meatuses plus the spheno-ethmoidal recess; each meatus receives a defined set of sinus openings. They warm, humidify and filter inspired air, give the sense of smell, help in resonance of the voice, and their mucosa and paranasal sinuses are common sites of disease. Sinus drainage is the classic examination table: frontal, anterior ethmoidal and maxillary sinuses open into the middle meatus, posterior ethmoidal cells into the superior meatus, the sphenoidal sinus into the spheno-ethmoidal recess, and the nasolacrimal duct into the inferior meatus.

1.Boundaries, regions and general form

Each nasal cavity is an elongated wedge with a wide base below (on the hard palate) and a narrow apex above and behind. It has a roof, a floor, a medial wall (the septum) and a lateral wall. Anteriorly each cavity opens onto the face through the naris (nostril) and posteriorly through the choana into the nasopharynx. The cavities are separated from the anterior cranial fossa above, the oral cavity below and the orbits laterally, which explains the direction in which sinus and nasal infection spreads.

Three regions are described in each cavity:

  • Nasal vestibule (vestibulum nasi) — a small dilated space just inside the naris, lined by skin with coarse hairs (vibrissae) that trap large particles.
  • Respiratory region — the largest part, with a thick, richly vascular mucosa that conditions the air.
  • Olfactory region — a small area at the apex, in the roof and upper part of the septum and superior concha, lined by olfactory epithelium.

The nasal septum is partly cartilaginous (anteriorly), partly bony (perpendicular plate of the ethmoid and the vomer), with contributions from the nasal crests of the maxilla and palatine bones. Deviation of the septum is a frequent, functionally significant finding.

2.The lateral wall: conchae, meatuses and their openings

Three curved shelves project medially and inferiorly from the lateral wall — the superior, middle and inferior conchae (conchae nasales). They divide the cavity into four air channels, increasing the surface of contact between mucosa and inspired air and creating turbulent, slow flow that favours conditioning of the air. The inferior concha is a separate bone; the superior and middle conchae are parts of the ethmoidal labyrinth.

Only thin plates of bone separate the sinuses from dangerous neighbours, and this determines the complications: frontal sinusitis may produce an orbital abscess or a subdural empyema; ethmoidal infection may spread into the orbit; sphenoidal disease may involve the cavernous sinus; maxillary infection may spread through the floor to the teeth or, from a decayed tooth, in the opposite direction. The subarachnoid space and the hypophysis lie immediately above the roof, which is why the transsphenoidal route is used for pituitary surgery.

4.Mucosa, olfaction and respiratory protection

The respiratory region is covered by respiratory epithelium: pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium rich in goblet cells, resting on a lamina propria containing seromucous glands, lymphoid aggregates and large, thin-walled venous channels known as swell bodies. Engorgement of these venous sinusoids alternates from side to side roughly every 20–30 minutes, slightly obstructing one cavity and directing most airflow to the other; this contributes to hydration of the mucosa. The cilia carry the mucus blanket, with entrapped particles, posteriorly to be swallowed — the mucociliary escalator. The nasal mucosa is the first site of contact for inhaled allergens, and its lymphoid tissue is the basis of local immunity.

The olfactory region is yellowish and small. Its epithelium contains bipolar olfactory cells (cranial nerve I), supporting (sustentacular) cells that provide insulation and pigment, and regenerative basal cells. The axons of olfactory cells pass through the foramina of the cribriform plate to the olfactory bulb. The lamina propria here contains Bowman's glands, whose watery secretion dissolves odorous molecules and flushes them away.

The nose also functions as a resonating chamber for the voice, and the trigeminal mucosa triggers protective reflexes — sneezing, nasal congestion, and reflex apnoea on strong chemical stimulation.

5.Blood supply and innervation

The blood supply comes from both carotid systems, with a rich submucosal anastomosis:

  • External carotid origin: terminal branches of the maxillary artery (sphenopalatine, greater and lesser palatine) and of the facial artery (superior labial, lateral nasal).
  • Internal carotid origin: anterior and posterior ethmoidal arteries from the ophthalmic artery.
  • The anteroinferior part of the septum — Kiesselbach's area — is the meeting point of anterior ethmoidal, sphenopalatine, greater palatine and superior labial branches, and is the usual source of epistaxis.

Venous blood drains by way of the facial and ophthalmic veins and the pterygoid venous plexus, which communicates with the cavernous sinus; retrograde flow through valveless veins is the route by which nasal or facial sepsis can reach the intracranial venous sinuses.

General sensation is carried by the trigeminal nerve: anterior regions by the ophthalmic (V1) division and posterior regions by the maxillary (V2) division (nasopalatine, greater and lesser palatine, infra-orbital nerves). Smell is cranial nerve I. Secretion is parasympathetic from the greater petrosal nerve (VII), which joins maxillary branches in the pterygopalatine fossa and relays in the pterygopalatine ganglion; sympathetic fibres arise from the T1 segment, relay in the superior cervical ganglion and travel with vessels and V2 branches.

Must-memorise
  • Three conchae and four channels: inferior meatus, middle meatus, superior meatus and the spheno-ethmoidal recess.
  • Middle meatus = frontal (frontonasal duct), anterior ethmoidal cells, ethmoidal bulla (middle cells), maxillary sinus.
  • Superior meatus = posterior ethmoidal cells; spheno-ethmoidal recess = sphenoidal sinus; inferior meatus = nasolacrimal duct.
  • Maxillary sinus is the largest and its ostium sits high above the floor, so it drains poorly — the commonest sinus to become infected.
  • Nerve supply of the sinuses is the same as that of the corresponding nasal mucosa: V1 for frontal and ethmoidal, V2 for maxillary, V1/V2 for sphenoidal.
  • Kiesselbach's area on the anteroinferior septum is the site of most nosebleeds.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Epistaxis: bleeding from Kiesselbach's area, usually from an anterior ethmoidal or sphenopalatine branch; managed by pressure over the alae, vasoconstrictor packing, cautery or arterial ligation in severe cases. Persistent posterior bleeding can be dangerous, especially in older patients.
  • Acute sinusitis: pain, tenderness and discharge; the maxillary sinus is most often affected. Purulent material cannot drain from the high ostium and pools in the floor, which is why the upright position does not empty it and why the roots of the upper molars and premolars, which lie immediately below the floor, may be involved in dental infection.
  • Complications of sinusitis: orbital cellulitis or abscess (ethmoidal and frontal), cavernous sinus thrombosis (sphenoidal, ethmoidal), subdural empyema, osteomyelitis of the frontal bone.
  • CSF rhinorrhoea: after fracture of the cribriform plate or of the sphenoid; clear fluid drips from the nose and the infection may ascend to the meninges.
  • Imaging: on CT the air-filled sinuses are black (low density), the mucosal lining and fluid are grey; the osteomeatal complex of the middle meatus is the key region in endoscopic sinus surgery.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Which sinus drains into the inferior meatus? — None; the inferior meatus receives only the nasolacrimal duct.
  • Why does the nose heat and humidify air so efficiently? — The extensive contact surface of the conchae, the rich submucosal venous sinusoids (counter-current warming), seromucous glands, and slow, turbulent airflow.
  • Where would you look on a radiograph for the maxillary sinus and what bone forms its roof? — The maxilla; its roof is the floor of the orbit.
  • Which sinus lies next to the pituitary gland? — The sphenoidal sinus, approached through its roof for transsphenoidal pituitary surgery.
  • How do nasal contents reach the intracranial venous sinuses? — Through valveless veins draining to the pterygoid plexus and from there to the cavernous sinus, or by direct spread through the thin ethmoidal roof.

III.16The cartilages, ligaments and cavity of the larynx. The anatomy of the hyoid bone. Laryngoscopic image.

Core answerThe larynx (larynx) is a musculocartilaginous tube in the anterior neck, suspended from the hyoid bone above and continuous with the trachea below, which guards the airway, produces the voice and closes during swallowing. Its skeleton is built of three unpaired cartilages (thyroid, cricoid, epiglottis) and three paired cartilages (arytenoid, corniculate, cuneiform), united by synovial joints (cricothyroid and cricoarytenoid) and by the fibro-elastic membrane — the conus elasticus with its thickened upper margin, the vocal ligament, and the quadrangular membrane with its thickened lower margin, the vestibular ligament. The cavity is divided by two pairs of folds into vestibule, middle chamber (with the laryngeal ventricle) and infraglottic cavity; the narrowest point is the rima glottidis. On indirect laryngoscopy the typical picture is a pearly-white pair of vocal folds forming an anteriorward-pointing V, with the ventricular (false) folds pink and lateral to them and the piriform fossae alongside.

1.Position, extent and gross relations

The larynx lies in the anterior part of the neck, its superior border at the level of the hyoid bone (about C3) and its inferior border at the cricoid cartilage (about C6), where it continues as the trachea. Behind it is the laryngopharynx; in front, it is covered by the infrahyoid (strap) muscles, the deep cervical fascia and the thyroid gland, whose isthmus lies over the first tracheal rings. Laterally run the carotid sheaths with the thyroid lobes; in front of the lower part the skin and subcutaneous tissue form the median cricothyroid ligament, an emergency airway landmark. It moves upwards and forwards on swallowing and is highly mobile in the neck.

Palpable landmarks are the laryngeal prominence (Adam's apple), the superior thyroid notch, the upper border of the thyroid cartilage, the cricothyroid space (median cricothyroid ligament) and the rounded arch of the cricoid cartilage.

3.Joints, membranes and ligaments

Two pairs of synovial joints move the cartilages relative to each other and are the mechanical basis of phonation:

  • Cricothyroid joints — between the inferior horns of the thyroid and the sides of the cricoid. They allow the thyroid cartilage to tilt forwards and downwards on the cricoid, which lengthens and tightens the vocal ligaments.
  • Crico-arytenoid joints — between the bases of the arytenoid cartilages and the sloping facets of the cricoid lamina. They allow the arytenoids to slide towards and away from each other and to rotate, so that the vocal processes pivot medially (adduction) or laterally (abduction).

5.The hyoid bone

The hyoid bone (os hyoideum) is a small U-shaped bone lying horizontally in the upper neck at about the level of C3, where it can be palpated between the mandible and the thyroid cartilage and moved from side to side. It has a body anteriorly and two pairs of horns: the greater horns, which project posteriorly and continue the line of the body, and the lesser horns, which project upwards from the junction of body and greater horn. It has no direct articulation with any other bone; it is suspended by muscles and by the stylohyoid ligament from the styloid process, and it provides attachment for the tongue, the larynx and the pharynx — it lies at the interface of the floor of the mouth, the larynx and the pharynx.

6.The laryngoscopic image

Indirect laryngoscopy uses a small warmed mirror held in the oropharynx; the image is inverted, so the anterior structures appear at the top. Direct laryngoscopy is performed with a curved blade whose tip is placed in the vallecula to lift the epiglottis forwards; it requires an unconscious patient or one in whom the gag reflex is abolished. Flexible fibreoptic endoscopy through the nose gives a direct, upright view and allows assessment of movement.

The normal image shows, from anterior to posterior: the epiglottis with its free margin, the paired ary-epiglottic folds, the piriform fossae laterally, and the arytenoid and corniculate tubercles posteriorly. The vestibular folds (false cords) are pink and lie lateral to the pearly-white, sharp-edged vocal folds, which meet anteriorly at the anterior commissure and diverge posteriorly. During quiet inspiration the rima glottidis is triangular, widening into a rhomboid only on forced inspiration; on phonation of a high-pitched 'ee' the vocal folds adduct, lengthen and become tense; during a Valsalva manoeuvre they close completely, and the false folds are apposed. The examiner looks for symmetry, mobility, mucosal irregularity, nodules, oedema or a mass.

Must-memorise
  • Cartilages: unpaired — thyroid, cricoid, epiglottis; paired — arytenoid, corniculate, cuneiform. Cricoid is the only complete ring; the epiglottis is elastic cartilage.
  • Vocal ligament = thickened free margin of the conus elasticus; vestibular ligament = thickened free margin of the quadrangular membrane.
  • Thyrohyoid membrane transmits the internal laryngeal nerve and superior laryngeal vessels.
  • Median cricothyroid ligament — the emergency airway, between the inferior thyroid notch and the cricoid arch.
  • Cavity: vestibule → rima vestibuli → middle chamber (ventricle, saccule) → rima glottidis → infraglottic cavity.
  • Hyoid at C3, no direct joint, suspended by the stylohyoid ligament; greater horn and lower body from the 3rd pharyngeal arch, lesser horn and upper body from the 2nd arch.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Acute epiglottitis (mainly in children): rapid swelling of the epiglottis with stridor and a muffled voice; the lateral neck radiograph shows the thumb sign. The airway must not be manipulated without facilities for immediate intubation.
  • Cricothyrotomy: the median cricothyroid ligament is perforated when the airway is obstructed above the vocal folds; only skin, superficial tissue and small vessels lie between it and the skin.
  • Laryngeal trauma: the thyroid and cricoid cartilages are injured by blunt blows; the airway may be disrupted, and the cricothyroid and crico-arytenoid joints may be dislocated, producing an immobile vocal fold.
  • Laryngoscopy in the examination setting: the larynx is inspected for hoarseness, suspected tumour, a foreign body, or vocal fold palsy; on phonation a paralysed fold lies paramedian and does not move.
  • Laryngomalacia in infants: floppy epiglottis and ary-epiglottic folds causing inspiratory stridor and a 'floppy' image.

  • Which joint tenses the vocal folds and how? — The cricothyroid joint: the thyroid cartilage tilts forwards and downwards, lengthening the vocal ligaments.
  • Name the structures pierced by the thyrohyoid membrane. — The internal laryngeal nerve and the superior laryngeal artery and vein.
  • What separates the true from the false vocal fold? — The gap between the vocal and vestibular ligaments, which opens laterally into the laryngeal ventricle and saccule.
  • Why is the laryngeal inlet safe during swallowing? — The larynx moves up and forwards, the epiglottis is folded down over the inlet, and the rima glottidis is closed; the bolus passes through the piriform fossae into the oesophagus.
  • Which part of the laryngeal cavity is the narrowest? — The rima glottidis, between the vocal folds.

III.17The muscles, blood supply and innervation of the larynx. The histology of the larynx.

Core answerThe muscles of the larynx are divided into extrinsic muscles, which move the whole larynx (suprahyoid, infrahyoid and pharyngeal muscles), and intrinsic muscles, which act on the cricothyroid and crico-arytenoid joints and on the vocal ligaments. The intrinsic group has one abductor — the posterior crico-arytenoid — several adductors (lateral crico-arytenoid, transverse and oblique arytenoids, thyro-arytenoid) and two tensors (cricothyroid and vocalis). All intrinsic muscles are supplied by the recurrent laryngeal nerve except the cricothyroid, which is supplied by the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve. Blood comes from the superior and inferior laryngeal arteries; the mucosa is respiratory epithelium except over the vocal folds and the upper (superior) part of the epiglottis, where it is non-keratinized stratified squamous.

1.Classification

Extrinsic muscles attach the larynx to the hyoid bone, the mandible, the sternum and the pharynx; they move the whole larynx up or down and so participate in swallowing and in phonation. Intrinsic muscles have both attachments on the laryngeal cartilages and fibro-elastic membrane; they alter the tension of the vocal ligaments, open and close the rima glottidis, narrow the vestibule and close the laryngeal inlet. They achieve this by moving the cricothyroid and crico-arytenoid joints, by pulling directly on the vocal folds, and by forcing the soft tissues of the quadrangular membrane towards the midline.

4.Innervation

All motor and sensory innervation of the larynx comes from the vagus nerve [X] through two branches on each side.

  • Superior laryngeal nerve arises from the inferior vagal ganglion high in the neck and divides just above the level of the superior horn of the hyoid. Its internal branch pierces the thyrohyoid membrane with the superior laryngeal artery and is mainly sensory to the laryngeal cavity down to the vocal folds, including the laryngeal surface of the epiglottis. Its external branch descends on the inferior constrictor and supplies the cricothyroid.
  • Recurrent laryngeal nerve is sensory below the vocal folds and motor to all intrinsic muscles except cricothyroid. The left recurrent nerve arises in the thorax and hooks under the arch of the aorta lateral to the ligamentum arteriosum; the right arises in the root of the neck and hooks under the right subclavian artery. Both ascend in the groove between the oesophagus and the trachea and enter the larynx deep to the margin of the inferior constrictor.

This asymmetry matters clinically: a mass in the aortopulmonary window paralyses the left cord; a right apical lung tumour (Pancoast tumour) paralyses the right. The vocal folds also receive autonomic fibres along blood vessels and proprioceptive fibres that are of little clinical significance.

Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Recurrent laryngeal nerve injury: the vocal fold takes the cadaveric position (half-way between abduction and adduction) and the voice is hoarse; bilateral injury causes stridor and may require tracheostomy.   • External branch of the superior laryngeal nerve injury (for example during ligation of the superior thyroid vessels): loss of high-pitched phonation and fatigue of the voice, while the vocal fold remains mobile.
  • Laryngeal carcinoma: hoarseness, stridor or dysphagia; glottic tumours are often diagnosed early and spread late, while supraglottic tumours spread to deep cervical nodes.
  • Reinke's oedema: fluid in the superficial lamina propria of the vocal folds in smokers, giving a deep, husky voice and polypoid folds.
  • Vocal fold nodules: bilateral fibrotic thickenings from vocal abuse, typically near the junction of the anterior and middle thirds of the fold.

III.18The anatomy of the trachea. Organization of the bronchial system. The histological properties of the trachea, the bronchi and bronchioli. Functional structure and cell types of the alveolus.

Core answerThe trachea is a flexible tube about 12 cm long that begins at the lower border of the cricoid cartilage (about C6) and bifurcates at vertebral level T4/T5, at the sternal angle, into the right and left main bronchi. Its wall is kept open by C-shaped hyaline cartilage rings whose open ends face posteriorly and are joined by the trachealis muscle. The bronchial tree continues as lobar and segmental bronchi, then bronchioles, terminal and respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts, alveolar sacs and alveoli; as the branches narrow, cartilage and glands disappear, the epithelium flattens, and smooth muscle and elastic tissue become relatively more abundant. The alveolus is lined by type I pneumocytes (about 95 % of the surface area) and type II pneumocytes (which produce surfactant) and contains alveolar macrophages; the blood–air (alveolocapillary) barrier consists of endothelium, fused basal laminae, type I pneumocyte and surfactant.

1.The trachea: extent, relations and structure

The trachea (trachea) begins at the lower border of the cricoid cartilage, at about the level of C6, and ends at its bifurcation into the two main bronchi at vertebral level T4/T5 — a level that can be marked on the body surface by the sternal angle, the plane separating the superior from the inferior mediastinum. It is about 12 cm long. The carina is the hook-shaped ridge of the last tracheal cartilage that projects backwards in the midline between the origins of the two main bronchi; it is the landmark used at bronchoscopy.

The wall is built in three layers. The mucosa consists of respiratory epithelium, a fibro-elastic lamina propria and a thick elastic sheet; the submucosa contains seromucous glands and lymphoid tissue; the adventitia carries the C-shaped rings of hyaline cartilage, whose perichondria are linked by fibro-elastic tissue. The open ends of the rings face posteriorly and are joined by the trachealis (smooth) muscle in the membranous wall; its contraction reduces the tracheal lumen and speeds airflow during coughing.

2.Organisation of the bronchial system

The trachea bifurcates into two main (primary) bronchi, each of which enters the root and hilum of a lung. The right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical than the left, so inhaled foreign bodies lodge on the right more often. The right main bronchus gives its lobar branch to the superior lobe while still in the root (the eparterial bronchus, superior to the right pulmonary artery); on the left the superior lobe bronchus arises within the lung. Each lobar (secondary) bronchus supplies one lobe: three on the right, two on the left. Each then divides into segmental (tertiary) bronchi, each supplying one of the ten bronchopulmonary segments of that lung. Beyond that lie the bronchioles and the respiratory portion.

The total cross-sectional area of the airways increases at each generation, so airflow velocity falls, which favours gas exchange.

3.Histology of the trachea, bronchi and bronchioli

The respiratory epithelium is pseudostratified ciliated columnar and contains six cell types: ciliated columnar cells, goblet cells (about one third of the population, storing mucinogen in their theca), short regenerative basal cells, brush cells with long microvilli, serous cells, and DNES (neuroendocrine) cells that respond to hypoxia and form part of the pulmonary neuroepithelial bodies. The cilia drive the mucus blanket towards the larynx — the mucociliary escalator.

In the trachea the C-rings lie in the adventitia; in secondary and tertiary bronchi the cartilage is broken into plates that no longer have open arms facing posteriorly, so the smooth muscle migrates inwards and forms two helical layers between the lamina propria and the submucosa. Bronchioles lose cartilage and glands altogether; their patency depends on elastic fibres radiating from the adventitia and on a relatively thick smooth-muscle coat. Clara (club) cells secrete a surfactant-like material that helps to keep the bronchiolar lumen open, detoxify inhaled substances and act as stem cells.

4.The alveolus: cell types, surfactant and the air–blood barrier

Alveoli are the terminal air spaces; there are about 300 million in the two lungs, each about 200 μm in diameter, giving a total surface of roughly 140 m² for gas exchange. Neighbouring alveoli share an interalveolar septum which contains the continuous capillary network, elastic and reticular fibres and connective tissue cells; the openings between adjacent alveoli (alveolar pores) allow collateral ventilation, and a smooth muscle sphincter encircles the mouth of each alveolus.

Elastic fibres are essential to the passive recoil of expiration; their destruction in emphysema is the reason the lungs lose elasticity and the small airways collapse.

Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Foreign body aspiration: the right main bronchus is wider and more vertical, so the object usually lodges on the right, producing unilateral wheeze and air trapping.
  • Tracheostomy is performed through the second and third tracheal rings, dividing the thyroid isthmus if necessary; the cricoid and cricothyroid region are avoided because stenosis and vocal damage are likely.
  • Asthma: bronchospasm of the abundant smooth muscle of the bronchioles plus mucosal oedema and mucus plugging; the response to sympathomimetic bronchodilators is possible because the smooth muscle bears sympathetic receptors.
  • Emphysema: destruction of alveolar walls and elastic tissue reduces both the surface for gas exchange and the elastic recoil, so small airways close during expiration (air trapping).
  • Respiratory distress syndrome of the newborn: immaturity of type II pneumocytes with insufficient surfactant, alveolar collapse, ground-glass lung fields and hyaline membranes.
  • Squamous metaplasia of the respiratory epithelium in chronic smokers: ciliated cells are lost and goblet cells increase, so mucus cannot be cleared and infection follows.

  • At what vertebral level does the trachea bifurcate? — T4/T5, which corresponds on the surface to the sternal angle; the carina lies at this level.
  • How does the histology of a bronchus differ from that of a bronchiole? — The bronchus has cartilage plates and glands; the bronchiole has neither but has a relatively thicker smooth-muscle coat and Clara cells.
  • What are the cell types of the alveolar region and their roles? — Type I (gas exchange), type II (surfactant and regeneration), macrophage (clearance).
  • Why do the alveoli not collapse at end-expiration? — Surfactant lowers surface tension, elastic fibres maintain radial traction, and the alveolar pores allow collateral ventilation.
  • What happens to the bronchial wall after chronic irritation? — Metaplasia of the epithelium with goblet-cell hyperplasia, loss of cilia and hypertrophy of mucous glands, so clearance fails.

III.19Development of the respiratory system.

Core answerThe respiratory system develops as an endodermal outgrowth of the ventral wall of the foregut — the respiratory (lung) diverticulum — which appears in the 4th week and grows into the surrounding splanchnic mesoderm. The diverticulum forms the laryngotracheal groove, which deepens and is separated from the oesophagus by the tracheo-oesophageal septum, giving rise to the larynx, trachea and bronchial tree. The endoderm gives the epithelium and glands; the splanchnic mesoderm gives the cartilage, smooth muscle, connective tissue and blood vessels. Further growth proceeds through five stages — embryonic, pseudoglandular, canalicular, terminal saccular and alveolar — whose approximate weeks must be known, and failure at any step produces a characteristic malformation: tracheo-oesophageal fistula, laryngeal web, pulmonary hypoplasia or respiratory distress syndrome.

1.The lung bud and the separation of the airway from the gut

In the 4th week a median respiratory diverticulum (lung bud) appears on the ventral aspect of the foregut, just caudal to the pharyngeal pouches. It grows caudally and ventrally into the splanchnic (visceral) mesoderm of the foregut. As the diverticulum elongates, its cranial end forms the laryngotracheal groove, whose lips fuse to create the laryngotracheal tube. The tube is separated from the dorsal part of the foregut by the tracheo-oesophageal septum, so that the ventral derivative becomes the airway and the dorsal one becomes the oesophagus.

Two rules must be stated at the start of any answer: the epithelium and the glands of the whole respiratory tract derive from endoderm, while the cartilages, muscles, connective tissue and blood vessels derive from the surrounding splanchnic mesoderm. This is why the respiratory epithelium is endodermal but the laryngeal cartilage is mesodermal in origin.

The distal end of the tube then divides into two bronchial buds, which grow laterally into the pericardioperitoneal canals and become surrounded by the pleural cavities. The right bud divides into three, the left into two, giving the lobar pattern. Subsequent branching is a reciprocal interaction between the endodermal epithelium and the mesenchyme, which determines the number and pattern of the airways.

2.Development of the larynx

The larynx develops around the laryngeal inlet, from the region between the 4th and 6th pharyngeal arches. Its cartilages come from the branchial arch cartilages: the thyroid cartilage mainly from the 4th arch, the cricoid, arytenoid and corniculate from the 6th arch, and the epiglottis from the hypopharyngeal eminence at the junction of the 3rd and 4th arches; the elastic cartilages retain their elasticity for life. The laryngeal muscles are derived from the mesoderm of the 4th and 6th arches, and this explains their innervation: the 4th-arch muscle is the cricothyroid, supplied by the superior laryngeal nerve, while the 6th-arch muscles are those supplied by the recurrent laryngeal nerve.

The laryngeal lumen is temporarily obliterated by epithelial proliferation and then recanalises. Failure of recanalisation produces a laryngeal web or atresia; incomplete separation of the larynx from the pharynx may give a laryngeal cleft. The larynx also descends in the neck during childhood, which is why the infant larynx is high and the epiglottis lies close to the soft palate, allowing simultaneous breathing and feeding.

4.Maturation, surfactant and the fetal circulation

Surfactant is produced by type II pneumocytes: it is stored in lamellar bodies and released into the alveolar lining, where it lowers surface tension and prevents alveolar collapse at end-expiration. Production begins in the canalicular stage and increases greatly in the last weeks of pregnancy; glucocorticoids accelerate its maturation. The lungs are not functionally mature until there is enough surfactant and enough surface area, which is why prematurity is the main risk factor for respiratory distress syndrome.

The lower respiratory tract is fluid-filled in the fetus; the fluid derives from the pulmonary epithelium and amniotic fluid is a small component. Fetal breathing movements are important for the growth of the respiratory muscles and lungs. At birth the first breath must overcome the surface tension of the fluid-filled airways; with the following breaths, the fluid is absorbed, the pulmonary vascular resistance falls, the pulmonary circulation increases and the ductus arteriosus closes functionally within the first days.

5.Anatomical basis of congenital malformations

  • Tracheo-oesophageal fistula with oesophageal atresia: failure of the tracheo-oesophageal septum to close or an abnormal separation of the two tubes; the commonest pattern is a blind upper oesophageal pouch with a fistula between the distal oesophagus and the trachea. The newborn presents with choking, cyanosis on feeding and an air-filled stomach. Part of the VACTERL association.
  • Laryngeal web or atresia: incomplete recanalisation; presents with stridor or aphonia.
  • Bronchial atresia or stenosis: abnormal branching, with distal overinflation or bronchiectasis.
  • Pulmonary hypoplasia: reduced airway branching and small lung volume (e.g. in congenital diaphragmatic hernia, oligohydramnios, bilateral renal agenesis); it is the degree of hypoplasia that determines survival in diaphragmatic hernia.
  • Respiratory distress syndrome: deficiency of surfactant in preterm neonates; hyaline membranes, atelectasis and ground-glass lung fields on radiography; treated by antenatal steroids and exogenous surfactant.
  • Congenital pulmonary airway malformation and pulmonary sequestration: abnormal overgrowth or an isolated mass of lung tissue with an anomalous systemic arterial supply, often from the aorta.

Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Oesophageal atresia with fistula: the prenatal ultrasound may show a small stomach bubble, and polyhydramnios is common; after birth a nasogastric tube coils in the upper pouch on the chest radiograph.
  • Congenital diaphragmatic hernia (Bochdalek): abdominal contents in the thorax compress both lungs and cause pulmonary hypoplasia; the heart may be displaced.
  • Preterm lung on a radiograph: low volume, fine reticulogranular (ground-glass) opacity and air bronchograms, with hyaline membranes on histology.

  • Laryngeal atresia with the fetal lungs full of fluid: on prenatal ultrasound the lungs are large and echogenic and the diaphragm may be inverted.

  • Why does a baby born at 22 weeks not survive? — The canalicular stage is incomplete: not enough respiratory bronchioles, too thick a barrier and virtually no surfactant.

III.20The gross anatomy and histology of the lungs, the bronchopulmonary segments and pleura. The innervation of the pleura.

Core answerEach lung has the shape of a half-cone with a base on the diaphragm, an apex above rib I, a costal and a mediastinal surface and three borders; the right lung has three lobes and an oblique and a horizontal fissure, the left lung two lobes, an oblique fissure and a lingula. Each lung is divided into ten bronchopulmonary segments (some fused on the left), the smallest functionally independent and resectable unit, each supplied by its own segmental bronchus. The lung and the inner surface of the thoracic wall are covered by pleura — visceral and parietal (costal, diaphragmatic, mediastinal, cervical) — with the costomediastinal and costodiaphragmatic recesses in between. The parietal pleura is somatically innervated: costal and cervical parts and the periphery of the diaphragmatic part by intercostal nerves, the mediastinal part and the centre of the diaphragmatic part by the phrenic nerve; the visceral pleura is autonomic and insensitive.

1.Form, surfaces and borders

The lungs lie on either side of the mediastinum, within the pleural cavities. Each is a half-cone with a base (basis pulmonis) resting on the diaphragm, an apex (apex pulmonis) projecting above rib I into the root of the neck, a costal surface against the ribs and intercostal spaces, and a mediastinal surface against the mediastinum anteriorly and the vertebral column posteriorly. The mediastinal surface bears the hilum, a comma-shaped area where the root enters. There are three borders: the inferior, which is sharp and separates the base from the costal surface; the anterior, sharp and overlapping the pericardium (with the cardiac notch on the left); and the posterior, smooth and rounded, fitting against the vertebral column. The right lung is a little larger than the left because the heart bulges to the left.

3.The root and the hilum

The root of the lung (radix pulmonis) is a short tubular collection of structures joining the lung to the mediastinum, covered by a sleeve of mediastinal pleura that reflects on the lung as visceral pleura; the area enclosed by this reflection is the hilum. The root contains a pulmonary artery, two pulmonary veins, a main bronchus, bronchial vessels, nerves and lymphatics. A thin fold of pleura, the pulmonary ligament, hangs from the root below the hilum and stabilises the inferior lobe.

Generally the pulmonary artery is superior, the pulmonary veins inferior and the bronchus posterior and central. On the right the superior lobar bronchus branches within the root and lies above the pulmonary artery (eparterial), whereas on the left the artery lies above the bronchus. The classical mnemonic for the arrangement of the main structures is RALS: Right — pulmonary Artery anterior to the bronchus; Left — pulmonary Artery Superior to the bronchus. The phrenic nerve passes anterior to the root and the vagus nerve posterior to it — a fact that explains which nerve is injured in different mediastinal operations.

4.Bronchopulmonary segments

A bronchopulmonary segment is the area of lung supplied by a segmental bronchus with its accompanying pulmonary artery branch; the pulmonary veins run intersegmentally, between segments. Each segment is shaped like an irregular cone with its apex at the origin of the segmental bronchus and its base at the lung surface. A segment is the smallest functionally independent region of the lung and the smallest area that can be removed without damaging adjacent tissue. There are ten in each lung, but some fuse on the left.

7.Histology of the lungs and pleura

The pleura is a thin serous membrane: a single layer of flat mesothelial cells on a layer of collagen and elastic fibres with vessels, lymphatics and nerves. The visceral pleura is firmly adherent to the lung and carries a rich lymphatic network; the parietal pleura is thicker and is attached to the endothoracic fascia. Between the two lies only a film of serous fluid, which allows frictionless sliding during respiration and holds the two layers together by surface tension.

The lung parenchyma consists of the branching airways with their accompanying vessels and the alveolar tissue. Intrapulmonary bronchi retain cartilage plates and seromucous glands; their smooth muscle forms two helical layers. Bronchioles have no cartilage and no glands but a relatively thick smooth muscle coat and Clara (club) cells. The alveoli are lined by type I and type II pneumocytes, contain alveolar macrophages and lie in a capillary-rich interalveolar septum with elastic fibres. Lymphoid tissue (BALT) lies along the airways, and a network of lymphatics follows the bronchi and the pleura, connecting the two systems at the hilum.

Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Pneumothorax: air in the pleural cavity separates the two layers, elastic recoil collapses the lung and the surface tension mechanism is lost; a tension pneumothorax pushes the mediastinum to the opposite side and is an emergency.
  • Pleural effusion: fluid collects in the costodiaphragmatic recess, so the first radiological sign is blunting of the costophrenic angle; a large effusion collapses the lung and may need drainage.
  • Thoracocentesis and chest drain: fluid is aspirated in the region of the costodiaphragmatic recess, and a chest drain is inserted in the 'safe triangle' of the axilla (anterior border of latissimus dorsi, lateral border of pectoralis major, below the axillary vessels). The needle is passed over the upper border of the lower rib to avoid the intercostal nerve and vessels, which lie in the costal groove.
  • Pleuritic pain from the costal pleura is sharp and inspiratory; referred shoulder-tip pain from the central diaphragm or mediastinal pleura is due to phrenic (C3–C5) afferents.
  • Surgical resection: the segmental architecture allows segmentectomy, lobectomy or pneumonectomy; the bronchopulmonary segments are also the basis of the radiological localisation of pneumonia and of lung cancer staging.
  • Mesothelioma: malignant tumour of the pleural mesothelium, strongly associated with asbestos exposure; it produces a pleural effusion and thickens the pleura.
Likely follow-up questions
  • How do you define a bronchopulmonary segment? — The area supplied by a segmental bronchus and its pulmonary artery branch, with the pulmonary veins running intersegmentally; the smallest resectable unit of the lung.
  • Which segments form the middle lobe? — S IV (lateral) and S V (medial) on the right; on the left they are the superior and inferior lingular segments.
  • Where would you insert a chest drain and why there? — In the safe triangle of the axilla, over the costodiaphragmatic recess and above the upper border of the lower rib, avoiding the intercostal vessels and nerve.
  • What are the differences between visceral and parietal pleura? — Nerve supply (autonomic vs somatic), blood supply (bronchial vs systemic intercostal vessels), lymphatic drainage and pain sensitivity.
  • Why is the right lung more liable to aspirated material? — The right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical.
  • How is the apex of the lung protected? — By the cervical pleura reinforced by the suprapleural membrane attached to rib I and vertebra C VII.

III.21The blood supply, innervation and lymphatic drainage of the lungs.

Core answerThe lung has two circulations. The pulmonary arteries and veins form the functional system: deoxygenated blood reaches the lung through the right and left pulmonary arteries and oxygenated blood leaves through two pulmonary veins on each side. The bronchial arteries, arising from the thoracic aorta or from a posterior intercostal artery, form the nutritive system for the bronchial walls, glands, large vessels and the visceral pleura; the bronchial veins drain partly into the azygos and hemi-azygos systems and partly into the pulmonary veins. The lung is innervated by the anterior and posterior pulmonary plexuses formed by vagal and sympathetic branches — vagal fibres constrict the bronchioles, sympathetic fibres dilate them. Lymphatics drain from superficial (subpleural) and deep (peribronchial) networks through bronchopulmonary and tracheobronchial to paratracheal nodes, and then by the bronchomediastinal trunks into the thoracic duct or right lymphatic duct.

1.The dual blood supply

The clue to the whole topic is that the lung is the only organ receiving blood from two separate arterial systems, with different functions and different pressures. The pulmonary circulation is a low-pressure, high-flow system for gas exchange; the bronchial circulation is a small-volume, systemic-pressure system that keeps the airway walls and supporting tissues alive. The two systems anastomose at the level of the bronchial wall and of the visceral pleura, and this anastomosis becomes clinically important in chronic inflammation, when the bronchial arteries hypertrophy and can bleed profusely.

2.Arterial supply: the bronchial arteries

The bronchial arteries arise proximally from the aorta and pass on the posterior surfaces of the bronchi, dividing with them into the lung. They ramify within the lung and supply the bronchial walls, glands, walls of the large vessels and the visceral pleura. The pattern is not symmetrical:

  • Right bronchial artery is normally a single vessel arising from the third right posterior intercostal artery; occasionally it arises from the upper left bronchial artery.
  • Two left bronchial arteries typically arise directly from the anterior surface of the thoracic aorta — a superior one at about vertebral level T V and an inferior one below the left main bronchus.

The bronchial veins drain the proximal part of the bronchial tree: the right bronchial veins end in the azygos vein, the left bronchial veins in the accessory hemi-azygos vein or in the superior intercostal vein. A substantial fraction of the blood from the peripheral lung, however, drains through the pulmonary veins into the left atrium, so that a small amount of desaturated blood bypasses the alveoli — the physiological right-to-left shunt.

  • Pleural disease: because the visceral pleura is insensitive, pain in pleurisy arises from the parietal pleura, which is somatically innervated.

  • Which nerve constricts the bronchioles and which dilates them? — Vagus constricts; sympathetic dilates.

  • Why is the visceral pleura insensitive to pain? — It is supplied only by autonomic visceral afferents, which do not carry pain.

III.22The anatomy of the pulmonary circulation, topography and branches of the pulmonary trunk.

Core answerThe pulmonary trunk (truncus pulmonalis) arises from the conus arteriosus of the right ventricle at the pulmonary valve, inside the pericardial sac, and ascends, lying first anterior and then to the left of the ascending aorta, with which it shares a common sheath of serous pericardium. It divides into the right and left pulmonary arteries at about the level of the T V–T VI intervertebral disc, close to the left border of the sternum at the level of the third left costal cartilage and just below the bifurcation of the trachea; the ligamentum arteriosum connects the bifurcation to the aortic arch. The right pulmonary artery is longer and passes behind the ascending aorta and the superior vena cava and in front of the right main bronchus; the left is shorter, passes in front of the descending aorta and behind the superior pulmonary vein. Each side has two pulmonary veins draining into the left atrium. This is a low-pressure, low-resistance, high-flow circuit, and its obstruction by embolism or disease is the basis of pulmonary hypertension.

1.Functional anatomy of the pulmonary circulation

The pulmonary circulation carries the whole cardiac output from the right ventricle to the alveolar capillaries and returns it to the left atrium. It is a low-pressure, low-resistance system: the walls of the pulmonary arteries are thin, the capillary bed is enormous, and the flow is distributed so that the alveoli are evenly perfused. The functional consequences are that the right ventricle is a thin-walled pump for a low pressure, that the circuit is easily obstructed, and that it immediately receives everything injected into the systemic venous circulation.

The circuit runs as follows.

right ventricle → pulmonary valve → pulmonary trunk → right and left pulmonary arteries → lobar and segmental arteries → alveolar capillaries → venules → two pulmonary veins on each side → left atrium.

2.The pulmonary trunk: origin, course and relations

The pulmonary trunk begins at the pulmonary valve, at the orifice of the conus arteriosus of the right ventricle slightly anterior to the aortic orifice, and is enclosed within the pericardial sac as the most anterior of the great vessels at its origin. It ascends for a few centimetres, moving posteriorly and to the left; it lies initially anterior and then to the left of the ascending aorta, and both are surrounded by the same visceral layer of serous pericardium. Its normal calibre is about that of the aorta at its origin but its wall is distinctly thinner.

Its important relations are:

  • Right and posterior: the ascending aorta.
  • Posterior (above): the left atrium and the left auricle, with the left coronary artery and the circumflex branch passing between the trunk and the auricle.
  • Anterior: the sternum, the left pleura and lung and, at its origin, the right ventricular outflow tract.
  • Left: the left auricle and, beyond the pericardium, the left phrenic nerve which descends anterior to the root of the left lung.
  • Bifurcation: connected to the concavity of the aortic arch by the ligamentum arteriosum, the remnant of the fetal ductus arteriosus; the left recurrent laryngeal nerve hooks under the arch lateral to the ligament, and the region between the pulmonary artery and the aorta is the aortopulmonary window where enlarged nodes may compress that nerve.

The bifurcation occurs slightly inferior to the tracheal bifurcation and to its left, at about the level of the T V–T VI intervertebral disc; on a chest radiograph the left border of the pulmonary trunk forms a small convexity below the aortic knuckle (the pulmonary bay), and the main pulmonary artery is seen on CT leaving the right ventricle anterior to the ascending aorta.

On each side a superior and an inferior pulmonary vein carry oxygenated blood from the lungs. Clinical / radiological correlation

  • Pulmonary hypertension: chronic elevation of pulmonary arterial pressure causes hypertrophy of the right ventricle and eventually right heart failure; causes include chronic lung disease, left heart disease, recurrent embolism and idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension.

  • Tetralogy of Fallot: pulmonary stenosis with an overriding aorta, VSD and right ventricular hypertrophy; reduced pulmonary blood flow causes cyanosis.
  • Pulmonary artery catheterisation (Swan–Ganz) uses the right ventricular outflow tract and the pulmonary trunk to measure pressures in the right heart and the pulmonary capillary wedge pressure; the pulmonary artery is also ligated during pneumonectomy.

III.23The tributaries of the superior vena cava. The azygos-hemiazygos system. The lymphatic drainage of the thoracic cavity.

Core answerThe superior vena cava is formed behind the lower edge of the right first costal cartilage by the union of the two brachiocephalic veins, descends vertically and ends at the lower edge of the right third costal cartilage in the right atrium; its lower half lies inside the pericardial sac and it receives the azygos vein immediately before entering the pericardium. The azygos system consists of the azygos vein on the right and the hemi-azygos and accessory hemi-azygos veins on the left; they drain the thoracic wall (posterior intercostal veins) and thoracic viscera, and form an important collateral pathway between the superior and inferior venae cavae. The thoracic duct is the principal lymphatic channel of the body: it begins in the abdomen, enters the thorax through the aortic hiatus, ascends between the aorta and the azygos vein, crosses to the left at about T V and ends in the left venous angle; thoracic lymph nodes (parasternal, intercostal, diaphragmatic, posterior mediastinal and tracheobronchial) drain into the bronchomediastinal trunks and hence into the thoracic duct or right lymphatic duct.

1.The superior vena cava

The superior vena cava (vena cava superior) is a short, vertically oriented valveless trunk. It begins behind the lower edge of the right first costal cartilage where the right and left brachiocephalic veins unite, and terminates at the lower edge of the right third costal cartilage, where it opens into the right atrium. Its lower half lies within the pericardial sac, and therefore in the middle mediastinum; the upper half belongs to the superior mediastinum. The azygos vein joins it immediately before it enters the pericardial sac, and it also receives pericardial and mediastinal veins. On a chest radiograph it forms the right upper border of the mediastinal shadow.

Its relations are: the right pleura and lung laterally, the ascending aorta and the right pulmonary artery medially, the right phrenic nerve and the pericardiophrenic vessels lying on its right side within the fibrous pericardium, and the tracheobronchial nodes medially.

2.The brachiocephalic veins and the tributaries of the superior vena cava

Each brachiocephalic vein is formed behind the sternoclavicular joint by the union of the internal jugular and subclavian veins. The left brachiocephalic vein is much longer than the right because it crosses the midline anterior to the branches of the aortic arch; the right is short and vertical. 4.The thoracic duct and the right lymphatic duct

The thoracic duct is the principal lymphatic channel of the body. It begins in the abdomen as a confluence of lymph trunks, sometimes dilated into a cisterna chyli, and extends from about vertebra L II to the root of the neck. It enters the thorax through the aortic hiatus posterior to the aorta, ascends through the posterior mediastinum between the thoracic aorta on the left and the azygos vein on the right, posterior to the oesophagus and the diaphragm and anterior to the vertebral bodies. At about vertebral level T V it moves to the left of the midline, enters the superior mediastinum, passes posterior to the aortic arch and between the oesophagus and the left mediastinal pleura, and ends — after being joined by the left jugular and left subclavian trunks — at the junction of the left subclavian and left internal jugular veins (the left venous angle).

The thoracic duct receives: the abdominal confluence of lymph trunks; descending thoracic lymph trunks draining the lower six or seven intercostal spaces on both sides; upper intercostal trunks draining the upper left intercostal spaces; ducts from the posterior mediastinal and posterior diaphragmatic nodes; and, at its termination, the left bronchomediastinal trunk, the left jugular trunk and the left subclavian trunk. III.24The topography of the vagus nerve in the thoracic cavity. The anatomy and function of the phrenic nerve.

Core answerThe vagus nerve [X] enters the thorax through the superior thoracic aperture and descends in the superior and posterior mediastinum on each side. The right vagus crosses the right subclavian artery (giving the right recurrent laryngeal nerve) and then the right lateral surface of the trachea and the posterior aspect of the root of the right lung; the left vagus crosses the left side of the aortic arch (giving the left recurrent laryngeal nerve lateral to the ligamentum arteriosum) and the posterior aspect of the root of the left lung. Both then form the oesophageal plexus, which condenses into an anterior vagal trunk (mainly left) and a posterior vagal trunk (mainly right) that pass through the oesophageal hiatus into the abdomen. The vagus supplies parasympathetic fibres and visceral afferents to the thoracic viscera. The phrenic nerve (C3–C5, mainly C4) descends on the anterior scalene muscle, enters the thorax between the subclavian vein and artery, passes anterior to the root of the lung on the side of the pericardium, and is the sole motor supply to the diaphragm; it also carries sensation from the pericardium and the mediastinal and central diaphragmatic pleura.

1.Origin and cervical course of the vagus

The vagus nerve leaves the medulla through the jugular foramen together with the glossopharyngeal and accessory nerves, and descends in the carotid sheath between the internal jugular vein and the internal carotid (then common carotid) artery, posterolateral to them. It gives the auricular branch, the pharyngeal branches, the superior laryngeal nerve and the cardiac branches in the neck. It then passes through the superior thoracic aperture into the superior mediastinum, where its course differs on the two sides.

4.The phrenic nerve: origin and course

The phrenic nerve is a somatic nerve formed from the anterior rami of C3, C4 and C5, mainly C4. It forms on the surface of the anterior scalene muscle in the neck, descends within the prevertebral fascia deep to the sternocleidomastoid, and enters the thorax through the superior thoracic aperture between the subclavian vein and the subclavian artery. From there it descends anterior to the root of the lung on each side, accompanied by the pericardiacophrenic vessels, and runs on the side of the fibrous pericardium to reach the diaphragm.

  • Right phrenic nerve: lies lateral to the right vagus and lateral and slightly posterior to the beginning of the right brachiocephalic vein; descends along the right side of the SVC, enters the middle mediastinum, passes along the right side of the pericardial sac within the fibrous pericardium and anterior to the root of the right lung; it leaves the thorax by passing through the diaphragm with the inferior vena cava at the caval opening (T VIII).
  • Left phrenic nerve: lies lateral to the left vagus and lateral and slightly posterior to the left brachiocephalic vein; descends across the left lateral surface of the aortic arch, passing superficially to the left vagus and the left superior intercostal vein; then follows the left side of the pericardium, anterior to the root of the left lung, and pierces the diaphragm near the apex of the heart.

The key differential is therefore simple: the phrenic nerve is anterior to the lung root, the vagus is posterior to it.

5.Function of the phrenic nerve and its clinical anatomy

The phrenic nerve provides the entire motor innervation of the diaphragm, including the crura, and supplies somatic sensory fibres to the mediastinal pleura, the fibrous and parietal serous pericardium, the central part of the diaphragmatic pleura and the peritoneum on the central part of the diaphragm. The peripheral part of the diaphragm receives additional sensory fibres from the lower intercostal nerves. This double sensory supply explains the two patterns of referred pain: central diaphragmatic irritation produces shoulder-tip pain (C3–C5 dermatomes), while peripheral irritation produces pain over the lower thorax or upper abdomen.

III.25Organization of the thoracic part of the sympathetic chain.

Core answerThe sympathetic trunk (truncus sympathicus) is a paired paravertebral chain of ganglia running from the skull base to the coccyx, and its thoracic part lies in the posterior mediastinum, under the parietal pleura. In the thorax it consists of about 11 to 12 ganglia, numbered with the thoracic spinal nerves, connected to those nerves by white rami communicantes (preganglionic, from T1 to L2) and grey rami communicantes (postganglionic). Each ganglion gives medial branches: the upper five give postganglionic cardiopulmonary splanchnic nerves to the cardiac and pulmonary plexuses, and the lower seven give the preganglionic greater, lesser and least splanchnic nerves, which pass through the crura of the diaphragm to the coeliac, aorticorenal and renal plexuses. It is continuous above with the cervical and below with the lumbar trunk, and it carries both sympathetic efferents and visceral afferents, including the pain fibres of the heart.

1.General organisation and position

The sympathetic trunks are two parallel cords of ganglionated nerve tissue, one on each side of the vertebral column, extending from the base of the skull to the coccyx. In the neck they lie behind the carotid sheath; in the thorax they are the most lateral of the posterior mediastinal structures, lying on the heads of the ribs in the upper part of the thorax and becoming progressively more medial below, until they rest on the lateral aspect of the vertebral bodies. In the superior part of the posterior mediastinum they lie anterior to the necks of the ribs; throughout their course they are covered by the parietal pleura. They leave the thorax by passing behind the diaphragm under the medial arcuate ligament or through the crura of the diaphragm, and continue as the lumbar sympathetic trunks.

The thoracic part contains 11 or 12 ganglia — 11 or 12 because the first thoracic ganglion may be fused with the inferior cervical ganglion to form the stellate (cervicothoracic) ganglion, and the lowest thoracic ganglion may be fused with the first lumbar. The ganglia are numbered with the thoracic spinal nerve with which they are associated (T1 to T12).

2.Rami communicantes: white and grey

Each thoracic sympathetic ganglion is connected to the corresponding anterior ramus of the thoracic spinal nerve by two small roots whose difference is a classic examination question.

3.Branches of the thoracic sympathetic ganglia

The medial branches are of two functionally different types.

  • Cardiopulmonary splanchnic nerves — small postganglionic branches from the upper five thoracic ganglia; they join the cardiac plexus and the pulmonary plexuses and supply the heart, lungs and thoracic vessels. They contain visceral afferents as well. They are called 'splanchnic' in the sense of viscera, but they are postganglionic.
  • Thoracic splanchnic nerves — the greater, lesser and least splanchnic nerves, formed by branches of the lower seven ganglia. These are large and are composed mainly of preganglionic fibres, together with visceral afferents; they pass through the crura of the diaphragm to relay in the abdominal prevertebral ganglia. The name is a warning against assuming that every splanchnic nerve is postganglionic.

4.Functions of the thoracic sympathetic outflow

The sympathetic outflow to the trunk and limbs arises from the T1 to L2 segments; the thoracic chain distributes it to three groups of targets:

  • Thoracic viscera: acceleration of the heart, increased contractility and conduction velocity, dilatation of the bronchioles, inhibition of bronchial secretion, and vasoconstriction of the thoracic vessels; the coronary arteries are influenced through the sympathetic nerves.
  • Body wall: vasoconstriction of the skin and muscle vessels (the basis of thermoregulation), sudomotor secretion of sweat glands and pilomotor contraction of the arrector pili; these reach the wall through the grey rami and the somatic nerve branches.
  • Abdominal viscera through the splanchnic nerves: vasoconstriction of the splanchnic bed, inhibition of gastrointestinal motility and secretion, relaxation of the sphincters, glycogenolysis and lipolysis, release of catecholamines from the adrenal medulla, and reduced renal blood flow with renin release.

Because the sympathetic outflow is segmentally organised, the effects on the body wall are also segmental: a sympathetic block or a lesion produces a band of dry, warm skin with loss of sweating, and this is used diagnostically in sympathectomy.

5.Visceral afferents and referred pain

The thoracic sympathetic trunk also carries visceral afferent fibres, which travel from the thoracic viscera through the sympathetic chain and the white rami communicantes to the cell bodies in the upper thoracic dorsal root ganglia. These are the fibres that conduct pain from the heart, aorta and lungs. Their segmental level explains the segmental pattern of referred cardiac pain: ischaemia of the heart is felt over the anterior chest, the left shoulder and the inner side of the left arm, in the dermatomes of the upper thoracic segments, because the visceral afferents enter the same segments as the somatic afferents of those dermatomes. Pain from the oesophagus and from the thoracic aorta is referred in the same manner. By contrast, the vagal afferents of the thoracic viscera are involved in reflex control and do not transmit pain.

Must-memorise

  • Position: anterior to the necks of the ribs above, on the lateral aspect of the vertebral bodies below, covered by the parietal pleura.
  • White rami: T1–L2, preganglionic, myelinated. Grey rami: all levels, postganglionic, unmyelinated.
  • Upper five thoracic ganglia: postganglionic cardiopulmonary splanchnic nerves to the cardiac and pulmonary plexuses.

  • Cardiac pain is referred to T1–T5 dermatomes; the afferents travel with the sympathetic nerves, not the vagus.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Horner syndrome: interruption of the sympathetic pathway anywhere from the hypothalamus to the T1 level produces ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis and enophthalmos. In the thorax it may be caused by a Pancoast tumour of the lung apex invading the sympathetic chain at T1.
  • Sympathectomy and sympathectomy-like procedures: used for palmar and axillary hyperhidrosis, Raynaud phenomenon, refractory angina (cardiac sympathectomy) and some forms of complex regional pain syndrome; compensatory sweating is the commonest side effect.
  • Stellate ganglion block: interrupts the sympathetic supply of the head, neck and upper limb and is used in complex regional pain syndrome, Raynaud syndrome and hyperhidrosis; the needle is placed at the level of the C6 transverse process.
  • Coeliac plexus (splanchnic) block: used to relieve the pain of upper abdominal malignancy, especially pancreatic cancer, by interrupting the greater and lesser splanchnic nerves before they relay.
  • Referred cardiac pain: myocardial ischaemia produces pain in the chest, left shoulder and left arm because the visceral afferents enter the upper thoracic segments, where they converge with somatic afferents.
  • Autonomic dysreflexia in high spinal cord injury: an unbalanced sympathetic discharge below the lesion causes severe hypertension and headache.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Why are there white rami only from T1 to L2? — Because the sympathetic preganglionic neurons are located only in the intermediolateral cell columns of T1–L2; above and below these levels the trunk contains postganglionic fibres distributed through grey rami.
  • Where do the greater splanchnic nerves synapse? — In the coeliac ganglion, having arisen from T5 to T9/T10 and passed through the crus of the diaphragm.
  • What is the difference between the cardiopulmonary splanchnic nerves and the thoracic splanchnic nerves? — Cardiopulmonary: small, postganglionic, from the upper five ganglia. Splanchnic: large, preganglionic, from the lower seven ganglia, to the abdominal prevertebral ganglia.
  • Where does the thoracic sympathetic trunk leave the thorax? — Posterior to the diaphragm, under the medial arcuate ligament or through the crura, continuing as the lumbar trunk.
  • Which structures cover the sympathetic trunk in the thorax? — The parietal pleura; this is why pleural disease may irritate the trunk, and why the trunk is encountered when the pleura is stripped.
  • Why is a Pancoast tumour associated with Horner syndrome? — It invades the sympathetic trunk at the T1 level in the apex of the thorax, interrupting the oculosympathetic pathway.

III.26General embryology I: fertilization, implantation, cleavage, gastrulation, development of the neuroectoderm. Differentiation of intraembryonic mesoderm.

Core answerFertilisation is the fusion of a spermatozoon with a secondary oocyte in the ampulla of the uterine tube; it restores the diploid chromosome number (46) and determines the sex of the embryo. Cleavage converts the zygote into a morula (day 3) and then into a blastocyst (day 4–5), which implants in the endometrium from about day 6 and is completely embedded by about day 11–12. In the third week gastrulation through the primitive streak turns the bilaminar disc into a trilaminar disc (ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm) and creates the notochord, and the induced neuroectoderm folds into the neural tube between about day 18 and the end of the fourth week. The intraembryonic mesoderm then separates into paraxial, intermediate and lateral plate mesoderm — the somites, the urogenital primordia, and the serous membranes, gut wall and body wall.

1.The timetable of the first four weeks

The prenatal period runs from fertilisation to birth and is divided into blastogenesis (the first two weeks), the embryonic period (fertilisation to the end of the eighth week, when the primordia of all organ systems are laid down) and the fetal period (from the beginning of the ninth week, when the organs grow and mature). Two clinical rules follow from this division: in blastogenesis the insult is usually all or nothing (miscarriage or an intact embryo), while the embryonic period is the phase most sensitive to teratogens, because the organ primordia are being formed.

2.Fertilisation

Fertilisation (fertilisatio) is the fusion of a haploid spermatozoon with a haploid secondary oocyte to form a diploid zygote (zygota). It normally takes place in the ampulla of the uterine tube, the dilated continuation of the infundibulum, within about a day of ovulation.

  • Oocyte: a secondary oocyte arrested in metaphase of meiosis II, surrounded by the zona pellucida and the corona radiata of follicular cells.
  • Capacitation: in the female genital tract the spermatozoon must be capacitated by factors of the tubal secretion before its acrosome can react; without capacitation it cannot fertilise the oocyte.
  • Penetration and acrosome reaction: the sperm passes through the corona radiata; the acrosome reaction releases hydrolytic enzymes that digest a path through the zona pellucida, and the sperm membrane fuses with the oolemma.
  • Completion of meiosis: the oocyte completes meiosis II and emits the second polar body; the male and female pronuclei approach each other and fuse.
  • Block to polyspermy: the cortical reaction immediately changes the zona pellucida (zona reaction), so that no further sperm can enter.
  • Result: the zygote has 46 chromosomes; the sex is determined at fertilisation by the sperm (X or Y), the oocyte always contributing an X.

3.Cleavage, the blastocyst and implantation

Cleavage is a series of mitotic divisions in which the total mass of the conceptus does not increase — the daughter cells (blastomeres) become smaller at each division. The intact zona pellucida prevents the conceptus from adhering prematurely to the tubal wall. By day 3 there are 8–16 blastomeres forming the morula (morula), which reaches the uterine cavity on day 4. Fluid then enters and cavitation produces the blastocyst (blastocystis): an outer layer of trophoblast and an eccentric inner cell mass, the embryoblast, with the fluid-filled blastocoele between them. The trophoblast gives the chorion and the fetal part of the placenta and induces the maternal part; the embryoblast gives the embryo, the amnion, the yolk sac and the allantois.

4.Gastrulation and the notochord

At the beginning of the third week the human embryo is a bilaminar disc: the dorsal epiblast (adjacent to the amniotic cavity) and the ventral hypoblast (facing the yolk sac). Gastrulation begins with the appearance, in the caudal midline of the epiblast, of the primitive streak (linea primitiva), whose cranial end carries the primitive node. Epiblast cells proliferate, migrate to the streak and ingress:

Gastrulation: epiblast → primitive streak and node → endoderm (replaces the hypoblast) + intraembryonic mesoderm (between ectoderm and endoderm) → remaining epiblast = ectoderm. The disc is now trilaminar.

  • Notochord (notochorda): a rod of cells growing cranially from the primitive node (notochordal process → notochordal plate → definitive notochord). It is the primary inducer of the neural plate, patterns the paraxial mesoderm into somites, and later becomes the nucleus pulposus of the intervertebral discs.
  • Oropharyngeal and cloacal membranes: small bilaminar areas where ectoderm and endoderm remain in contact without intervening mesoderm; they give the future mouth and the anal membrane.
  • Allantois: a small finger-like diverticulum of the hindgut that grows into the connecting stalk; its intra-embryonic remains become the urachus and its vessels become the umbilical vessels.

5.Neurulation and the development of the neuroectoderm

The neuroectoderm is the part of the ectoderm induced by the notochord. Its development is neurulation: first the ectoderm thickens into the neural plate (lamina neuralis) on about day 18, then its margins rise into neural folds while the median neural groove deepens. The folds fuse in the midline, beginning on about day 21 at the cervical level, and fusion spreads both cranially and caudally. The two open ends are the rostral (anterior) neuropore, which closes on about day 24, and the caudal (posterior) neuropore, which closes two to three days later; the neural tube is complete by the end of the fourth week.

Neurulation: neural plate → neural groove → neural folds → fusion starts at the cervical level → spreads cranially and caudally → rostral neuropore closes → caudal neuropore closes → neural tube.

  • Neural tube (tubus neuralis): the cranial part dilates into the three primary brain vesicles (prosencephalon, mesencephalon, rhombencephalon) by the end of the fourth week and into five secondary vesicles (telencephalon, diencephalon, mesencephalon, metencephalon, myelencephalon) by the end of the fifth week. The caudal part becomes the spinal cord; the lumen of the tube persists as the ventricles of the brain and the central canal of the spinal cord.
  • Neural crest (crista neuralis): cells set free from the margins of the neural folds as the tube closes. They migrate widely and form all neurons outside the CNS (spinal and autonomic ganglia), the adrenal medulla, Schwann cells, both leptomeninges (pia and arachnoid), melanocytes, Merkel cells, and the ectomesenchyme of the head and neck; the cardiac neural crest forms the aortopulmonary septum.
  • Surface ectoderm: as the tube sinks below the surface, the remaining ectoderm closes over it and becomes the epidermis, together with the lens and the inner ear.

6.Differentiation of the intraembryonic mesoderm

The intraembryonic mesoderm is at first a sheet on either side of the notochord. It becomes arranged in three longitudinal bands, each with a different fate:

  • Paraxial mesoderm (mesoderma paraxialis): thickens beside the neural tube and becomes segmented into somites (somiti), which are added craniocaudally from about the end of the third week. Each somite divides into a sclerotome (vertebrae and ribs), a myotome (skeletal muscle) and a dermatome (dermis of the back). The unsegmented paraxial mesoderm of the head and neck forms the muscles attached to the branchial skeleton.
  • Intermediate mesoderm (mesoderma intermedium): the urogenital band — nephric primordia (pronephros, mesonephros, metanephros), gonads and genital ducts.
  • Lateral plate mesoderm (mesoderma laminae lateralis): splits into a somatic (parietal) layer applied to the ectoderm and a splanchnic (visceral) layer applied to the endoderm, with the intraembryonic coelom between them. The somatic layer+ectoderm build the body wall and the parietal serous membranes; the splanchnic layer+endoderm build the gut wall and the visceral serous membranes; the coelom becomes the pericardial, pleural and peritoneal cavities.

  • Fertilisation normally in the ampulla of the uterine tube; the zygote is diploid (46) and the sex is fixed by the sperm.
  • Trophoblast → cytotrophoblast (Langhans cells, mitotic stem cells) + syncytiotrophoblast (invasive, hCG); embryoblast → embryo, amnion, yolk sac, allantois.
  • Gastrulation: primitive streak and node are the only source of the three germ layers; the notochord induces the neural plate and the somites and becomes the nucleus pulposus.
  • Neural tube → CNS (three brain vesicles by week 4, five by week 5; spinal cord); neural crest → PNS neurons, Schwann cells, adrenal medulla, leptomeninges, melanocytes, head ectomesenchyme.
  • Mesoderm: paraxial → somites → sclerotome (bone), myotome (muscle), dermatome (dermis); intermediate → urogenital; lateral plate → serous membranes, gut wall, coelom.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Ectopic pregnancy (graviditas extrauterina): implantation outside the uterine cavity, most often in the uterine tube, less often on the ovary, on the peritoneum or in the cervix. It threatens the mother through bleeding and tubal rupture, and must be excluded in any woman of fertile age with sudden acute abdominal pain.
  • Placenta praevia: if the blastocyst implants near the cervical os, the placenta forms in the lower uterine segment and obstructs the birth canal; it is a contraindication to vaginal delivery.
  • Teratogens produce malformations mainly in the embryonic period (weeks 3–8); radiation, infections and drugs acting during blastogenesis often follow the all-or-nothing rule.
  • Neural tube defects: failure of the neuropores to close — anencephaly when the rostral neuropore remains open, spina bifida when the caudal neuropore fails; periconceptional folate reduces the risk.
  • Twinning: dizygotic twins arise from two zygotes and are always dichorionic and diamniotic; monozygotic twins arise from one zygote and the number of amnions and chorions depends on how early the split occurs.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Where and when does fertilisation occur, and what does it restore? — Ampulla of the uterine tube, within about 24 hours of ovulation; it restores the diploid chromosome number and determines sex.
  • What is capacitation and what happens without it? — Conditioning of the sperm in the female tract that enables the acrosome reaction; without it the sperm cannot penetrate the zona pellucida.
  • What blocks polyspermy? — The cortical reaction (zona reaction).
  • Difference between cytotrophoblast and syncytiotrophoblast? — Mitotic stem cells with glycogen versus a mitotically inactive, invasive, hormone-producing syncytium.
  • What induces the neural plate, and what is the fate of the notochord? — The notochord; it persists as the nucleus pulposus of the intervertebral discs.
  • Which germ layer gives the skeleton, which the epidermis, which the gut epithelium? — Mesoderm, ectoderm and endoderm respectively.
  • What is a somite and what are its three parts? — A segment of the paraxial mesoderm dividing into sclerotome, myotome and dermatome.

III.27General embryology II: development of the amnion, yolk sacs, extraembryonic mesoderm. Formation of the placenta.

Core answerThe extraembryonic mesoderm splits into a somatic layer (lining the trophoblast and the amnion) and a splanchnic layer (covering the yolk sac), and the space between them becomes the chorionic cavity. The amnion is a thin membrane enclosing the embryo in amniotic fluid, and the yolk sac provides early nutrition, the first blood cells and the primordial germ cells. The chorion — syncytiotrophoblast, cytotrophoblast and extraembryonic mesenchyme — forms the chorionic villi, which pass from primary to secondary to tertiary; over the decidua basalis they become the chorion frondosum and form the fetal part of the placenta, while the decidua basalis forms the maternal part. The term placenta is a disc of 15–25 cm diameter and 400–600 g with 15–30 cotyledons, and it is hemochorionic: maternal blood bathes the villi but never mixes with fetal blood, which is separated from it by the placental barrier. The umbilical cord connects fetus and placenta and contains two umbilical arteries, one umbilical vein and Wharton's jelly.

2.Extraembryonic mesoderm and body folding

The extraembryonic mesoderm (mesoderma extraembryonicum) is a loose population of cells derived from the epiblast that fills the space between the trophoblast externally and the amnion and yolk sac internally. Cavities appear in it and coalesce into the chorionic cavity (extraembryonic coelom), which is completed on about day 13. The mesoderm is thus split into two layers:

  • Somatic (parietal) extraembryonic mesoderm: applied to the inner surface of the trophoblast and to the amnion; with the trophoblast it forms the chorion, and it later carries the vessels of the chorionic plate.
  • Splanchnic (visceral) extraembryonic mesoderm: covers the yolk sac and, with the endoderm, forms its wall; it is the site where the first blood islands and vessels appear.

Body folding follows in the third and fourth weeks. The flat trilaminar disc grows faster than its margins, so cranial, caudal and lateral folds turn it ventrally. The amnion expands with it, the yolk sac is constricted at its stalk into the narrow vitelline duct, and the proximal yolk sac plus the allantois are pushed into the connecting stalk that suspends the embryo in the chorionic cavity. The intraembryonic coelom becomes the body cavities, the amnion fuses with the chorion to form the amniochorion, and the fetal membranes are complete.

3.The amnion, amniotic fluid and the yolk sacs

3.1 Amnion

The amnion is the innermost fetal membrane. It arises from the embryoblast on about day 7–8, when a slit appears within the embryoblast (the epiblast): the cells lining the slit become amnioblasts, the cavity comes to lie between the embryoblast and the trophoblast, and the membrane around it is the amnion. Its wall is a simple cuboidal to squamous amniotic epithelium on a thin extraembryonic mesodermal layer. As the amniotic cavity enlarges it encloses the whole embryo, covers the fetal surface of the placenta and forms the outer coat of the umbilical cord. The fluid it contains (amniotic fluid) is at first a secretion of the amniotic epithelium and later is mainly fetal urine; it permits free movement and symmetrical growth, prevents adhesion of the embryo to the membranes, cushions it against mechanical injury and maintains a constant temperature. Too little fluid (oligohydramnios) or too much (polyhydramnios) is an important obstetric warning sign, and amniotic fluid cells are sampled at amniocentesis for prenatal diagnosis.

3.2 Yolk sacs

  • Primary yolk sac (saccus vitellinus primarius): the cavity bordered by the hypoblast above and by Heuser's membrane below, in the second week.
  • Secondary (definitive) yolk sac (saccus vitellinus): appears when the extraembryonic coelom splits the extraembryonic mesoderm; it is the smaller, definitive sac attached to the midgut by the vitelline duct.
  • Functions: early nutrition (before the placenta works), the site of the first blood islands and of early blood formation, the route by which primordial germ cells reach the gonadal ridges, and the source of the allantois (whose intra-embryonic part becomes the urachus and whose vessels become the umbilical vessels).
  • Fate: the yolk sac shrinks and is incorporated into the umbilical cord as a small remnant. Persistence of the vitelline duct gives a Meckel's diverticulum of the ileum.

4.Chorion, decidua and the chorionic villi

The chorion (chorion) is the outermost fetal membrane. It has three layers: the outer syncytiotrophoblast, the cytotrophoblast and the extraembryonic mesenchyme with the vessels of the embryo. Its cavity is the chorionic cavity, and its outer surface bears the chorionic villi (villi chorionici), which grow into the surrounding decidua. Two parts are distinguished by the number of villi: the chorion frondosum (bushy chorion over the embryo, with long branched villi; the future fetal placenta) and the chorion laeve (smooth chorion, whose villi disappear after the eighth week).

5.The placenta: architecture, circulation and barrier

The placenta is a temporary organ of mixed origin, part fetal and part maternal. The mature organ is a disc of 15–25 cm diameter, 2–3 cm thickness and 400–600 g weight. Its fetal surface is smooth, covered by amnion, with the fetal vessels converging towards the insertion of the umbilical cord; its maternal surface is rough and granular and is divided into 15–30 cotyledons.

  • Fetal part: the chorionic plate (extraembryonic mesenchyme) covered by amniotic epithelium, from which the tertiary villi grow towards the decidua. The villi are either anchoring villi (fixed to the decidual plate) or free villi (floating in maternal blood). The villous core contains loose connective tissue, capillaries, fibroblasts, myofibroblasts and Hofbauer cells (placental macrophages).
  • Maternal part: the decidual plate, from which placental septa grow towards the fetal part. The septa do not reach the chorionic plate, so they do not completely divide the intervillous space; they mark out the cotyledons.
  • Intervillous space (spatium intervillosum): contains about 150 mL of maternal blood, renewed by the spiral arteries at about 500 mL per minute. The maternal circulation is open (blood flows freely around the villi) while the fetal circulation is closed (umbilical arteries → villous capillaries → umbilical vein). Because maternal blood directly contacts the trophoblast, the human placenta is hemochorionic.
  • Functions: exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide, transfer of nutrients, removal of urea, uric acid, creatinine and bilirubin, an endocrine function (hCG and human chorionic somatomammotropin from the syncytiotrophoblast, progesterone and oestrogens from the trophoblast, relaxin from the decidual cells) and a barrier function. The placenta takes over progesterone production from the corpus luteum from the second trimester onwards.

6.The umbilical cord

The umbilical cord (funiculus umbilicalis) runs from the umbilicus of the fetus through the amniotic cavity to the fetal surface of the placenta. It is covered externally by a simple squamous to cuboidal amniotic epithelium. Its core is Wharton's jelly, a mucous connective tissue rich in hyaluronic acid with fibroblasts and macrophages, which protects the vessels from compression. At term the cord is about 2 cm wide and 50 cm long, roughly matching the length of the fetus.

Must-memorise
  • Chorionic cavity completed about day 13; amniotic cavity about day 7–8; tertiary villi from about day 21; placenta fully developed by the fourth month.
  • Villi: primary (trophoblast only) → secondary (mesenchymal core) → tertiary (vessels). Anchoring = fixed to the decidual plate; free = floating in the intervillous space.
  • Chorion frondosum + decidua basalis = placenta; chorion laeve = smooth chorion facing the capsular decidua.
  • Placenta: disc 15–25 cm, 2–3 cm thick, 400–600 g, 15–30 cotyledons; intervillous space about 150 mL; hemochorionic.
  • Placental barrier: 6 layers early, 2–3 at term (cytotrophoblast becomes discontinuous; syncytiotrophoblast and capillary endothelium remain).
  • Cord: 2 arteries, 1 vein, Wharton's jelly, amniotic epithelium; about 2 cm wide and 50 cm long.
  • hCG from the syncytiotrophoblast maintains the corpus luteum; the placenta takes over progesterone in the second trimester.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Placenta praevia: implantation near the internal os; the placenta covers or approaches the birth canal and is a contraindication to vaginal delivery.
  • Placenta accreta, increta, percreta: the villi attach to the myometrium, invade it, or pass through the whole uterine wall; a major cause of postpartum haemorrhage, usually with previous caesarean section.
  • Placental abruption: premature separation of the placenta, with vaginal bleeding and painful uterine contractions.
  • Gestational trophoblastic disease (hydatidiform mole, choriocarcinoma) arises from trophoblast cells; hCG in blood or urine diagnoses and monitors it.
  • Amniocentesis and cell-free fetal DNA: sampling of amniotic fluid or of free fetal DNA in maternal blood allows prenatal karyotyping, Rh determination and mutation analysis.
  • Cord abnormalities: a single umbilical artery is associated with other anomalies; true knots and cord prolapse compromise the fetal circulation.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Which structures form the placenta, and what are its two parts? — Chorion frondosum (fetal part: chorionic plate and tertiary villi) and decidua basalis (maternal part: decidual plate and septa).
  • How do primary, secondary and tertiary villi differ? — By the presence of a mesodermal core (secondary) and then of vessels (tertiary).
  • Why do maternal and fetal blood not mix? — The placental barrier; the placenta is hemochorionic but the two circulations are separate.
  • Name the layers of the placental barrier and say what happens to them at term. — Syncytiotrophoblast, cytotrophoblast and its basal lamina, villous connective tissue, capillary endothelium and its basal lamina; at term the cytotrophoblast becomes discontinuous and some basal laminae fuse, leaving 2–3 layers.
  • What does the umbilical cord contain and where do its vessels come from? — Two umbilical arteries (internal iliac) and one umbilical vein, Wharton's jelly and amniotic epithelium.
  • What is the function of the ductus venosus? — It bypasses the hepatic sinusoids, carrying oxygenated umbilical blood to the inferior vena cava.
  • What remains of the yolk sac and allantois in the adult? — Meckel's diverticulum (vitelline duct) and the urachus (median umbilical ligament) respectively.
