General Anatomy and the Upper Limb

Bone and joint architecture, the biology of muscle and tendon, and the complete regional anatomy of the shoulder girdle, arm, forearm and hand — joints, muscles, vessels, nerves, fascial spaces and radiology.

28 topics · Condensed edition

I.1General features and classification of bones. Types of ossification.

Core answer A bone is a composite organ built of bone tissue plus cartilage, marrow, vessels, nerves and periosteum. Bones are classified by shape (long, short, flat, irregular, sesamoid, pneumatic), by structure (compact and spongy) and by development (membranous, cartilaginous, mixed). Bone tissue forms by two mechanisms — intramembranous (desmal) ossification directly from mesenchyme and endochondral (chondral) ossification on a hyaline cartilage model; both add bone by apposition, and endochondral ossification additionally grows by interstitial proliferation at the epiphyseal plate.

Bone as a tissue and as an organ; functions

  • Mechanical — support of the body, protection of viscera (skull, thorax, pelvis), and levers on which muscles act.
  • Metabolic — the body's calcium and phosphate reservoir; about 99 % of body calcium is stored in bone as hydroxyapatite and is exchanged continuously with the plasma.
  • Haemopoietic — red marrow of spongy bone produces red cells, white cells and platelets; yellow marrow is a fat depot.
  • Other — conduction of sound (ossicles), and shaping of the body contour.

Intramembranous (desmal) ossification

  1. Mesenchyme condenses in a highly vascular membrane; mesenchymal cells express the osteogenic transcription factors Runx2 and osterix and differentiate into osteoblasts.
  2. Osteoblasts secrete osteoid, which calcifies; spicules of primary bone form trabeculae — this is the primary ossification centre.
  3. Trabeculae thicken and fuse; some osteoblasts are trapped in lacunae and become osteocytes, connected through canaliculi.
  4. The surfaces consolidate into compact plates (tabulae) on the outside and inside, with diploë between them.

Endochondral (chondral) ossification

  1. A miniature hyaline cartilage model forms from mesenchyme; its perichondrium becomes vascularised and turns into periosteum.
  2. Osteoblasts lay down a subperiosteal bone collar (intramembranous formation) around the middle of the model; the collar cuts off diffusion, so the central chondrocytes hypertrophy, die and the matrix calcifies.
  3. Osteoclasts perforate the collar; an osteogenic bud of vessels and osteoprogenitor cells invades the calcified cartilage, and bone is deposited on the calcified cartilage septa — the primary ossification centre in the diaphysis.
  4. Osteoclasts enlarge the cavity while the collar grows towards the ends; the diaphysis is replaced by bone.

Must-memorise
  • Six shape classes: long, short, flat, irregular, sesamoid, pneumatic — plus accessory bones as variants.
  • Only two ossification mechanisms: desmal from mesenchyme, chondral on a cartilage model; appositional growth everywhere, interstitial growth only in the epiphyseal plate.
  • Epiphyseal plate has five zones; the plate is the site of growth in length and of growth-plate fractures.
  • Periosteum = fibrous outer layer + cellular inner (cambium) layer; it provides appositional growth, callus and cortical blood supply, and it is very sensitive.
  • Primary (woven) bone is replaced everywhere except at tendon insertions and tooth alveoli.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Fracture healing: haematoma → granulation tissue → soft (fibrocartilaginous) callus → hard callus of woven bone (intramembranous and endochondral) → remodelling into lamellar bone along lines of stress.
  • Rickets / osteomalacia: defective mineralisation with a widened, irregular epiphyseal plate and bowing of weight-bearing bones; in adults the same defect is osteomalacia.
Likely follow-up questions
  • What is the difference between an epiphysis, a metaphysis and an apophysis? — The apophysis contributes neither to the articular surface nor to growth in length.
  • Why does a fractured bone unite but a destroyed articular surface heal poorly? — Periosteum and endosteum provide osteoprogenitor cells; articular cartilage is avascular and has no perichondrium.

I.2Connections between bones. Classification and general description of joints.

Core answer A joint (articulatio) is any connection between skeletal elements. There are two great classes: solid (continuous) joints, in which the surfaces are united by connective tissue or cartilage with no cavity, and synovial (cavitated) joints, in which the surfaces are covered by hyaline cartilage and separated by a fluid-filled cavity. Solid joints are fibrous (suture, syndesmosis, gomphosis) or cartilaginous (synchondrosis, symphysis), and may ossify into a synostosis. Synovial joints consist of articular cartilage, capsule, cavity with synovial fluid, ligaments and accessory structures, and are classified by the shape of their surfaces and by the number of axes of movement.

Blood supply, innervation and clinical anatomy

Must-memorise
  • Two structural classes: solid (fibrous, cartilaginous, bony synostosis) and synovial (cavitated).
  • Fibrous: suture, syndesmosis, gomphosis. Cartilaginous: synchondrosis, symphysis.
  • Six components of a synovial joint: articular cartilage, capsule (fibrous + synovial), cavity with synovial fluid, ligaments, articular disc, meniscus.
  • Seven shapes: plane, hinge, pivot, bicondylar, condylar (ellipsoid), saddle, ball and socket.
  • Hilton's law: the nerve of the muscle moving a joint also supplies the joint and the overlying skin.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Osteoarthritis: loss of articular cartilage with subchondral sclerosis, osteophytes, cysts and narrowing of the radiological joint space.
  • Rheumatoid arthritis: primary destruction of the synovial membrane with pannus formation and secondary erosion of cartilage.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Why are joint surfaces not in direct contact? — Hyaline articular cartilage is interposed; it distributes load and is nourished by synovial fluid.
  • What is the difference between a disc and a meniscus? — A disc divides the cavity completely; a meniscus is an incomplete crescent.

I.3General features of skeletal muscles and fasciae. Types of muscles. Sensory and motor innervation of muscles.

Core answer Skeletal muscle is the active organ of movement: striated, voluntary, multinucleated fibres arranged in fascicles, invested by three connective tissue sheaths and attached to bone through tendons or aponeuroses. It accounts for about 40 % of body mass, has its own blood and nerve supply and works in lever systems between an origin (fixed point) and an insertion (mobile point). Fasciae are superficial, deep and subserous. Muscles are classified by shape, by number of heads or bellies and by function. Each muscle receives somatic motor fibres (alpha and gamma motoneurons, forming motor units), sensory fibres (muscle spindles, tendon organs) and autonomic vasomotor fibres.

The skeletal muscle as an organ; general terminology

  • Origin (origo) — the more proximal, relatively fixed attachment; insertion (insertio) — the more distal attachment that is moved. The terms fixed point and mobile point are preferred, because in some movements they reverse (e.g. climbing).
  • Venter (belly) — the fleshy, contractile part; tendon (tendo) — dense regular connective tissue continuing the sheaths to the bone; aponeurosis — a flat, broad tendon (external oblique, epicranial aponeurosis).
  • Tendinous intersections (intersectiones tendineae) — flat tendons interrupting the belly of the rectus abdominis.
  • Sphincter — a circular muscle closing an opening; synergist, antagonist, fixator — muscles that assist, oppose or stabilise the prime mover (agonist).

  • Deep fascia (fascia profunda) — dense irregular connective tissue covering and separating muscle groups, sending intermuscular septa to the bone and so creating osteofascial compartments. Named examples: fascia brachii, fascia lata, thoracolumbar fascia, fascia of the leg.
  • Subserous fascia (fascia subserosa) — connects the serous lining of a body cavity to the wall (endothoracic fascia, fascia transversalis).

  • Three sheaths: endomysium (fibre), perimysium (fascicle), epimysium (whole muscle); endotenon continues into the tendon.

Likely follow-up questions
  • What is the difference between origin and insertion? — Origin is the relatively fixed proximal attachment, insertion the moved distal one; when the body moves instead, the roles reverse (fixed and mobile points).

I.4Biomechanism of the muscle contraction. Structural and functional features of myo- and osteotendinous junctions, tendon sheaths. Intratendinous torsion and compression during exercise.

Core answer Contraction is a sliding-filament process: myosin heads of the thick filaments form cyclic cross-bridges with actin of the thin filaments and pull them towards the M line, so the sarcomere shortens while the filaments themselves keep their length. It is triggered by excitation–contraction coupling — nerve impulse, acetylcholine at the motor end plate, depolarisation of the T-tubules, calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, calcium binding to troponin C. Force is transmitted from the sarcomere through the series-elastic component to the tendon. The force leaves the muscle at the myotendinous junction, where the folded sarcolemma and its basal lamina interlock with collagen, and enters bone at the osteotendinous junction, where tendon collagen continues through fibrocartilage and calcified fibrocartilage into bone as Sharpey's fibres. Tendon sheaths (synovial and fibrous) reduce friction, and in exercise tendons also undergo intratendinous torsion and compression, to which they adapt by fibrocartilaginous metaplasia.

Excitation–contraction coupling

  1. An action potential in the alpha motoneuron depolarises the presynaptic terminal and voltage-gated calcium channels open; synaptic vesicles release acetylcholine into the primary synaptic cleft.
  2. Acetylcholine binds nicotinic receptors of the motor end plate; ligand-gated sodium channels open and the sarcolemma depolarises. Acetylcholinesterase in the basal lamina of the junctional folds terminates the signal.
  3. The depolarisation spreads along the sarcolemma and down the T-tubules (invaginations of the sarcolemma filled with extracellular fluid), which lie at the I–A junction, two per sarcomere.
  4. In the triad (one T-tubule + two terminal cisternae of the sarcoplasmic reticulum) the voltage-sensitive dihydropyridine receptor of the T-tubule activates the ryanodine receptor calcium-release channel of the terminal cisterna, and calcium floods the sarcoplasm.

Intratendinous torsion and compression during exercise

Must-memorise
  • Sliding filament theory: filaments keep their length; A band constant, I band narrows, H band disappears.
  • Coupling chain: nerve → acetylcholine → end plate → T-tubule → DHP receptor → ryanodine receptor → Ca²⁺ → troponin C → tropomyosin moves.
  • Relaxation: calcium re-sequestered by the sarcoplasmic reticulum; ATP is needed both for the power stroke and for detachment.
  • Myotendinous junction: folded sarcolemma, basal lamina, reticular fibres, dystrophin. Osteotendinous junction: fibrocartilage, calcified fibrocartilage, Sharpey's fibres.
  • Synovial sheath lubricates; fibrous sheath is the pulley. Achilles and rotator cuff tendons are twisted (intratendinous torsion); tendons under retinacula are compressed and adapt by fibrocartilage.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Muscle strain: failure at or near the myotendinous junction after an eccentric contraction; MRI shows fluid and oedema in the muscle–tendon unit.
  • Tendinopathy and rupture: the calcaneal tendon ruptures typically in a tendon already weakened by overuse or degeneration, with a palpable gap in the tendon; complete rotator-cuff tears may even be asymptomatic.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Why does the A band not change during contraction? — It is the length of the thick filament, which does not shorten; the thin filaments only slide over it.
  • What is the series-elastic component? — The elastic material in series with the sarcomeres (titin, tendon, aponeurosis) that stores and returns elastic energy.

I.5The bones, joints and movements of the shoulder girdle. Radiological anatomy of the shoulder girdle.

Core answerThe shoulder girdle is built from two bones — the clavicle and the scapula. Its only true articulation with the axial skeleton is the sternoclavicular joint; the clavicle and scapula are joined to each other by the acromioclavicular joint; and the scapula glides on the thoracic wall through the scapulothoracic mechanism, which is not a true joint. Elevation–depression, protraction–retraction and upward–downward rotation of the scapula are produced by the trapezius, levator scapulae, rhomboids, serratus anterior, pectoralis minor and subclavius, and together these movements greatly increase the reach of the arm.

Sternoclavicular joint — surfaces, capsule, ligaments, synovium

  • Capsule: fibrous sleeve attached to the margins of the articular surfaces, lined by synovial membrane in both compartments.
  • Anterior and posterior sternoclavicular ligaments: thickenings of the capsule in front of and behind the joint.
  • Interclavicular ligament: links the two clavicular ends across the jugular notch and to the superior surface of the manubrium.
  • Costoclavicular (rhomboid) ligament: from the first rib and costal cartilage to the inferior surface of the medial clavicle; the main weight-bearing support of the joint, restricting elevation.

Acromioclavicular joint — surfaces, capsule, ligaments, synovium

  • the acromioclavicular ligament — a weak thickening of the capsule superiorly;
  • the coracoclavicular ligament — not attached to the joint at all, but the chief suspensory ligament of the limb on the clavicle. It consists of the anterior trapezoid ligament (trapezoid line) and the posterior conoid ligament (conoid tubercle).

Radiological anatomy of the shoulder girdle

  • Ossification: the clavicle is the first bone to ossify and is largely ossified at birth; the scapula ossifies from one centre for the body and separate centres for the coracoid, glenoid, acromion, medial border and inferior angle, which fuse during adolescence.
  • An unfused acromial apophysis (os acromiale) is a normal variant and must not be mistaken for a fracture; equally, the separate apophyses of the coracoid and acromion in a child are not fractures.
  • Ultrasound and MRI are the methods for the rotator cuff, the subacromial–subdeltoid bursa, the labrum and the acromioclavicular ligaments; CT is used for complex scapular or clavicular fractures.

Clinical anatomy of the girdle

  • Fracture of the clavicle — the typical site is the middle third. The clavicle is the only bony connection between the upper limb and the trunk, and fractures are common. The medial fragment is pulled upwards by the sternocleidomastoid, the lateral fragment is drawn downwards by the weight of the arm; the bone is subcutaneous, so the fracture is palpable, and non-union is common because the fragments move.
  • Acromioclavicular separation — a minor injury tears the capsule and acromioclavicular ligament; more severe trauma disrupts the conoid and trapezoid ligaments, and the clavicle rides upwards (step deformity). This is a girdle injury, not a dislocation of the shoulder joint.
  • Sternoclavicular dislocation — anterior is more common; a posterior dislocation is dangerous because the medial clavicle can compress the great vessels or the trachea in the root of the neck.
  • Winging of the scapula — injury to the long thoracic nerve (C5–C7) paralyses the serratus anterior; the medial border and inferior angle lift away from the chest wall, most obvious when the patient pushes forwards against a wall.
Must-memorise
  • Girdle = clavicle + scapula; two true joints (sternoclavicular, acromioclavicular) + one functional joint (scapulothoracic).
  • The sternoclavicular joint is the only true articulation of the upper limb with the axial skeleton; its disc divides it into two compartments; the costoclavicular ligament carries the weight.
  • The coracoclavicular ligament (trapezoid + conoid) is the chief suspensory ligament of the limb on the clavicle — it is why the acromioclavicular joint does not give way easily.
  • Six girdle movements: elevation, depression, protraction, retraction, upward rotation, downward rotation — know one prime mover and its nerve for each.
  • Close-packed: sternoclavicular = full retraction; acromioclavicular = full elevation of the arm.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • On an AP shoulder radiograph the humeral head overlaps the glenoid; on the scapular Y view it must sit in the centre of the Y. Displacement out of the Y indicates glenohumeral dislocation.
  • Bilateral acromioclavicular views are needed because the normal coracoclavicular distance varies between individuals; asymmetry is the sign of injury.
Likely follow-up questions
  • What is the only bony connection between the upper limb and the trunk? — the clavicle, through the sternoclavicular joint.
  • Why does the shoulder droop when the accessory nerve is injured? — the trapezius is the powerful elevator and rotator of the scapula; without it the scapula descends and cannot be rotated upwards.

I.6The gross and radiological anatomy and movements of the shoulder joint and the participating muscles. The morphological features and biomechanics of the rotator cuff.

Core answerThe shoulder (glenohumeral) joint is a synovial ball-and-socket (spheroidal) joint between the head of the humerus and the glenoid cavity of the scapula. It is multiaxial and the most mobile joint of the body, and it buys that mobility with a small, shallow socket; stability comes instead from the glenoid labrum, the capsule and its ligaments, the long head of biceps, the coraco-acromial arch and above all the rotator cuff — supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor and subscapularis. Flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, medial and lateral rotation and circumduction are each produced by a defined set of muscles, all of them innervated by branches of the brachial plexus.

Capsule, ligaments and synovial features

  • Fibrous capsule: attaches to the margin of the glenoid outside the labrum and to the anatomical neck of the humerus; on the medial side the attachment is lower, extending onto the shaft, and the capsule is here redundant (folded) so that it can unfold in abduction.
  • Glenohumeral ligaments — superior, middle and inferior thickenings of the anterior capsule running from the supraglenoid margin to the lesser tubercle and adjacent neck. The inferior one is the main static restraint against anteroinferior dislocation when the arm is abducted and laterally rotated.
  • Coracohumeral ligament — from the base of the coracoid process to the greater tubercle; strengthens the capsule superiorly and bridges the rotator interval between supraspinatus and subscapularis, which transmits the long biceps tendon.
  • Transverse humeral ligament — a band between the greater and lesser tubercles which holds the long biceps tendon in the intertubercular sulcus.

Biomechanics of the rotator cuff

  1. Dynamic stabilisation by compression (“concavity–compression”). The cuff pulls the head into the glenoid cavity; because the glenoid is deepened by the labrum, this compression resists translation in every direction and lets the deltoid move the arm without the head riding upwards.
  2. Balancing the deltoid — a force couple. The deltoid pulls the humerus almost vertically upwards; the inferior part of the cuff (infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis) pulls it downwards and medially. The two opposite forces produce a couple that rotates the humerus while keeping the head centred in the glenoid, and thereby prevents the head from impinging on the acromion.
  3. Rotation couples. In lateral rotation the posterior deltoid, infraspinatus and teres minor work against the subscapularis; in medial rotation the subscapularis, pectoralis major, latissimus dorsi and teres major work against the posterior deltoid. The antagonist partners co-contract, so the head stays centred while it rotates.
  4. Fine adjustment to the instantaneous centre of rotation. Because each cuff muscle has a slightly different line of pull, the central nervous system selects the combination that keeps the head congruent throughout the whole arc — the cuff is therefore a steering mechanism, not a prime mover.

Radiological anatomy

  • AP radiograph: the humeral head overlaps the glenoid; the glenohumeral joint space should be uniform and the head spherical; the acromiohumeral interval (the space between the acromion and the head, occupied by the supraspinatus tendon and the subacromial bursa) is narrowed when the cuff is torn and the head rides upwards.
  • Additional views: axillary view (joint space, anterior/posterior dislocation), scapular Y view (head centred), and Stryker notch or Garth views for Hill–Sachs lesions.
  • MRI: the method of choice for the tendons, labrum and capsule; MR arthrography distends the capsule and is best for labral tears; ultrasound is excellent for cuff tears, calcific deposits and the subacromial bursa and allows dynamic examination.
  • Ossification centres of the proximal humerus: the head appears in the first year, the greater tubercle later, the lesser tubercle later still, and the three fuse with the shaft around the end of growth (about 20 years); the epiphyseal plate is therefore a normal radiolucent line in adolescents and must not be called a fracture.

Clinical anatomy

  • Anterior dislocation is the commonest; the axillary nerve may be compressed as the head passes through the quadrangular space, and the radial nerve may be stretched, so sensation over the deltoid and the hand extensors must be tested before and after reduction.
  • Rotator cuff disease — impingement and tendinopathy, most often involving supraspinatus as it passes under the acromion; the subacromial space is of fixed size, so swelling, fluid in the bursa or bony spurs cause impingement in abduction. Degenerative change predisposes to partial- and full-thickness tears, with pain on combing the hair or abducting against resistance.
  • Subacromial (subdeltoid) bursitis — inflammation of the bursa makes every glenohumeral movement painful; treated by corticosteroid injection under ultrasound guidance.
  • Suprascapular nerve entrapment at the suprascapular notch gives pain and weakness of supraspinatus and infraspinatus with atrophy of the infraspinous fossa.
Must-memorise
  • Type: synovial ball-and-socket, multiaxial; surfaces = head of humerus + glenoid cavity; deepened by the labrum.
  • Rotator cuff = SITS: supraspinatus (abduction), infraspinatus (lateral rotation), teres minor (lateral rotation), subscapularis (medial rotation); all compress and stabilise the head.
  • Nerves of the cuff: suprascapular (C5, C6) for supra- and infraspinatus; axillary (C5, C6) for teres minor; upper and lower subscapular (C5, C6) for subscapularis.
  • The capsule is weak inferiorly, where dislocation occurs; the coraco-acromial arch is the protective roof.
  • Deltoid is the main abductor, supraspinatus initiates abduction; above 90 degrees the scapula must rotate upwards.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Painful arc between about 60 and 120 degrees of abduction suggests subacromial impingement; weakness without pain suggests a cuff tear.
  • Drop arm test — a complete supraspinatus tear prevents the patient from holding the abducted arm against gravity.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Why is the shoulder the most mobile but least stable joint? — the glenoid is small and shallow, the capsule is lax and the joint relies on the cuff and labrum for stability.
  • What is the force couple of abduction? — deltoid and supraspinatus elevate the arm; the inferior cuff (infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis) pulls the head downwards, so the resultant keeps the head centred in the glenoid.

I.7The gross and radiological anatomy and movements of the elbow joint and the participating muscles.

Core answerThe elbow is a compound synovial joint in which three articulations share one capsule and one synovial cavity: the humeroulnar and humeroradial joints, which are functionally a hinge for flexion and extension, and the proximal radio-ulnar joint, a pivot joint for pronation and supination. The capsule is reinforced by the ulnar and radial collateral ligaments and by the anular ligament of the radius; fat pads in the coronoid, radial and olecranon fossae are the radiological key to an effusion. Flexion is produced mainly by brachialis and biceps brachii, extension by triceps brachii and anconeus.

Clinical anatomy

  • Supracondylar fracture (children) — a transverse fracture above the epicondyles; the distal fragment is pulled posteriorly by the triceps and the brachial artery is “bowstrung” over the proximal fragment, so the forearm flexors may become ischaemic and undergo Volkmann's ischaemic contracture.

Must-memorise
  • Three articulations, one capsule: humeroulnar (hinge), humeroradial (spheroidal but hinge-constrained), proximal radio-ulnar (pivot).
  • Ligaments: ulnar collateral (anterior band strongest), radial collateral, anular ligament of the radius (forms most of the ring around the radial head).
  • The capsule does not attach to the radius — that is why the radial head can rotate; the sacciform recess allows the rotation.
  • Flexors: brachialis (main), biceps brachii, brachioradialis — musculocutaneous and radial nerves. Extensors: triceps (main), anconeus — radial nerve (C6–C8).
  • Close-packed: extension + supination. Carrying angle is present in extension and disappears in full pronation.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Always look for the posterior fat pad on the lateral view: it is the most sensitive plain-radiographic sign of an occult intra-articular fracture in the adult elbow.
  • In children, always compare with the other elbow and know the age of the patient — the ossification centres are symmetric and have sclerotic, rounded margins, whereas fractures are sharp and irregular.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Why is the elbow described as a compound joint? — three articulations with one capsule and one synovial cavity.
  • Why does the elbow not dislocate easily? — the deep trochlear notch grips the trochlea, and the strong collateral ligaments and the bony fossae limit movement.

I.8Pronation and supination in the forearm: participating joints and muscles.

Core answerPronation and supination are rotation of the radius around the ulna and occur only in the forearm. Two synovial pivot (trochoid) joints participate: the proximal radio-ulnar joint, where the radial head turns within the ring formed by the radial notch and the anular ligament, and the distal radio-ulnar joint, where the distal end of the radius moves anteromedially over the relatively fixed head of the ulna, the triangular articular disc holding the two together. The interosseous membrane holds the two bones together and transmits force between them. Supination is produced by biceps brachii and supinator, pronation by pronator teres and pronator quadratus, with brachioradialis bringing the forearm back to the mid-position.

  • Radiology: on an AP forearm in supination the radius and ulna are parallel; in pronation the radius crosses the ulna and the two bones overlap at their middle thirds.   • Galeazzi's fracture — fracture of the distal third of the radius with subluxation of the ulnar head at the wrist.

Must-memorise
  • Two pivot joints: proximal radio-ulnar (radial head in the ring of the radial notch + anular ligament) and distal radio-ulnar (ulnar head in the ulnar notch + articular disc).
  • Axis: centre of the radial head to the ulnar styloid; the ulna is the fixed unit and the radius rotates around it.
  • The articular disc separates the distal radio-ulnar joint from the wrist joint; the ulna therefore does not articulate with the carpal bones.
  • Supination — biceps brachii (elbow flexed) and supinator (any position); pronation — pronator teres (fast, resisted) and pronator quadratus (slow, unresisted).
  • Close-packed: proximal joint in full supination, distal joint in full pronation.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Loss of supination after a distal radial fracture means the distal radio-ulnar joint has been disrupted, not just the bone broken.
  • In a true lateral radiograph of the elbow the radial head must point to the capitulum; a radial head that does not is dislocated (Monteggia equivalent).
Likely follow-up questions
  • Where does pronation and supination take place? — only in the forearm, at the two radio-ulnar joints, not at the wrist.
  • Why does the supinator work better than biceps when the elbow is extended? — the biceps is at a mechanical disadvantage in extension and is also an elbow flexor; the supinator wraps directly around the radius.

I.9The gross and radiological anatomy and movements of the wrist joint and the participating muscles.

Core answerThe wrist (radiocarpal) joint is a synovial ellipsoid (condyloid) joint between the distal end of the radius and the triangular articular disc proximally, and the scaphoid, lunate and triquetrum distally; the ulna takes no direct part. It is biaxial — flexion–extension around a transverse axis and radial–ulnar deviation around an anteroposterior axis — and its capsule is reinforced by palmar radiocarpal, palmar ulnocarpal, dorsal radiocarpal and radial and ulnar collateral ligaments. The flexors are flexor carpi radialis, flexor carpi ulnaris, palmaris longus and the long finger flexors; the extensors are extensor carpi radialis longus, extensor carpi radialis brevis and extensor carpi ulnaris.

Functional (“coupled”) movements of the wrist

  • Wrist extension amplifies finger flexion. The long flexor tendons are at their optimal length for force production when the wrist is extended by about 20 degrees, and their weakest action is at about 40 degrees of wrist flexion. This is why a patient with a painful wrist cannot grip strongly.
  • The tenodesis effect: passive wrist extension in a paralysed hand causes the finger flexors to be pulled tight and the fingers to flex; passive wrist flexion opens the fingers. The hand and wrist work synergistically.
  • Functionally sufficient range: a 45-degree arc of flexion–extension is enough for most everyday use; personal support needs about 10 degrees of flexion and 15 degrees of extension, while eating, drinking and reading need about 5 degrees of flexion and 35 degrees of extension. This explains why extension of the wrist is functionally the more important direction.
  • Compensation: joints proximal to the wrist (the intercarpal and midcarpal joints, and pronation–supination of the forearm) can compensate for a considerable loss of radiocarpal movement.

Radiological anatomy

  • Projections: a posteroanterior (PA) and a lateral radiograph; the PA view is taken with the forearm in pronation so that the radius and ulna do not overlap.
  • Joint space: the radiocarpal joint space should be uniform, and the three carpal arcs described by Gilula must be smooth and unbroken — (1) the proximal convexity of the proximal carpal row, (2) the distal concavity of the proximal row, (3) the proximal convexity of the distal row (capitate and hamate). A step in any arc means carpal dislocation or instability.
  • Scapholunate interval: widening between scaphoid and lunate suggests scapholunate ligament tear and dissociation.
  • Ossification: all carpal bones are cartilaginous at birth. The capitate and hamate ossify first, in the first year; the triquetrum, lunate, scaphoid, trapezium and trapezoid follow; the pisiform is the last, shortly before puberty. The carpus is therefore the standard region for bone-age estimation from a left-hand radiograph.

Clinical anatomy

  • Fracture of the scaphoid — the commonest carpal injury, across the waist. Its blood supply enters distally in a proportion of people, so the proximal fragment can undergo avascular necrosis; a clinically suspected fracture with a normal radiograph is treated as a fracture until proven otherwise.
  • Kienböck's disease — avascular necrosis of the lunate with sclerosis on the radiograph, pain and later arthritis.
  • Colles' fracture — distal radius fracture with dorsal displacement; the wrist looks like a dinner fork, and wrist and hand function are both impaired.
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome — compression of the median nerve beneath the flexor retinaculum: numbness of the radial three and a half digits, thenar wasting, and a positive Tinel's sign.
Must-memorise
  • Type: synovial ellipsoid, biaxial; surfaces = distal radius + articular disc proximally, scaphoid + lunate + triquetrum distally.
  • The ulna does not articulate with the carpus — the triangular articular disc intervenes.
  • Ligaments: palmar radiocarpal (strongest), palmar ulnocarpal, dorsal radiocarpal (weak), radial and ulnar collateral.
  • Flexion = FCR, FCU, palmaris longus (+ long finger flexors); extension = ECRL, ECRB, ECU; radial deviation = FCR, ECRL, ECRB; ulnar deviation = FCU, ECU.
  • Close-packed: full extension. Ulnar deviation is greater than radial deviation because the radial styloid projects further distally.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • A painful wrist with a normal radiograph still needs a scaphoid view and repeat films: the fracture may be invisible for days.
  • Weakness of grip in a patient with wrist pain is mechanical, not neurological: the flexor tendons lose their optimal length when the wrist is flexed.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Why is the wrist joint called ellipsoid and not hinge? — it has two axes of movement (flexion–extension and radial–ulnar deviation) and allows circumduction.
  • Why can the hand be ulnar-deviated more than radially deviated? — the radial styloid process extends further distally, so the carpus abuts it sooner on radial deviation.

I.10Joints and movements of the hand. Radiological anatomy of the hand.

Core answerThe hand contains three groups of synovial joints: the carpometacarpal joints — a biaxial saddle joint for the thumb and plane joints for the fingers — the metacarpophalangeal joints, which are condylar (ellipsoid) joints allowing flexion–extension, abduction–adduction and circumduction, and the interphalangeal joints, which are pure hinges. The thumb is set apart: its saddle carpometacarpal joint, its independence from the deep transverse metacarpal ligaments and its 90-degree rotation give it opposition, the movement that makes the human hand a precision tool.

Radiological anatomy of the hand

  • Projections: a posteroanterior view of the whole hand with the fingers extended, an oblique view and a lateral view. The PA view is the standard for bone age, joint spaces and erosions.
  • Joint spaces: the carpometacarpal, metacarpophalangeal and interphalangeal joint spaces should be uniform and congruent, with no step in the cortex at the joint margins; the joint surfaces of adjacent phalanges should be parallel.
  • Sesamoid bones: two constant sesamoids lie on the palmar aspect of the MCP joint of the thumb (over the head of metacarpal I); they may also occur at the MCP joint of the index or little finger. They are normal and must not be read as fractures. The pisiform is a sesamoid in the tendon of flexor carpi ulnaris.
  • Growth: each metacarpal has a distal epiphysis (except the first, whose epiphysis is proximal), and each phalanx has a proximal epiphysis; the plates close in adolescence. The order and symmetry of epiphyses, together with the carpal ossification centres, are the basis of bone-age assessment.

Clinical anatomy

  • Bennett's fracture — an intra-articular fracture of the base of metacarpal I with subluxation, caused by the pull of the abductor pollicis longus; the CMC joint of the thumb is also the site of osteoarthritis.
  • Boxer's fracture — fracture of the neck of metacarpal V after a punch, with the head angulated palmarly; the knuckle disappears.
  • Gamekeeper's thumb — rupture of the ulnar collateral ligament of the MCP joint of the thumb after forced abduction; pinch grip becomes weak.
  • Mallet finger — avulsion of the extensor tendon from the base of the distal phalanx, so the DIP joint cannot be actively extended.
Must-memorise
  • CMC I = saddle (opposition); CMC II–V = plane, gliding, most mobile at V; MCP = condylar; IP = hinge.
  • Thumb: 2 phalanges, 1 IP joint, saddle CMC, no deep transverse metacarpal ligament to the index finger — the anatomical basis of independence.
  • Collateral ligaments of the MCP joints are taut in flexion and lax in extension.
  • Close-packed: MCP in full flexion, IP in full extension, CMC I in full opposition.
  • Intrinsic muscles: the median nerve supplies the thenar muscles and the lateral two lumbricals (mnemonic LOAF); the ulnar nerve supplies everything else in the hand, including all the interossei (mm. interossei) and the medial two lumbricals (mm. lumbricales).
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • In a lateral radiograph of the hand the metacarpals overlap, so a dislocated MCP joint is best confirmed on the PA and oblique views together.
  • Do not mistake the sesamoids of the thumb or the epiphyses of children for fractures; look for a sharp, non-sclerotic line breaking the cortex instead.
Likely follow-up questions
  • What is the difference between the thumb and the fingers in terms of joints? — saddle CMC with three planes of movement versus plane CMC; two phalanges versus three; independent ligaments.
  • What are the movements of the MCP joint and which muscle groups produce them? — flexion (long flexors and intrinsics), extension (long extensors), abduction (dorsal interossei), adduction (palmar interossei).

I.11The anatomy, innervation and function of the spinohumeral and thoracohumeral muscles.

Core answerThe spinohumeral and thoracohumeral muscles are the muscles that connect the axial skeleton to the shoulder region. The spinohumeral group arises from the vertebral column — trapezius, levator scapulae, rhomboid major, rhomboid minor, serratus anterior and latissimus dorsi; the thoracohumeral group arises from the thoracic wall — pectoralis major, pectoralis minor and subclavius. The trapezius is supplied by the accessory nerve (XI) with C3 and C4; the levator scapulae by direct C3 and C4 branches together with the dorsal scapular nerve (C4–C5, chiefly C5); the remaining muscles by branches of the brachial plexus (dorsal scapular, long thoracic, thoracodorsal and pectoral nerves). They position the scapula on the thorax and power the shoulder joint, and they are the muscles examined clinically in winging of the scapula and in shoulder droop.

Definition and place in the classification of the upper limb muscles

  • Spinohumeral muscles — arise from the vertebral column and attach to the shoulder region: trapezius, levator scapulae, rhomboid minor, rhomboid major, serratus anterior, latissimus dorsi. Note that the group name follows the old teaching that these muscles run from the spine to the humerus; in fact only latissimus dorsi reaches the humerus, the others insert on the scapula (and clavicle) and act on the shoulder girdle.
  • Thoracohumeral muscles — arise from the thoracic cage and attach to the shoulder region: pectoralis major, pectoralis minor, subclavius. Again, only pectoralis major reaches the humerus, pectoralis minor inserting on the coracoid process and subclavius on the clavicle.
  • Functionally all nine are positioners of the scapula and prime movers of the arm: they cross the sternoclavicular, acromioclavicular, scapulothoracic and glenohumeral joints, so their contraction can move the scapula, the clavicle or the humerus, depending on which segment is fixed.

Function and biomechanics

  • The scapula is the mobile base of the arm. Spinohumeral and thoracohumeral muscles fix the scapula on the thorax so that the deltoid and the rotator cuff have a stable origin; when they are paralysed, abduction above the horizontal becomes impossible.
  • Force couples of the scapula. Upward rotation is produced by the upper trapezius and the lower serratus anterior working as a couple; downward rotation by the rhomboids and levator scapulae with gravity. The stabilising couple of the scapula on the thorax is the trapezius (medial pull) against the serratus anterior (lateral pull).
  • Both ends can move. When the arm is fixed (as in climbing or pushing up from a chair), the same muscles move the trunk: latissimus dorsi and pectoralis major pull the body towards the fixed hand.
  • Arm power and posture. Pectoralis major and latissimus dorsi are the great adductors and medial rotators of the arm; trapezius and the rhomboids set the height and retraction of the shoulder, and hence the posture of the whole upper limb.

Clinical anatomy

  • Winging of the scapula — paralysis of serratus anterior after injury to the long thoracic nerve (radical neck dissection, axillary surgery, stab wound, or repetitive strain). The medial border and inferior angle lift off the chest wall, most obvious when the patient pushes forwards against a wall with straight arms.
  • Trapezius palsy — accessory nerve injury (neck surgery, trauma) produces a drooping shoulder, inability to shrug against resistance and weakness of abduction above 90 degrees; the scapula slides laterally and downwards.
  • Dorsal scapular nerve palsy — weakness of the rhomboids and slight lateral displacement of the scapula, with aching between the shoulder blades.
  • Thoracodorsal nerve palsy — latissimus dorsi weakness after axillary dissection; the patient cannot pull the raised arm down against resistance (tested with the arm abducted and the elbow extended).
Must-memorise
  • Spinohumeral (6): trapezius, levator scapulae, rhomboid minor, rhomboid major, serratus anterior, latissimus dorsi.
  • Thoracohumeral (3): pectoralis major, pectoralis minor, subclavius.
  • Nerves: accessory [XI] + C3, C4 for trapezius; dorsal scapular (C4, C5) for rhomboids; C3, C4 + dorsal scapular (C5) for levator scapulae; long thoracic (C5–C7) for serratus anterior; thoracodorsal (C6–C8) for latissimus dorsi; lateral and medial pectoral for pectoralis major; medial pectoral (C8, T1) for pectoralis minor; nerve to subclavius (C5, C6).
  • Only latissimus dorsi and pectoralis major of these nine reach the humerus; the rest insert on the scapula or clavicle.
  • Force couple of upward rotation: upper trapezius + lower serratus anterior.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Test serratus anterior by asking the patient to push against a wall with straight arms: the scapula should stay flat on the chest.
  • Test trapezius by asking the patient to shrug against resistance and to hold the arm abducted above the horizontal.
Likely follow-up questions
  • What exactly do the terms spinohumeral and thoracohumeral mean? — muscles from the vertebral column and from the thoracic wall respectively, acting on the shoulder region; the names describe the proximal attachment, not the insertion.
  • Why is the trapezius not innervated by the brachial plexus? — it is a branchial (pharyngeal arch) muscle, supplied by the accessory nerve, with only proprioceptive fibres from C3 and C4.

I.12Classification (types) and innervation of medium and large blood vessels. Functional and structural features of the arterioles and venules. Types of vascular anastomoses. Structure, function and types of capillaries.

Core answerEvery blood vessel has three tunics — tunica intima (endothelium, subendothelial layer, internal elastic lamina), tunica media (smooth muscle and elastic fibres) and tunica adventitia (fibroelastic coat carrying the vasa vasorum and nervi vasorum). Arteries are classified as elastic (conducting), muscular (distributing) and arterioles (resistance); veins as venules, medium and small veins and large veins (capacitance). The arteriole is the resistance vessel that maintains mean arterial pressure and distributes cardiac output, the postcapillary venule is the main exchange and leukocyte-emigration segment, and capillaries are continuous, fenestrated or sinusoidal. Vessels are joined by arterial, venous, collateral and arteriovenous anastomoses.

Common plan: the three tunics

  • Tunica intima (tunica intima) — a simple squamous endothelium on a basal lamina, a subendothelial layer of loose connective tissue, and in arteries the internal elastic lamina (lamina elastica interna), a fenestrated elastic sheet marking the intima/media boundary. Endothelial cells contain Weibel–Palade bodies, the storage site of von Willebrand factor; their luminal membrane carries angiotensin-converting enzyme and lipoprotein lipase, and they secrete nitric oxide, prostacyclin and endothelin.
  • Tunica media — the thickest coat of an artery: helically arranged smooth muscle cells with elastic fibres, type I and III collagen and ground substance. In muscular arteries its outer border is marked by the external elastic lamina. The smooth muscle is the effector of vasoconstriction and vasodilatation.
  • Tunica adventitia — fibroelastic connective tissue anchoring the vessel to its surroundings; it carries the vasa vasorum (small arteries supplying the outer media and adventitia, more prominent in veins) and the nervi vasorum (vasomotor nerves).
  • In large vessels the wall is too thick for nutrition from the lumen, hence the vasa vasorum. Capillaries have no media at all; their contractile elements are pericytes, which share the capillary basal lamina.

Innervation of medium and large blood vessels

  • The wall contains both effector and sensory nerve endings; the vasomotor nerves enter with the adventitia as the nervi vasorum.
  • Sympathetic noradrenergic vasoconstrictor fibres are the principal efferent supply. Postganglionic fibres release noradrenaline, which diffuses to the smooth muscle of the media; contraction spreads from cell to cell through gap junctions. Reduced noradrenaline release, or antagonism of its receptors, produces vasodilatation.
  • Parasympathetic vasodilator fibres releasing vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (VIP) supply salivary glands and erectile tissue.
  • Sensory endings: baroreceptors of the carotid sinus (glossopharyngeal nerve, IX) and of the aortic arch (vagus nerve, X) monitor pressure; peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid and aortic bodies) monitor blood gas composition. Peptidergic, CGRP-containing afferents serve nociception.

Likely follow-up questions

I.13The systemic circulation: the large branches of the aorta and the great veins.

Core answerThe systemic circulation carries oxygenated blood from the left ventricle through the aorta and its branches to all tissues and returns it through the superior and inferior venae cavae to the right atrium. It is arranged in series with the pulmonary circulation but in parallel between individual organs, and it is linked to the tissues by the microcirculation. The aorta is described in four parts — ascending aorta, arch, descending thoracic aorta and abdominal aorta — and the great veins are the brachiocephalic veins, the superior vena cava, the azygos system and the inferior vena cava. The hepatic portal vein is the one important exception, passing through the liver before it reaches the caval system.

The circuit and its functional design

Systemic circuit: left ventricle → ascending aorta → arch → descending aorta → muscular arteries → arterioles → capillaries → venules → veins → superior and inferior venae cavae → right atrium.

  • Series arrangement of the systemic and pulmonary circuits: the whole cardiac output of the left ventricle passes through the systemic circuit, then through the right heart and the lungs.
  • Parallel arrangement of the organs within the systemic circulation: each organ receives blood of identical composition, and the arterioles of each organ set its share of the output.
  • Microcirculation — arteries and veins are connected only through the capillary beds, where exchange occurs.
  • Hepatic portal circulation — the venous blood of the abdominal gut, spleen, pancreas and gallbladder passes first through the liver; only the hepatic veins open into the inferior vena cava.

Clinical and radiological correlations

  • Coarctation of the aorta — congenital narrowing just distal to the origin of the left subclavian artery; the abdomen and lower limbs are underperfused while the upper body is hypertensive. Collateral flow enlarges the intercostal arteries, which erode the inferior margins of the ribs and produce rib notching on the chest radiograph; cardiac failure may follow.
  • Aortic dissection — a longitudinal split between intima and media creates a false channel; in the ascending aorta or arch it may occlude the coronary or cerebral arteries and cause myocardial infarction or stroke.
  • Traumatic aortic injury — deceleration tears the aorta at its three fixed points: the aortic valve, the ligamentum arteriosum, and the point where it passes behind the median arcuate ligament of the diaphragm.
  • Abdominal aortic aneurysm — dilation, usually infrarenal, treated by open or endovascular grafting because of the risk of rupture.
Must-memorise
  • Aorta in four parts: ascending (coronary arteries only), arch (brachiocephalic trunk, left common carotid, left subclavian), descending thoracic (TIV to TXII), abdominal (TXII to LIV, then two common iliac arteries).
  • Levels: aortic hiatus TXII; oesophagus TX; inferior vena cava through the central tendon at about TVIII.
  • Superior vena cava = union of the brachiocephalic veins; formed behind the right first costal cartilage, ends at the right third costal cartilage; receives the azygos vein.
  • Inferior vena cava = union of the common iliac veins at LV; tributaries: lumbar, right gonadal, renal, right suprarenal, inferior phrenic and hepatic veins.
  • No caval tributaries from the gut, spleen, pancreas or gallbladder — these belong to the portal system.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Rib notching in coarctation is the radiological trace of dilated posterior intercostal arteries acting as collaterals.
  • Radio-femoral delay — the femoral pulse is delayed and weaker than the radial pulse distal to a coarctation.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Which are the only branches of the ascending aorta? — The right and left coronary arteries.
  • Where does the thoracic aorta begin and end? — At the lower edge of TIV and at the aortic hiatus, at the lower edge of TXII.

I.14Branching system of the subclavian artery. Anastomoses between subclavian and external iliac arteries.

Core answerThe right subclavian artery arises from the brachiocephalic trunk behind the sternoclavicular joint, the left directly from the arch of the aorta; both arch over the cervical pleura and the apex of the lung, pass posterior to the anterior scalene muscle and end at the lateral border of rib I, where they become the axillary artery. The anterior scalene divides the artery into three parts. Its branches are the vertebral artery, the thyrocervical trunk (inferior thyroid, transverse cervical, suprascapular arteries), the internal thoracic artery and the costocervical trunk (deep cervical and supreme intercostal arteries), with a dorsal scapular artery that may arise from the third part. Through the internal thoracic artery, the superior epigastric artery and the inferior epigastric artery the subclavian artery anastomoses with the external iliac artery, which is the main collateral route of the anterior abdominal wall.

Origin, course and termination

  • Right subclavian artery — begins posterior to the sternoclavicular joint as one of the two terminal branches of the brachiocephalic trunk; arches superiorly and laterally, anterior to the cervical pleura and posterior to the anterior scalene muscle.
  • Left subclavian artery — begins lower in the thorax as a direct branch of the arch of the aorta, posterior to the left common carotid artery and lateral to the trachea; ascends and arches laterally in the same relation to the pleura and the anterior scalene muscle.
  • Termination — both arteries groove the superior surface of rib I and end at its lateral border, where the axillary artery begins. The artery, the brachial plexus and the subclavian vein all pass between the neck and the upper limb over rib I, through the axillary inlet.
  • Relations — the subclavian vein lies anterior to the artery and anterior to the anterior scalene muscle; the artery runs in the scalene triangle (interscalene space) between the anterior and middle scalene muscles. The phrenic nerve crosses anterior to the anterior scalene; on the left the thoracic duct and the vagus nerve are related medially; the sympathetic trunk lies posteriorly.

  • Coarctation of the aorta — the narrowing lies just distal to the left subclavian artery. The upper limbs are hypertensive and the femoral pulse is weak and delayed.
  • Subclavian steal syndrome — stenosis or occlusion of the proximal subclavian artery makes blood flow retrogradely down the ipsilateral vertebral artery during exercise of the arm, producing vertebrobasilar insufficiency (dizziness, visual disturbance).
  • Thoracic outlet syndrome — the subclavian artery and the brachial plexus may be compressed in the scalene triangle, between the clavicle and rib I, or beneath the pectoralis minor.
  • Central venous catheterisation — the subclavian vein is punctured in the infraclavicular fossa; it lies anterior to the artery, separated from it by the anterior scalene muscle, and joins the internal jugular vein to form the brachiocephalic vein.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Pulses and blood pressure — the subclavian pulse is felt above the clavicle; comparison of the two radial pulses and of the two arms is the first bedside test of a subclavian stenosis.
  • Radiology — the artery is shown by subclavian or aortic arch angiography; in coarctation the dilated internal thoracic and intercostal collaterals produce rib notching on the chest radiograph.

  • Three parts divided by the anterior scalene; all branches from the first part except the costocervical trunk (second part on the right).
  • Branches: vertebral artery, thyrocervical trunk (inferior thyroid, transverse cervical, suprascapular), internal thoracic artery, costocervical trunk (deep cervical, supreme intercostal); dorsal scapular artery from the third part.
  • Internal thoracic artery ends at the sixth intercostal space as the superior epigastric and musculophrenic arteries.
  • Main subclavian–external iliac anastomosis: internal thoracic → superior epigastric → inferior epigastric → external iliac.
Likely follow-up questions

  • Which vessel passes through the transverse foramen of CVI and why does it matter? — The vertebral artery; it is the first branch of the subclavian and a channel of the posterior cerebral circulation.

I.15The branching system and anastomoses of the axillary artery.

Core answerThe axillary artery is the continuation of the subclavian artery from the lateral border of rib I to the lower border of the teres major muscle, where it becomes the brachial artery. It lies in the axilla with the brachial plexus and the axillary vein inside the axillary sheath, and its relations change as it passes behind the pectoralis minor muscle, which divides it into three parts. It gives six named branches: superior thoracic from the first part; thoraco-acromial and lateral thoracic from the second part; subscapular, anterior circumflex humeral and posterior circumflex humeral arteries from the third part. Its anastomoses — the scapular arcade and the acromial network — connect it with the subclavian artery and secure the blood supply of the shoulder.

Origin, course and sheath

  • Origin — at the lateral border of rib I, as the continuation of the subclavian artery.
  • Course — enters the axilla through the axillary inlet, runs along its medial wall and then turns laterally below the shoulder joint; its distal (third) part lies on the medial side of the coracobrachialis muscle, and it continues into the interval between the coracobrachialis and the long head of the triceps.
  • Termination — at the lower border of the teres major muscle, where it becomes the brachial artery.
  • Axillary sheath — a sleeve of prevertebral fascia enclosing the axillary artery, the axillary vein and the brachial plexus. This is why a single injection of local anaesthetic into the sheath produces a brachial plexus block for the whole limb.

Three parts and relations to the brachial plexus

  • First part — lies in the axillary inlet, related anteriorly to the clavicle and the subclavius muscle and medially to the first rib and the cervical pleura; the cords of the brachial plexus lie lateral to it.
  • Second part — posterior to the pectoralis minor and surrounded by the cords of the brachial plexus; the axillary vein lies anterior and medial to the artery.
  • Third part — related anteriorly to the roots of the median nerve (which embrace it), medially to the ulnar nerve and the medial cutaneous nerves of the arm and forearm, laterally to the musculocutaneous nerve and coracobrachialis, and posteriorly to the radial and axillary nerves.

  • Crutch compression — prolonged pressure in the axilla can compress the axillary artery and the brachial plexus.

  • Scapular arcade = suprascapular + dorsal scapular + circumflex scapular arteries: the collateral route around the shoulder.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • The axillary artery is surrounded by the brachial plexus, so a single sheath injection anaesthetises the whole upper limb.
  • Anterior shoulder dislocation in an elderly patient may rupture the axillary artery; check the radial pulse before and after reduction.
Likely follow-up questions

  • Which branch of the axillary artery is the largest and what does it supply? — The subscapular artery; it supplies the posterior wall of the axilla and scapular region.

I.16The branching system of the brachial artery; collateral circulation of the elbow.

Core answerThe brachial artery is the continuation of the axillary artery from the lower border of the teres major muscle and runs through the anterior compartment of the arm to the cubital fossa, where it divides at the level of the neck of the radius into the radial and ulnar arteries. Its main branches are the profunda brachii artery (which gives the radial and middle collateral arteries), the superior ulnar collateral artery, the inferior ulnar collateral artery, muscular branches and the humeral nutrient artery. Around the elbow these descending collaterals anastomose with the ascending recurrent branches of the forearm — radial recurrent, interosseous recurrent, and anterior and posterior ulnar recurrent arteries — forming the collateral circulation of the elbow, which secures the supply of the forearm when the brachial artery is occluded.

Course and relations

  • Begins at the lower border of the teres major muscle; lies in the anterior compartment of the arm. Proximally it is on the medial side of the arm, related medially to the ulnar nerve and the basilic vein, and laterally to the median nerve.
  • Distally it moves laterally to lie midway between the medial and lateral epicondyles of the humerus, crosses anterior to the elbow joint and lies immediately medial to the tendon of the biceps brachii in the cubital fossa.
  • It is palpable along its whole length and can be compressed against the medial side of the humerus in the proximal arm.
  • The median nerve is its constant companion: lateral to the artery proximally, crossing to its medial side distally.

  • Arterial blood gas sampling — the brachial artery is punctured medial to the biceps tendon in the cubital fossa, a site with good collateral support.
  • Arteriovenous fistula — a brachiocephalic (brachial artery to cephalic vein) fistula at the elbow is a standard vascular access for haemodialysis.

  • Collateral pairs: radial collateral → radial recurrent; middle collateral → interosseous recurrent; superior ulnar collateral → posterior ulnar recurrent; inferior ulnar collateral → anterior ulnar recurrent.
  • Cubital fossa contents lateral to medial: biceps tendon, brachial artery, median nerve.
  • The ulnar nerve passes behind the medial epicondyle and does not enter the cubital fossa.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Supracondylar fracture in a child with an absent radial pulse and a cold hand is a vascular emergency; the elbow collateral network may not compensate for an acute tear.
  • The brachial artery is the vessel auscultated during sphygmomanometry, and its pulse is felt medial to the biceps in the mid-arm.
Likely follow-up questions

  • Name the four collateral arteries of the elbow and their partners. — Radial collateral with the radial recurrent, middle collateral with the interosseous recurrent, superior ulnar collateral with the posterior ulnar recurrent, inferior ulnar collateral with the anterior ulnar recurrent.

I.17Topography and branches of the palmar arterial arches.

Core answerThe hand is supplied by two anastomotic arches formed by the radial and ulnar arteries. The superficial palmar arch is formed mainly by the ulnar artery and is completed laterally by the superficial palmar branch of the radial artery; it lies deep to the palmar aponeurosis and superficial to the long flexor tendons and gives three common palmar digital arteries and a proper digital artery to the little finger. The deep palmar arch is formed mainly by the terminal part of the radial artery and is completed medially by the deep palmar branch of the ulnar artery; it lies deeper in the palm on the metacarpals and gives three palmar metacarpal arteries, three perforating branches, the princeps pollicis artery and the radialis indicis artery. Together the two arches secure a dual arterial supply to the hand.

General arrangement of the arterial supply of the hand

  • The ulnar artery enters the hand on the medial side of the wrist, lateral to the pisiform and superficial to the flexor retinaculum; it is the main supply of the medial three and one-half digits.
  • The radial artery leaves the forearm around the lateral side of the wrist, crosses the floor of the anatomical snuffbox, and reaches the deep plane of the palm by passing between the two heads of the first dorsal interosseous muscle; it is the main supply of the thumb and the lateral side of the index finger.
  • The two arteries are interconnected by the superficial and deep palmar arches, by the dorsal carpal arch and its dorsal metacarpal arteries, and by the perforating branches — so either artery alone can supply the whole hand.

The superficial palmar arch

  • Formation — mainly the continuation of the ulnar artery, completed on the lateral side of the palm by the superficial palmar branch of the radial artery.
  • Position — superficial in the palm: it lies deep to the palmar aponeurosis and superficial to the long flexor tendons of the digits; it is more distal than the deep arch.
  • Branches: a palmar digital artery to the medial side of the little finger; three large common palmar digital arteries, which supply the lateral side of the little finger, both sides of the ring and middle fingers and the medial side of the index finger. Before bifurcating into the proper palmar digital arteries they are joined by the palmar metacarpal arteries from the deep arch.

The deep palmar arch

  • Formation — mainly the terminal part of the radial artery, completed on the medial side of the palm by the deep palmar branch of the ulnar artery, which curves round the hook of the hamate into the deep plane.
  • Position — lies proximal to the superficial arch, on the palmar surfaces of the metacarpal bones and deep to the long flexor tendons; it is the deeper and more proximal of the two arches.
  • Branches: three palmar metacarpal arteries (which join the common palmar digital arteries); three perforating branches (which pass dorsally between the heads of the dorsal interossei to anastomose with the dorsal metacarpal arteries); and, arising from the radial artery at this level, the princeps pollicis artery (main supply of the thumb) and the radialis indicis artery (lateral side of the index finger).

  • Palpation sites — the radial pulse at the wrist lateral to the flexor carpi radialis tendon and in the floor of the anatomical snuffbox; the ulnar pulse lateral to the pisiform.
Must-memorise

  • Deep arch: mainly radial + deep palmar branch of ulnar; on the metacarpals; gives three palmar metacarpal arteries, three perforating branches, princeps pollicis and radialis indicis arteries.
  • The deep arch lies deeper and more proximal than the superficial arch.
  • The princeps pollicis artery is the main artery of the thumb; the radialis indicis supplies the lateral side of the index finger.
  • Dual supply: radial and ulnar arteries are joined at both arches, through the perforating branches and at the dorsal carpal arch.
Clinical / radiological correlation

  • Hand ischaemia after radial artery cannulation is rare but is the reason for the Allen test.

  • Which artery forms the deep arch and which completes it? — The radial artery, completed by the deep palmar branch of the ulnar artery.

I.18The venous and lymphatic drainage of the upper limb.

Core answerThe upper limb is drained by two interconnected venous systems. The superficial veins lie in the subcutaneous tissue: the dorsal venous network of the hand drains into the cephalic vein laterally and the basilic vein medially, and the two are connected in the cubital fossa by the median cubital vein. The deep veins are paired venae comitantes that accompany the arteries — radial, ulnar and interosseous veins, then the brachial veins, the axillary vein and the subclavian vein, which becomes the brachiocephalic vein. Most lymph from the limb passes to the axillary lymph nodes; superficial lymphatics run with the superficial veins through the cubital nodes (those of the thumb, index finger and dorsum of the hand may bypass them), deep lymphatics follow the arteries, and from the apical group the subclavian trunk carries the lymph into the thoracic duct on the left or the right lymphatic duct on the right.

General plan

  • Two venous systems — superficial (subcutaneous, highly variable, used for venepuncture) and deep (paired, accompanying the arteries) — connected by perforating veins at many levels, so obstruction of one route is compensated by the other.
  • Both systems contain valves that direct blood towards the heart; the flow is assisted by the contraction of the muscles of the limb (muscle pump).
  • Lymphatics form a superficial plexus with the superficial veins and a deep plexus with the arteries; the two communicate freely.

  • Lymphatics from the thumb, index finger and the dorsum of the hand may run with the cephalic vein directly to the infraclavicular and apical axillary nodes without passing through the cubital nodes.
  • Deep lymphatics accompany the arteries and their deep veins and drain into the humeral group of axillary nodes.
  • All lymph from the upper limb therefore passes to the axillary nodes; no other node group receives the limb's lymph — but not every vessel passes through the cubital nodes.

  • Venepuncture — the superficial veins of the cubital fossa (median cubital, cephalic, basilic) are the standard sites for blood sampling and cannulation; a tourniquet proximal to the elbow makes them prominent.

Must-memorise

  • Axillary groups: humeral (lateral), pectoral (anterior), subscapular (posterior), central, apical; the apical group forms the subclavian trunk.

  • Axillary node clearance for breast cancer removes the main drainage route of the limb and is the commonest cause of upper limb lymphoedema.

  • Which superficial vein is on the lateral side of the limb and where does it end? — The cephalic vein; it ends in the axillary vein after crossing the deltopectoral groove.
  • What is the basilic hiatus? — The opening in the deep fascia of the middle of the arm through which the basilic vein passes to join the brachial veins.

I.19The organization of spinal cord segments and spinal nerves. The general organization and features of the cranial nerves.

Core answerThe spinal cord is built metamerically: each spinal segment emits one pair of spinal nerves through a dorsal (sensory) root carrying the spinal ganglion and a ventral (motor) root, and the two unite in the intervertebral foramen into a mixed spinal nerve that divides into dorsal, ventral and communicating rami. There are 31 pairs of spinal nerves; the ventral rami of adjacent segments form the cervical, brachial, lumbar and sacral plexuses, while thoracic ventral rami stay segmental as intercostal nerves. The 12 pairs of cranial nerves belong to the PNS, leave (or enter) the skull through named foramina, and carry general somatic and visceral as well as special sensory and branchial motor fibres.

The spinal cord and its segments

  • 31 pairs: 8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral, 1 coccygeal.
  • Because the cord is much shorter than the vertebral canal, the lumbosacral roots run obliquely below the conus as the cauda equina; a pial filum terminale continues from the apex of the conus to the coccyx.
  • The dural sac and subarachnoid space extend down to the SII vertebra, which is why CSF is sampled below the LII level.
  • Meninges: dura mater (separated from bone by the epidural space), arachnoid mater, pia mater; the pia forms the segmentally arranged denticulate ligaments that anchor the cord within the dural sleeve.

  • Grey matter is H-shaped: anterior (ventral) horn = motor neuron cell bodies; posterior (dorsal) horn = sensory relay neurons; the intermediolateral cell column (lateral horn) between T1 and L2 holds the preganglionic sympathetic neurons.

  • What exactly is a spinal segment? — The transverse slice of cord giving one pair of spinal nerves; there are 31, not 33–35, because the homologous segments have fused.

I.20Organization and supply areas of the brachial plexus.

Core answerThe brachial plexus is the somatic nerve plexus of the upper limb, formed by the anterior rami of C5–C8 and most of T1. It is built in five stages — roots → trunks → divisions → cords → branches — begins in the neck between the scalene muscles, crosses the first rib and enters the axilla, where the three cords surround the second part of the axillary artery. Its branches supply every muscle and skin area of the shoulder girdle and limb, plus the scapular region and upper thoracic wall.

Roots and their topography

  • Roots = anterior rami of C5, C6, C7, C8 and most of T1, emerging from the intervertebral foramina.
  • Close to their origin the roots receive grey rami communicantes from the sympathetic trunk, which carry postganglionic sympathetic fibres for the limb.
  • The roots pass posterior to the vertebral artery and between the anterior and middle scalene muscles through the scalene hiatus (hiatus scaleni), lying superior and posterior to the subclavian artery.
  • C5 also contributes a small branch to the phrenic nerve (C3–C5).

Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Erb–Duchenne (upper plexus) palsy — roots C5–C6, e.g. birth injury or a fall on the shoulder: deltoid, supraspinatus, biceps and brachioradialis are lost, so the limb hangs adducted and medially rotated with the forearm pronated and the hand flexed (waiter’s tip).
  • Klumpke (lower plexus) palsy — roots C8–T1: paralysis of the small muscles of the hand (claw hand) and sensory loss on the medial forearm and hand; T1 sympathetic fibres may be involved giving a Horner syndrome.

  • Why does the C7 root form a single middle trunk yet reach two cords? — Because its anterior division joins the lateral cord and its posterior division joins the posterior cord; C7 does not enter the medial cord, which is formed by C8 and T1 only.
  • Which nerve is endangered in an axillary lymph node clearance and with what deformity? — The long thoracic nerve; serratus anterior paralysis gives a winged scapula.

I.21Branches of the median nerve, functional loss of the median nerve.

Core answerThe median nerve (C5–C8, T1) is formed by the union of a lateral and a medial root in the axilla anterior to the third part of the axillary artery. It runs with the brachial artery, passes between the two heads of pronator teres into the forearm, supplies most of the flexor compartment (all except FCU and the medial half of FDP) through its muscular branches, and enters the hand through the carpal tunnel. In the hand it gives the recurrent branch to the three thenar muscles and the palmar digital nerves to the lateral three and a half digits, so a lesion produces thenar wasting with loss of thumb opposition and a characteristic sensory loss.

The two classic deformities

  • Ape hand — wasting of the thenar eminence with the thumb lying flat beside the palm, because opposition, abduction and the superficial head of the short flexor of the thumb are lost while the adductor pollicis (ulnar nerve) is intact and pulls the thumb into the palm.

Must-memorise
  • Median nerve = two roots (lateral C5–C7 + medial C8–T1) = C5–C8, T1 overall; no major branches in the arm (small articular twigs to the elbow joint may be given off).

  • The anterior interosseous nerve is purely motor: FPL, lateral FDP, pronator quadratus.
  • The palmar branch leaves before the carpal tunnel and is therefore spared in carpal tunnel syndrome — the single most useful localising point.
  • Recurrent branch: loops around the distal flexor retinaculum, so it is at risk in carpal tunnel release.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome — compression of the median nerve under the flexor retinaculum: nocturnal burning pain and paraesthesia in the lateral three and a half digits, thenar wasting and loss of opposition; a positive Tinel sign on tapping over the retinaculum; treated by division of the flexor retinaculum.
  • Supracondylar fracture of the humerus in children — the commonest high median lesion; the anterior interosseous branch is often the first to fail.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Why is the hand of benediction a median, not an ulnar, sign? — Because the flexors of the index and middle fingers (FDS, lateral FDP) plus their lumbricals are median; the ulnar-supplied ring and little fingers still flex.
  • How do you distinguish an anterior interosseous lesion from a carpal tunnel lesion? — AIN: pure motor, no sensory loss, weak pinch with a flat OK sign. Carpal tunnel: sensory loss plus thenar wasting, long flexors normal.

  • Loss of finger abduction and adduction — the patient cannot spread the fingers against resistance; the interosseous spaces look hollow (wasting of the dorsal interossei is visible and palpable on the dorsum).

  • Little finger held abducted at rest (Wartenberg's sign): the extensor digiti minimi (radial nerve, still working) is unopposed once the ulnar-innervated palmar interossei and hypothenar muscles are lost — not because abductor digiti minimi is unopposed.
Must-memorise
  • Ulnar nerve = C8, T1 (with occasional C7); medial cord; no branch in the arm.
  • Forearm: FCU and medial half of FDP only. Hand: all intrinsic muscles except the three thenar muscles and the two lateral lumbricals.
  • Runs behind the medial epicondyle (cubital tunnel) and superficial to the flexor retinaculum beside the pisiform.
  • The deep branch passes through Guyon’s canal between the hook of the hamate and the flexor tendons — a site of entrapment by ganglia.
  • Claw hand is worse with a wrist lesion than with an elbow lesion (the ulnar paradox) because the long flexors of the ring and little fingers still work.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Cubital tunnel syndrome — compression behind the medial epicondyle from leaning on the elbow or from a fracture; numbness of the little finger is often the first symptom, with wasting of the interossei later.
  • Guyon’s canal syndrome — compression at the wrist by a ganglion, a hook-of-hamate fracture or prolonged pressure (cycling, crutch use): intrinsic muscle wasting with the long flexors intact.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Why is the little finger clawed more than the index? — The two lateral lumbricals (median) still extend the interphalangeal joints of the index and middle fingers, so the imbalance is greatest in the little and ring fingers.
  • Name the two ulnar-innervated forearm muscles. — Flexor carpi ulnaris and the medial half of flexor digitorum profundus (ring and little fingers).

I.23Branches of the radial nerve, functional loss of the radial nerve.

Core answerThe radial nerve (C5–C8, with an inconsistent T1 contribution) is the largest terminal branch of the posterior cord. It leaves the axilla through the triangular interval with the profunda brachii artery, spirals in the radial groove of the humerus directly on bone, crosses the lateral intermuscular septum into the anterior compartment, and divides at the lateral margin of the cubital fossa into a deep branch (which becomes the posterior interosseous nerve) and a superficial branch. It is the nerve supply of the entire extensor musculature of the upper limb, so its lesion produces wrist drop, finger drop and a small dorsolateral sensory loss.

The two classic pictures

  • Wrist drop (manus pendula) — the hand hangs flexed at the wrist because all wrist extensors are paralysed; the fingers and thumb cannot be extended at the MCP joints. If the triceps is intact, elbow extension and the triceps jerk are preserved.
  • Saturday-night palsy — prolonged compression of the radial nerve in the radial groove while the arm hangs over the back of a chair during sleep (often after alcohol); the classic cause of a painless wrist drop that recovers spontaneously over weeks.
  • Finger drop with preserved wrist extension points to the posterior interosseous nerve, not to the radial groove — an important localising distinction.
  • Trophic and vasomotor changes are mild; the sensory deficit is smaller than the motor deficit because the radial territory is mainly dorsal.
Must-memorise
  • Radial nerve = posterior cord, C5–C8 (T1); the largest terminal branch of the brachial plexus.
  • Passes through the triangular interval with the profunda brachii artery and lies on bone in the radial groove — hence the risk in midshaft humeral fractures.
  • It supplies all muscles of the posterior compartments of arm and forearm, and only skin in the hand.
  • Terminal branches: deep (motor, becomes posterior interosseous) and superficial (sensory).
  • Key localisers: triceps power (proximal lesion), wrist extension (ECRL spared distal to the elbow), sensory loss on the dorsum of the hand.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Midshaft fracture of the humerus — the nerve is tethered to bone by the profunda brachii artery, so it is stretched or divided; wrist drop and numbness on the dorsum of the hand must always be documented before and after reduction.
  • Saturday-night palsy — a compression neuropathy with a good prognosis; management is splinting of the wrist and hand in extension and physiotherapy while re-myelination occurs.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Why is wrist extension preserved in a posterior interosseous nerve lesion? — Because extensor carpi radialis longus is innervated by the radial nerve proper, before it divides.
  • Which muscle's paralysis causes the wrist to deviate radially when the patient attempts extension? — Loss of extensor carpi ulnaris (posterior interosseous nerve) with intact ECRL.

I.24Skin innervation of the upper limb.

Core answerThe skin of the upper limb is innervated by two overlapping systems: the dermatomes, which are the segmental territories of the spinal nerves (C4–T2) distributed through the brachial plexus, and the cutaneous (peripheral) nerves, which are the named branches of the plexus and carry fibres of several segments. Because the two maps cut across each other, the pattern of sensory loss distinguishes a root lesion (dermatomal, “band-like”, with minimal overlap) from a peripheral nerve lesion (territory of one named nerve).

General principles

  • Dermatome = the area of skin supplied by the sensory fibres of one spinal segment, i.e. one dorsal root and its spinal ganglion. Adjacent dermatomes overlap, so division of a single root causes only a narrow strip of sensory loss.
  • Peripheral (cutaneous) nerve territory = the skin area supplied by one named nerve (e.g. lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm); it may be much larger than a dermatome and crosses several dermatome boundaries.
  • The upper limb dermatomes are carried by the ventral rami of C5–T1 (plus C3, C4 for the shoulder and T2 for the axilla), all mixed in the brachial plexus.
  • The dorsal rami supply only the paravertebral skin of the back — they do not reach the limb.

Clinical use

  • Root versus nerve: a C6 root lesion affects the thumb dermatome plus the biceps reflex and elbow flexion; a median nerve lesion at the wrist affects the palmar lateral three and a half digits plus the thenar muscles but spares the C6 dermatome of the thumb dorsum and the biceps jerk.

  • Reflexes: biceps C6, triceps C7, finger flexors C8.

  • Testing sensation is done with cotton wool (light touch) and a pin (pain) in the exclusive zones, comparing both sides; vibration and joint position test the posterior columns.
  • Axillary nerve injury after shoulder dislocation: a small numb patch over the lower deltoid is the only sensory clue.
Likely follow-up questions
  • What is the difference between a dermatome and a cutaneous nerve territory? — A dermatome is a segmental (root) area with overlap; a cutaneous territory is the whole area of one named nerve, often spanning several dermatomes.
  • Which two nerves supply the dorsum of the little finger? — The dorsal branch of the ulnar nerve; distally the palmar digital nerves also send dorsal branches to the nail bed.

I.25Axillary fossa, triangular and quadrangular axillary spaces.

Core answerThe axillary fossa (axilla) is an irregularly shaped pyramidal space between the neck and the arm, with four walls, an inlet (apex) at the clavicle and first rib, and a floor of skin and fascia. All major structures passing into and out of the upper limb travel through it: the axillary artery and vein, the cords and branches of the brachial plexus, and the axillary lymph nodes. Openings between the muscles of its posterior wall — the quadrangular space, triangular space and triangular interval — provide the routes to the posterior scapular and deltoid regions.

Shape, inlet and floor

  • The axilla is a pyramidal space: four sides, an inlet (apex) directed superiorly towards the neck, and a floor (base) directed inferiorly.
  • Inlet (apex): margins are entirely bony — the posterior surface of the clavicle anteriorly, the lateral border of rib I medially and the superior border of the scapula (as far as the coracoid process) posteriorly; the apex itself points laterally and is formed by the medial aspect of the coracoid process.
  • Floor: a dome of skin and deep fascia between the anterior and posterior axillary folds; the anterior fold lies more superiorly than the posterior. The lateral part of the floor opens into the arm.
  • The axillary sheath (a prolongation of the prevertebral fascia) wraps the axillary artery, vein, cords and terminal branches.

  • Crutch palsy — pressure in the axilla on the radial nerve (or on the whole posterior cord) produces a wrist drop.
  • Shoulder dislocation — the axillary nerve is stretched as it passes around the surgical neck, so deltoid function and the skin over the lower deltoid must be tested before and after reduction.
Must-memorise

  • Quadrangular space = axillary nerve + posterior circumflex humeral vessels.
  • Triangular space = circumflex scapular vessels. Triangular interval = radial nerve + profunda brachii artery.
  • Axillary artery parts are separated by pectoralis minor; the cord names follow the second part.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Sentinel node biopsy uses the lymphatic drainage of the mammary gland through the pectoral (anterior) group of axillary nodes — the anatomical basis of axillary staging.
  • Axillary vein puncture for central lines uses the vein in the axilla; the axillary sheath explains why arterial and venous bleeding in this region is poorly contained.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Which structure divides the axillary artery into three parts? — The pectoralis minor muscle.
  • What is the difference between the triangular space and the triangular interval? — Both are bordered by the long head of triceps and teres major; the triangular space is closed superiorly by subscapularis and transmits the circumflex scapular vessels, whereas the triangular interval is closed laterally by the humerus and transmits the radial nerve and profunda brachii artery.

I.26Topography of the volar and dorsal wrist regions: tendons, tendon sheaths, osteofibrous compartments, vessels and nerves. The carpal tunnel.

Core answerAt the wrist the carpal bones form a deep carpal arch whose medial and lateral prominences are spanned by the flexor retinaculum, converting the arch into the carpal tunnel (long flexor tendons plus the median nerve) and, on the medial side, the ulnar canal (ulnar nerve and artery). On the dorsum the extensor retinaculum holds the extensor tendons in six osteofibrous compartments, each with its own synovial sheath. The tunnels and compartments explain the entrapment syndromes of the wrist.

Bony framework: the carpal arch

  • The carpal arch is open anteriorly (palmar). Its medial base is the pisiform and the hook of the hamate; its lateral base is the tubercles of the scaphoid and trapezium.
  • The proximal carpal row is formed by the scaphoid, lunate, triquetrum and pisiform; the distal row by the trapezium, trapezoid, capitate and hamate.
  • Palpable landmarks at the wrist: pisiform (medial), tubercle of the scaphoid (lateral, in the floor of the snuffbox), styloid processes of radius and ulna.

  • Mechanism: any increase in the volume of the tunnel contents or decrease in its size raises the pressure, causing venous congestion, oedema and ischaemic damage of the median nerve.

  • Retinaculum attachments: scaphoid and trapezium laterally; pisiform and hook of hamate medially.
  • Six dorsal compartments in order (radial to ulnar): APL+EPB, ECRL+ECRB, EPL, ED+EI, EDM, ECU.
  • Two synovial sheaths in the carpal tunnel: one common for FDS and FDP, one for FPL.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Thenar wasting is a late sign; sensory symptoms precede it, so early decompression prevents permanent loss of opposition.
  • Recurrent branch injury during retinaculum release abolishes opposition while leaving sensation intact — a well-known surgical complication, since the branch curves around the distal edge of the retinaculum.

  • Why is the median nerve the most vulnerable structure in the carpal tunnel? — It is the most superficial structure under the retinaculum and has no sheath or tendon to cushion it.
  • Where does the ulnar nerve run relative to the carpal tunnel? — Superficial to the flexor retinaculum, in the ulnar canal, between the pisiform and the hook of the hamate.

I.27Dorsum of the hand. The anatomical snuffbox (foveola radialis).

Core answerThe dorsum of the hand is covered by thin skin and loose subcutaneous tissue containing the dorsal venous network (origin of the cephalic and basilic veins) and the terminal branches of the superficial radial and dorsal ulnar nerves. Deep to the extensor tendons lie the dorsal interossei. Between the tendons of the thumb, at the base of the first metacarpal, is the triangular anatomical snuffbox (foveola radialis), whose floor is the scaphoid and trapezium and which transmits the radial artery — the region is examined clinically for scaphoid fracture and for the radial pulse.

Skin and superficial fascia

  • The skin is thin, loosely attached and hair-bearing, with the subcutaneous tissue arranged in loose areolar planes — hence the easy spread of oedema and the mobility of the skin.
  • Dorsal venous network lies over the metacarpals; its lateral end continues as the cephalic vein (crossing the anatomical snuffbox into the forearm) and its medial end as the basilic vein.
  • Cutaneous nerves: the dorsolateral area from the superficial branch of the radial nerve, the dorsomedial area from the dorsal branch of the ulnar nerve; both give dorsal digital nerves that reach approximately the terminal interphalangeal joints, the nail beds being supplied by dorsal branches of the palmar digital nerves.
  • Lymphatics of the dorsum run with the cephalic and basilic veins to the axillary and, for the medial side, sometimes the infraclavicular nodes.

Deep fascia and the extensor apparatus

  • The extensor tendons, held in their six compartments at the wrist, fan out over the metacarpals, interconnected by oblique intertendinous connections on the dorsum — which is why the fingers tend to move together in extension.
  • Over each MCP joint the extensor tendon expands into the triangular dorsal digital expansion (extensor hood): its apex attaches to the distal phalanx, its central part to the middle phalanx (index to little fingers) or proximal phalanx (thumb), and its base wraps the sides of the MCP joint and attaches mainly to the deep transverse metacarpal ligaments.
  • The lumbricals, interossei and abductor digiti minimi insert into the free margins of the hoods, and in the thumb the adductor pollicis and abductor pollicis brevis do so. Through the hood the intrinsic muscles can flex the MCP joint while extending the interphalangeal joints — the mechanism of the “intrinsic-plus” hand.
  • Extensor digiti minimi, extensor indicis and extensor pollicis brevis also join the hoods of their digits.

Vessels of the dorsum

  • The dorsal carpal arch is formed by the dorsal carpal branch of the radial artery and the dorsal carpal branch of the ulnar artery; it lies on the carpal bones deep to the extensor tendons.
  • From it arise the dorsal metacarpal arteries, which divide into dorsal digital arteries for the adjacent sides of the fingers; they anastomose with dorsal branches of the proper palmar digital arteries.
  • The first dorsal metacarpal artery arises from the radial artery before it penetrates the back of the hand and supplies the adjacent sides of the thumb and index finger.
  • Deep veins follow the arteries; the superficial dorsal venous network is the clinically important venous access on the dorsum.

Clinical importance

  • Scaphoid fracture — tenderness in the snuffbox after a fall on the outstretched hand. The scaphoid is best palpated within the snuffbox when the hand is in ulnar deviation. Because its blood supply enters distally and runs retrogradely, a fracture through the waist risks avascular necrosis of the proximal pole and non-union.
  • Radial pulse — the radial artery can be felt in the floor of the snuffbox in the relaxed hand, and its terminal branches can be rolled against the extensor pollicis longus tendon.
  • Venous access — the cephalic vein adjacent to the snuffbox is a preferred site for a short-term intravenous cannula.
  • Superficial radial nerve — its branches lie subcutaneously over the snuffbox and can be damaged by tight wristbands or cannulation, producing numbness of the dorsolateral hand.
Must-memorise
  • Snuffbox: lateral border = APL + EPB; medial border = EPL; floor = scaphoid + trapezium (+ ECRL/ECRB tendons); content = radial artery, superficial radial nerve, cephalic vein.
  • Dorsal interossei (4) abduct the index, middle and ring fingers; all intrinsic muscles of the hand are ulnar except the three thenar muscles and two lateral lumbricals (median).
  • Cephalic vein from the lateral end of the dorsal venous network; basilic vein from the medial end.
  • Dorsal cutaneous supply: superficial radial nerve laterally, dorsal branch of the ulnar nerve medially.
  • The extensor hood (dorsal digital expansion) is the insertion site of the intrinsic muscles and the mechanism of IP joint extension.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Scaphoid fracture is notoriously occult on early radiographs; snuffbox tenderness with a normal film is treated as a fracture until proven otherwise (repeat views, CT or MRI).
  • Avascular necrosis of the proximal scaphoid pole, and of the lunate (Kienböck), are the classic examples of retrograde intra-osseous blood supply in the carpus.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Why is a scaphoid fracture so important? — Its blood supply enters distally, so a waist fracture can cut off the proximal pole and cause avascular necrosis and non-union.
  • What lies in the floor of the snuffbox? — The scaphoid and trapezium, with the tendons of ECRL and ECRB distally.

I.28Palm of the hand: muscles, fasciae, compartments, vessels and nerves.

Core answerThe palm is a layered region: skin, superficial fascia, the palmar aponeurosis, then the superficial palmar arch with the digital nerves, the flexor tendons with the lumbricals, the deep palmar arch with the deep branch of the ulnar nerve, and finally the interossei and adductor pollicis against the metacarpals. Its muscles are the thenar, hypothenar and metacarpal (lumbrical and interosseous) groups, supplied by the median and ulnar nerves; the blood supply comes from the superficial (ulnar) and deep (radial) palmar arches.

Fasciae of the palm

  • Palmar aponeurosis (aponeurosis palmaris) — a triangular thickening of the deep fascia over the palm: its apex is continuous with the palmaris longus tendon (or anchored to the flexor retinaculum) and its fibres radiate to the bases of the digits, with transverse fibres interconnecting them. It is firmly attached to the skin distally, and the vessels, nerves and long flexor tendons lie deep to it.
  • Palmaris brevis — a small quadrangular subcutaneous muscle over the hypothenar eminence, from the palmar aponeurosis and flexor retinaculum to the dermis of the medial margin; it deepens the cup of the palm and improves grip. Nerve: superficial branch of the ulnar (C8, T1).
  • Fibrous digital sheaths — begin anterior to the MCP joints and extend to the distal phalanges, holding the flexor tendons against the phalanges; the annular (A1–A5) and cruciate (C1–C3) pulleys prevent bowstringing. Their synovial sheaths allow frictionless gliding and are the site of flexor tenosynovitis.
  • Deep fascia of the palm sends lateral and medial septa to the third metacarpal and to the fifth metacarpal, dividing the palm into fascial compartments.

Compartments and fascial spaces of the palm

  • Thenar compartment — the three thenar muscles, separated by the lateral septum; it is the site of thenar wasting in median nerve lesions.
  • Hypothenar compartment — the three hypothenar muscles, medial to the medial septum.
  • Central (midpalmar) compartment — the flexor tendons, their sheaths and the lumbricals, between the two septa.
  • Adductor compartment — adductor pollicis, deepest and most lateral, against metacarpal III.

Nerves of the palm

  • Median nerve — enters through the carpal tunnel, gives the recurrent branch to the thenar muscles and the palmar digital nerves to the lateral three and a half digits; the digital nerves also supply the first and second lumbricals.
  • Ulnar nerve — enters superficial to the flexor retinaculum, divides at the pisiform into the superficial branch (palmaris brevis and skin of the medial one and a half digits) and the deep branch (hypothenar muscles, adductor pollicis, all interossei, the two medial lumbricals, and articular branches to the wrist).
  • Radial nerve — only the superficial branch reaches the hand, and it supplies skin (dorsolateral) only.
  • Communicating branches between the median and ulnar nerves in the palm are common, which makes the territory of the superficial ulnar branch variable.
Must-memorise
  • All intrinsic muscles of the hand are ulnar except the three thenar muscles and the two lateral lumbricals (median) — and the deep head of flexor pollicis brevis may also be ulnar.
  • Thenar = abductor pollicis brevis, flexor pollicis brevis, opponens pollicis. Hypothenar = abductor, flexor and opponens digiti minimi.
  • Lumbricals: lateral two median, medial two ulnar. Interossei: all ulnar. Dorsal interossei abduct (DAB), palmar interossei adduct (PAD).
  • Superficial arch mainly ulnar (superficial to flexor tendons); deep arch mainly radial (deep to flexor tendons).
  • Six layers of the palm: skin → palmar aponeurosis → superficial arch and digital nerves → flexor tendons and lumbricals → deep arch and deep ulnar nerve → interossei and adductor pollicis.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Dupuytren contracture — degenerative thickening of the palmar aponeurosis, most often affecting the fibres to the ring and little fingers, pulls them into fixed flexion and requires surgical removal of the abnormal tissue.
  • Palmar space infection — pus in the thenar or midpalmar space tracks along the flexor sheaths through the carpal tunnel into the forearm; the hand is held in a characteristic posture with loss of the palmar concavity.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Which muscles form the thenar eminence and which nerve supplies them? — Abductor pollicis brevis, flexor pollicis brevis and opponens pollicis; the recurrent branch of the median nerve (C8, T1).
  • What is the action of the lumbricals and why is it special? — They flex the MCP joints while extending the interphalangeal joints, because they insert into the extensor hood — an action no other muscle can produce.
