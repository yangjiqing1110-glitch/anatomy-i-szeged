Cross-sectional Anatomy

Fourteen transverse sections answered in the order an examiner reads an image: bones, fascial compartments, muscles, vessels and nerves — with the tables you need to name every structure on the cut surface.

IV.1Middle of the body of humerus: muscular compartments, muscles, vessels and nerves (two cross-sections).

Core answerBoth cross-sections pass through the middle third of the arm, where the humerus and the medial and lateral intermuscular septa divide the limb into an anterior (flexor) compartment — biceps brachii, brachialis and coracobrachialis, supplied by the musculocutaneous nerve — and a posterior (extensor) compartment — triceps brachii, supplied by the radial nerve. The neurovascular bundle (brachial artery and veins, median nerve) lies on the medial side; the radial nerve and profunda brachii artery lie in the radial groove on the posterior surface of the humerus. The two sections differ in one decisive point: at the level of the deltoid tuberosity the deltoid is still present laterally and the ulnar nerve is still in the anterior compartment, while below the deltoid insertion the ulnar nerve has already pierced the medial intermuscular septum into the posterior compartment.

1.The two levels and the bone

Level A is taken through the deltoid tuberosity, in the upper part of the middle third of the humerus: the deltoid is inserting on the lateral surface of the bone, so the muscle is part of the section. Level B is taken a few centimetres more distally, below the insertion of the deltoid, where the muscle is no longer present and the anterior compartment is covered only by the biceps. In both planes the humerus is a ring of compact bone around a medullary cavity, roughly triangular to cylindrical, with the radial groove (sulcus n. radialis) running diagonally on its posterior surface and the nutrient foramen on its anteromedial surface; the bone is the main septum between the two compartments.

2.From the skin to the bone

  • Skin and superficial fascia: thin, mobile skin over an areolar layer containing the two large subcutaneous veins and the cutaneous nerves of the arm.
  • Cephalic vein (v. cephalica): lateral, in the groove between the deltoid and the biceps; it is the most constant vein for peripheral access in this region.
  • Basilic vein (v. basilica): medial; in the distal half of the arm it pierces the deep fascia and runs medial to the brachial artery, while proximally it is still subcutaneous.
  • Cutaneous nerves: the superior lateral cutaneous nerve of the arm (axillary nerve) over the deltoid; the inferior lateral cutaneous nerve of the arm and the posterior cutaneous nerve of the forearm (radial nerve) pierce the lateral head of the triceps; the medial cutaneous nerve of the arm and the medial cutaneous nerve of the forearm (medial cord) run on the medial side.
  • Deep fascia (brachial fascia) completely encloses the arm and sends the medial and lateral intermuscular septa to the humerus. These septa, with the bone, complete the two compartments; the medial septum is pierced by the ulnar nerve and the superior ulnar collateral artery, the lateral septum by the radial nerve and the radial collateral artery.
  • Neurovascular bundle: the brachial artery with its paired brachial veins and the median nerve lie in the medial part of the anterior compartment, covered by the fascia and crossed by the basilic vein.

4.Contents: where each vessel and nerve lies

Contents of the two sections
  • Level A: the brachial artery with its paired venae comitantes lies on the medial side of the humerus, applied to the medial head of the triceps and brachialis; the median nerve is lateral or anterolateral to it; the ulnar nerve is still in the anterior compartment on the medial side, next to the superior ulnar collateral artery; the radial nerve and the profunda brachii lie deep on the posterior surface in the radial groove, between the lateral and medial heads of the triceps; the basilic vein is in the superficial fascia medially (cephalic vein laterally); the axillary nerve and posterior circumflex humeral artery are already inside the deltoid.
  • Level B: the brachial artery is still medial, with the median nerve medial to it; the ulnar nerve has passed through the medial intermuscular septum and lies in the posterior compartment, anterior to the medial head of the triceps; the radial nerve is approaching the lateral intermuscular septum, still with the profunda brachii; the basilic vein is now deep to the deep fascia, medial to the brachial artery; the cephalic vein runs in the superficial fascia over the lateral border of the biceps.
  • In both planes the medial side is the "danger side": skin and superficial fascia with the medial cutaneous nerves → deep fascia → basilic vein → the neurovascular bundle applied to the medial surface of the humerus. At level A the bundle is anterior, with the ulnar nerve its most medial element; at level B the ulnar nerve has moved behind the septum, leaving the brachial vessels and median nerve as the medial contents.

5.Imaging of the section

The level is easily identified on axial images: the humerus lies in the middle of a rounded muscular mass, the brachial vessels and median nerve form the medial "danger point", and the radial nerve is seen against the posterior surface of the bone just lateral to the midline.

Must-memorise
  • The arm has two compartments: anterior = biceps brachii, brachialis, coracobrachialis (musculocutaneous n.); posterior = triceps brachii (radial n.). The humerus, medial and lateral intermuscular septa and brachial fascia bound them.
  • Radial nerve + profunda brachii in the radial groove between the lateral and medial heads of the triceps — the danger zone of a midshaft humeral fracture (wrist drop).
  • Median nerve stays with the brachial artery throughout the arm: lateral to it proximally, medial to it distally; no major branch in the arm.
  • Ulnar nerve pierces the medial intermuscular septum in the middle of the arm; that is the difference between the two cross-sections.
  • Deltoid is cut only at the higher level; axillary nerve and posterior circumflex humeral artery are its neurovascular pedicle.
  • The brachial artery can be compressed against the medial side of the humerus; it is palpable along its whole course.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Midshaft fracture of the humerus: the radial nerve is tethered against the bone in the radial groove, so it is the nerve most often injured — weakness of the wrist and finger extensors (wrist drop) and sensory loss on the dorsum of the hand.
  • Surgical-neck fracture of the humerus: the axillary nerve is damaged against the surgical neck — deltoid paralysis and a small patch of anaesthesia over the deltoid.
  • Brachial artery injury: penetrating trauma in the medial bicipital groove causes severe bleeding and distal ischaemia; the artery can be compressed against the humerus to control haemorrhage.
  • Compartment syndrome of the arm: after crush injury or prolonged pressure; pain on passive stretch of the flexors, tenseness of the compartment, and a risk of Volkmann-type contracture if decompression is delayed.
  • Venous access: the basilic vein is used for peripherally inserted central catheters and the cephalic vein for pacemaker leads; both are superficial at this level.
Likely follow-up questions
  • What separates the two compartments of the arm? — The humerus plus the medial and lateral intermuscular septa of the brachial fascia.
  • Where exactly is the radial nerve at mid-arm, and what accompanies it? — In the radial groove on the posterior surface of the humerus, deep to the lateral head of the triceps, with the profunda brachii artery.
  • Why does the ulnar nerve change compartments? — It pierces the medial intermuscular septum in the middle of the arm to reach the posterior compartment and the groove behind the medial epicondyle.
  • Which muscles of the anterior compartment are supplied by the radial nerve? — None as a main supply; the radial nerve contributes to the lateral part of brachialis (C7) but the compartment is musculocutaneous.
  • What would you see on the two sections that tells them apart? — The deltoid laterally and the position of the ulnar nerve (anterior at level A, posterior at level B).

IV.2Cubital fossa: muscular compartments, vessels and nerves (two cross-sections).

Core answerThe cubital fossa is the triangular space anterior to the elbow, bounded by the brachioradialis laterally and the pronator teres medially, with the interepicondylar line as its base, the brachialis and supinator as its floor and the bicipital aponeurosis as the roof of its distal part. Its contents, from lateral to medial, are the tendon of biceps brachii, the brachial artery and the median nerve; the radial nerve lies lateral to the fossa between brachialis and brachioradialis and divides here into superficial and deep branches, while the ulnar nerve passes behind the medial epicondyle and never enters the fossa. The first cross-section lies at the base of the fossa (through the elbow joint); the second lies distally at the bicipital aponeurosis, where the brachial artery has just divided into the radial and ulnar arteries.

1.Definition, boundaries and the two levels

The fossa is a depression anterior to the elbow joint, a transition zone between the arm and the forearm. Its base is the imaginary line between the medial and lateral epicondyles; its lateral border is the brachioradialis, its medial border the pronator teres; the two borders meet inferiorly at the apex. The floor is formed mainly by the brachialis, laterally by the supinator and the capsule of the elbow joint; the roof is skin, superficial fascia and, distally, the bicipital aponeurosis (aponeurosis musculi bicipitis brachii).

2.From the skin to the floor

  • Skin and superficial fascia: the most important vessel here is the median cubital vein (v. mediana cubiti), which runs diagonally across the roof from the cephalic vein on the lateral side to the basilic vein on the medial side. Its pattern is variable, which matters for venepuncture.
  • Cutaneous nerves: the lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm (continuation of the musculocutaneous nerve) laterally and the medial cutaneous nerve of the forearm medially.
  • Deep fascia and roof: the bicipital aponeurosis is a flat fibrous expansion from the medial side of the biceps tendon to the deep fascia of the forearm; its sharp medial edge is palpable. It lies between the median cubital vein superficially and the brachial artery plus median nerve deeply, and protects the artery from a venepuncture needle. The deep fascia also bridges the two epicondyles, completing the roof.
  • Floor: brachialis (with the biceps tendon descending in front of it), the supinator laterally and the anterior capsule of the elbow joint; the radial nerve separates the brachialis from the brachioradialis on the lateral side.

3.Muscular compartments at the elbow

At this level the compartments are meeting: the arm's anterior and posterior compartments end, and the forearm's anterior and posterior compartments begin. The section therefore cuts the distal ends of the upper-arm muscles and the proximal origins of the forearm muscles.

  • Level A — from lateral to medial: tendon of the biceps brachii → brachial artery → median nerve.   • Level B: the brachial artery divides into the radial artery (lateral, deep to the brachioradialis) and the ulnar artery (medial, deep to the pronator teres); the median nerve passes between the humeral and ulnar heads of the pronator teres; the radial nerve gives its superficial branch, which runs into the forearm deep to the brachioradialis, and its deep branch, which passes between the two heads of the supinator; the ulnar nerve enters the forearm between the heads of the flexor carpi ulnaris.
  • The median cubital vein lies in the superficial fascia above the bicipital aponeurosis, so a venepuncture at this site is separated from the brachial artery by the aponeurosis.

5.Imaging of the section

On axial MRI the cubital fossa is read as a triangle between the brachioradialis and the pronator teres, with the brachialis as its floor; the median nerve and brachial artery are the two round structures in front of the brachialis, and the biceps tendon is the flat structure at the lateral corner. Radiographs show the bony base of the fossa and are used for fractures and for the fat-pad signs of an elbow effusion.

Must-memorise
  • Boundaries: base = interepicondylar line; lateral = brachioradialis; medial = pronator teres; floor = brachialis and supinator; roof = skin, fascia and the bicipital aponeurosis.
  • Contents lateral → medial: biceps tendon → brachial artery → median nerve.
  • The radial nerve divides here into superficial and deep branches; the deep branch enters the supinator and becomes the posterior interosseous nerve.
  • The ulnar nerve does not enter the fossa; it passes posterior to the medial epicondyle.
  • The brachial artery divides into radial and ulnar arteries at the apex of the fossa, near the neck of the radius.
  • The bicipital aponeurosis separates the median cubital vein (superficial) from the brachial artery and median nerve (deep).
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Blood pressure: the stethoscope is placed over the brachial artery in the cubital fossa, medial to the biceps tendon, while the cuff compresses the artery against the humerus.
  • Venepuncture and dialysis fistula: the median cubital vein is the favourite site for venepuncture; surgical anastomoses here or at the wrist (radial artery to cephalic vein) create a high-flow fistula for dialysis.
  • Supracondylar fracture of the humerus: the distal fragment may injure the brachial artery and the median nerve, producing acute forearm ischaemia and, if untreated, a Volkmann ischaemic contracture.
  • Cubital tunnel syndrome: the ulnar nerve is compressed behind the medial epicondyle; numbness of the little finger and weakness of the intrinsic hand muscles result.
  • Radial nerve injury at the elbow: a fracture of the proximal radius or a lesion in the supinator produces finger drop, but wrist extension is largely preserved because the extensor carpi radialis longus is supplied by the radial nerve above the supinator.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Name the contents of the cubital fossa from lateral to medial. — Biceps tendon, brachial artery, median nerve.
  • What forms the roof and what does it protect? — The bicipital aponeurosis; it separates the median cubital vein from the brachial artery and median nerve.
  • Where does the radial nerve lie in relation to the fossa, and what does it do here? — Between the brachialis and brachioradialis; it divides into superficial and deep branches.
  • Why is the ulnar nerve not a content of the fossa? — It runs posterior to the medial epicondyle in the cubital tunnel.
  • Where does the brachial artery end? — At the apex of the fossa, near the neck of the radius, where it divides into the radial and ulnar arteries.
  • Which structures cross the fossa superficially? — The median cubital vein, the medial and lateral cutaneous nerves of the forearm, and the bicipital aponeurosis.

IV.3Upper part of the forearm (supinator's level): bones, muscular compartments, muscles, vessels and nerves (one cross-section).

Core answerThis section lies just distal to the cubital fossa, at the level of the radial tuberosity and the supinator. The radius lies laterally and the ulna medially, joined by the upper part of the interosseous membrane, which with the lateral intermuscular septum and the deep fascia separates the anterior (flexor) from the posterior (extensor) compartment. Anteriorly lie the superficial flexors (pronator teres, flexor carpi radialis, palmaris longus, flexor carpi ulnaris) and the beginning intermediate and deep layers; posteriorly lie the brachioradialis, the radial wrist extensors, the superficial extensors and, deepest of all, the supinator wrapped around the radius. The key relations are the median nerve between the two heads of pronator teres, the deep branch of the radial nerve between the two layers of the supinator, and the ulnar nerve between the two heads of flexor carpi ulnaris.

1.The level and the bones

The plane passes through the proximal forearm at the height of the radial tuberosity. Both bones are separate here and triangular in cross-section, with anterior, posterior and interosseous borders and anterior, posterior and lateral (radius) or medial (ulna) surfaces. The radius is the smaller, laterally placed bone: its head and neck are just above the plane, its radial tuberosity projecting medially receives the biceps tendon, and the anterior oblique line gives origin to the radial head of flexor digitorum superficialis. The ulna is the larger, medially placed bone; its coronoid region with the radial notch lies just above the plane, and the supinator crest on its posterolateral surface gives origin to the deep layer of the supinator. The interosseous membrane (membrana interossea) stretches between the interosseous borders; its free upper margin lies just below the radial tuberosity and its collagen fibres pass inferiorly from the radius to the ulna. It is the main septum between the compartments and transfers force from the hand to the radius and thence to the humerus.

4.Contents: where each vessel and nerve lies

Contents of the section
  • Median nerve: enters the forearm between the humeral and ulnar heads of the pronator teres, then passes between the humero-ulnar and radial heads of the flexor digitorum superficialis; it runs down the middle of the anterior compartment deep to the superficial flexors. It gives the anterior interosseous nerve, which runs on the anterior surface of the interosseous membrane with the anterior interosseous artery.
  • Ulnar nerve: enters the forearm between the humeral and ulnar heads of the flexor carpi ulnaris; it runs on the medial side between the flexor carpi ulnaris and the flexor digitorum profundus.
  • Radial nerve: lateral to the fossa it divides into a superficial branch, which runs deep to the brachioradialis with the radial artery, and a deep branch, which passes between the superficial and deep layers of the supinator and then continues as the posterior interosseous nerve in the posterior compartment.
  • Brachial artery divides at the neck of the radius into the radial artery (lateral, deep to the brachioradialis) and the ulnar artery (medial, deep to the pronator teres). The ulnar artery gives the common interosseous artery, which divides into anterior and posterior interosseous arteries; the posterior interosseous artery passes dorsally over the upper margin of the interosseous membrane.

5.Imaging of the section

On axial MRI the two bones are easy to identify: the radius is round and lateral, the ulna is triangular and medial, and the thin dark line between them is the interosseous membrane. The muscle groups are read around them: the superficial flexors form the anterior mass, the brachioradialis and radial extensors the anterolateral mass, and the extensor group the posterior mass.

IV.4Middle part of the forearm: bones, muscular compartments, muscles, vessels and nerves (one cross-section).

Core answerAt mid-forearm the radius (lateral) and ulna (medial) are both narrow shafts joined by the interosseous membrane, which separates the anterior (flexor) from the posterior (extensor) compartment. The anterior compartment contains the four superficial flexors, the flexor digitorum superficialis (intermediate layer) and the flexor digitorum profundus plus flexor pollicis longus (deep layer); the posterior compartment contains the brachioradialis, extensor carpi radialis longus and brevis, extensor digitorum, extensor digiti minimi, extensor carpi ulnaris, anconeus and the deep extensors. Four neurovascular planes dominate the picture: the median nerve between flexor digitorum superficialis and profundus; the ulnar nerve and artery between flexor carpi ulnaris and flexor digitorum profundus; the radial artery with the superficial branch of the radial nerve between brachioradialis and flexor carpi radialis; and the anterior interosseous nerve and artery on the interosseous membrane.

1.The level and the bones

The section passes through the middle third of the forearm, where the two bones are at their narrowest and are almost parallel. The radius is lateral and roughly cylindrical; the ulna is medial and triangular with a sharp subcutaneous posterior border. The interosseous membrane spans between them and is the most important landmark of the section, because almost every deep vessel and nerve lies in relation to it or to the bones. The radial artery turns laterally, the ulnar artery medially, and the median nerve occupies the midline of the anterior compartment.

  • At mid-forearm the radius is lateral and small, the ulna medial and triangular; the interosseous membrane is the midline structure.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Compartment syndrome of the forearm: after a crush injury or a supracondylar fracture; the deep flexor compartment is especially vulnerable. Pain on passive extension of the fingers, a tense forearm and paraesthesia are the warning signs; a late result is the Volkmann ischaemic contracture.
  • Anterior interosseous nerve syndrome: weakness of the flexor pollicis longus and of the lateral half of the flexor digitorum profundus — the patient cannot flex the tip of the thumb or index finger, and there is no sensory loss.
  • Laceration of the radial or ulnar artery is common in glass injuries because both are relatively superficial in the distal forearm; the dual supply of the hand usually allows ligation of one without loss of the hand.
  • Nerve entrapment must be localised by the pattern of weakness: pronator teres for the median nerve, the cubital tunnel above for the ulnar nerve, the supinator for the posterior interosseous nerve.

IV.5Lower part of the forearm (pronator quadratus' level): bones, muscular compartments, muscles, vessels and nerves (one cross-section).

Core answerIn the lower forearm the two bones are broad and close together, and the pronator quadratus fills the anterior interval between the distal radius and ulna. The muscle bellies have largely become tendons: anteriorly the flexor digitorum superficialis and profundus tendons (eight in all), the flexor pollicis longus tendon, the flexor carpi radialis and palmaris longus tendons and, medially, the flexor carpi ulnaris tendon; posteriorly the tendons of the brachioradialis, the radial wrist extensors, the thumb extensors, the extensor digitorum, extensor digiti minimi and extensor carpi ulnaris, with the extensor pollicis longus turning around the dorsal tubercle of the radius. The median nerve comes to lie superficially between the flexor carpi radialis and palmaris longus tendons, ready to enter the carpal tunnel; the ulnar nerve and artery lie medially, and the radial artery lies laterally, between the brachioradialis and flexor carpi radialis tendons.

1.The level and the bones

The plane passes through the distal quarter of the forearm. The radius is now broad and flattened anteroposteriorly, with the dorsal tubercle (Lister's tubercle) on its posterior surface and the ulnar notch on its medial side; the ulna ends in its small head and styloid process. The distal radio-ulnar joint is present, and the pronator quadratus spans the anterior surfaces of both bones just proximal to the joint. The interosseous membrane remains as a thin sheet between them; distally its aperture transmits the anterior interosseous vessels to the posterior compartment.

IV.6Wrist: carpal tunnel, bones, tendinous sheaths, tendons, vessels and nerves (one cross-section).

Core answerThe wrist section passes through the carpal arch, which is converted into the carpal tunnel by the flexor retinaculum. The arch is bounded medially by the pisiform and the hook of the hamate and laterally by the tubercles of the scaphoid and trapezium; the floor is formed by the distal carpal row. Through the tunnel pass the nine flexor tendons (four flexor digitorum superficialis, four flexor digitorum profundus, one flexor pollicis longus) and the median nerve, which lies anterior (superficial) to the tendons. Outside the tunnel, the ulnar nerve and artery run superficial to the retinaculum in the ulnar (Guyon's) canal, the flexor carpi radialis tendon has its own compartment, the palmaris longus tendon lies superficial to the retinaculum, and the radial artery crosses dorsally. The extensor tendons are held by the extensor retinaculum in six compartments with their own synovial sheaths.

1.The level and the bones

The section lies at the level of the wrist joint, immediately proximal to the metacarpals. The distal ends of the radius and ulna lie just above the plane, and the section cuts the two rows of carpal bones (ossa carpi), eight in all, arranged in a proximal and a distal row. The four bony landmarks that bound the carpal arch are the key to the section: the scaphoid tubercle and the trapezium tubercle laterally, and the pisiform with the hook of the hamate medially.

Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome: compression of the median nerve under the flexor retinaculum causes pain and pins-and-needles in the median distribution (thumb, index, middle and lateral half of the ring finger), sparing the thenar skin because its cutaneous branch leaves the nerve proximal to the retinaculum; thenar wasting and weakness appear later.   • Ulnar tunnel (Guyon's canal) syndrome: compression of the ulnar nerve at the wrist causes numbness of the little finger and weakness of the intrinsic hand muscles.
  • De Quervain's tenosynovitis affects the first extensor compartment (abductor pollicis longus and extensor pollicis brevis); pain on thumb movement and a positive Finkelstein test.
  • Scaphoid fracture: the blood supply enters distally and runs retrogradely, so a proximal-pole fracture risks avascular necrosis; tenderness in the anatomical snuffbox after a fall on the hand is the clinical clue.
  • Kienböck's disease: avascular necrosis of the lunate with pain, stiffness and later arthritis.

  • Where is the anatomical snuffbox and what bounds it? — Over the scaphoid and trapezium; APL and EPB laterally, EPL medially, with the radial artery in its floor.

IV.7Upper part of the thigh (femoral triangle's level): muscular compartments, vessels and nerves (one cross-section).

Core answerThe section passes through the upper third of the thigh, a few centimetres below the inguinal ligament, where the femoral vessels and nerve lie in the femoral triangle and the muscle is divided into anterior (quadriceps and sartorius, femoral nerve), medial (pectineus, adductors, gracilis, obturator externus, obturator nerve) and posterior (hamstrings, sciatic nerve) compartments. The femoral triangle has the inguinal ligament as its base, the sartorius as its lateral border and the adductor longus as its medial border; its floor is formed by the iliopsoas, pectineus and adductor longus. Its contents from lateral to medial are the femoral nerve, femoral artery, femoral vein and the femoral canal with lymphatics, the artery and vein enclosed in the femoral sheath while the nerve lies outside it.

1.The level and the bones

The plane lies below the inguinal ligament, so the hip joint and the pelvic bones are above the level of the section; the only bone cut is the femur, through its proximal shaft, whose posterior surface bears the gluteal tuberosity and the beginning of the linea aspera. The femur lies in the centre of the section, surrounded by the quadriceps anteriorly, the adductors medially and the hamstrings posteriorly.

IV.8Lower part of the thigh (adductor canal's level): muscular compartments, muscles, vessels and nerves (one cross-section).

Core answerA transverse section through the distal third of the thigh cuts the shaft of the femur and shows the three compartments of the thigh: an anterior compartment (the quadriceps femoris with sartorius), a medial compartment (adductor magnus, gracilis and the ending of the adductor longus) and a posterior compartment (the hamstrings with the sciatic nerve, already splitting into its tibial and common fibular divisions). The defining feature of this level is the adductor canal (canalis adductorius), the subsartorial space in which the femoral artery, femoral vein, saphenous nerve and nerve to vastus medialis run obliquely towards the adductor hiatus.

1.Level of the section, bones and orientation

The plane is placed in the distal (lower) third of the thigh, above the knee joint. Only one bone is cut — the femur (femur) — and it appears as a closed ring of thick compact cortex around a medullary cavity, roughly round to oval, positioned slightly anterior to the mid-point of the section because the hamstrings bulk large behind it. The tibia, fibula and patella are not in the section at this level.

  • A CT or MRI slice is displayed in the anatomical position as seen from below (inferior view): the top of the image is anterior, the bottom is posterior, and the right side of the image is the patient's left side.
  • The section is asymmetrical: the anterior compartment is broad and rounded, the medial compartment forms a thick wedge on the medial side, and the posterior compartment bulges behind the femur.
  • Because the femur is here a straight shaft without condyles, the muscles still surround the bone on all sides; this is the last level at which this is true before the popliteal fossa opens up.

2.Superficial to deep: skin, fascia, septa

  • Skin and subcutaneous fat form a continuous ring, thinnest anteriorly and medially, thickest posterolaterally.
  • Superficial fascia contains, on the medial side, the great saphenous vein (v. saphena magna) with its accompanying saphenous nerve branches; posteriorly lie the posterior cutaneous nerve of the thigh and small veins draining to the popliteal region.
  • Deep fascia (fascia lata) is a stout ring that sends two intermuscular septa to the femur: the lateral intermuscular septum (between vastus lateralis and biceps femoris) and the medial intermuscular septum (between vastus medialis and the adductors). A third, thinner posterior septum separates the adductors from the hamstrings. These septa, together with the femur and the septa of the thigh's own fascial sheath, define the three compartments.
  • The deep fascia is not uniformly tense: over the adductor canal it is reinforced by the sartorius muscle and by the vastoadductor membrane (septum vastoadductorium), which closes the canal anteriorly and is pierced by the saphenous nerve and the descending genicular artery.

Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Vascular access and the canal: the femoral artery is palpated and punctured in the femoral triangle, where it is superficial; this is the standard route for coronary and peripheral angiography.   • Saphenous nerve entrapment in the canal or at the vastoadductor membrane causes medial knee and leg pain; the nerve is also the target of a saphenous nerve block for medial knee surgery.
  • Adductor canal syndrome — compression of the femoral artery in the canal, typically by the adductor magnus or vastus medialis, producing exercise-related medial thigh pain in young patients.

  • Which structures change their relative position between the femoral triangle and the adductor canal? — The femoral vein moves from medial to posterior relative to the artery, and the saphenous nerve moves to the front of the artery.
  • What exactly passes through the adductor hiatus, and what does not? — The femoral artery and femoral vein pass through it to become the popliteal vessels; the saphenous nerve leaves the canal by piercing the vastoadductor membrane above the hiatus and does not pass through it.

IV.9Popliteal fossa: bones, muscular compartments, vessels and nerves (one cross-section).

Core answerA transverse section through the knee at the level of the popliteal fossa cuts the distal femur (condylar part) and the knee joint in front and opens behind into a fat-filled diamond bounded above by the hamstrings and below by the heads of the gastrocnemius, floored by the posterior surface of the femur, the knee capsule and the popliteus, and roofed by the deep fascia. Through this space pass, from superficial to deep, the tibial nerve, the popliteal vein and the popliteal artery, together with the common fibular nerve laterally and the small saphenous vein in the roof.

1.Level of the section, bones and orientation

  • The plane passes through the distal femur at the level of the condyles: the section shows the medial and lateral femoral condyles as two large, rounded areas of trabecular bone joined anteriorly by the trochlear groove, with the intercondylar fossa between them posteriorly.
  • Anteriorly the patella and the quadriceps tendon/patellar ligament are cut; the knee joint cavity and its capsule lie immediately behind the patella.
  • A slightly more distal plane cuts the tibial plateau (medial and lateral tibial condyles) instead of the femoral condyles; the relations of the popliteal fossa are unchanged.
  • The fibula appears only on lower planes, at the level of its head and neck, lateral to the lateral tibial condyle.
  • Orientation: axial images are viewed from below — anterior is at the top, and the right of the image is the patient's left side.

Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Baker's cyst (popliteal cyst): a synovial cyst communicating with the knee joint, appearing on MRI as a fluid-filled (bright on T2) loculation between the medial head of gastrocnemius and the semimembranosus tendon — a common differential of a popliteal mass.

  • Common fibular nerve palsy: compression or injury at the fibular neck causes foot drop and loss of eversion (superficial fibular n.) plus loss of dorsiflexion and sensory loss in the first web space (deep fibular n.).
  • Popliteal (Baker's) cyst rupture produces calf pain and swelling that mimics deep vein thrombosis — a classic clinical trap.

  • What pierces the roof of the popliteal fossa? — The small saphenous vein and the posterior cutaneous nerve of the thigh.
  • Why is the common fibular nerve more often injured than the tibial nerve? — It is superficial and fixed against the neck of the fibula, where it lies just under the skin and deep fascia.
  • Which muscles form the floor of the fossa? — Popliteus is the fleshy floor; the capsule, the femur and the tibia complete it.

IV.10Middle part of the leg: bones, muscular compartments, muscles, vessels and nerves (one cross-section).

Core answerA transverse section through the middle of the leg cuts the triangular shaft of the tibia anteromedially and the slender fibula laterally, joined by the interosseous membrane; the deep fascia and two intermuscular septa divide the limb into three compartments — anterior (extensor), lateral (fibular) and posterior (flexor), the posterior one being further split by the transverse intermuscular septum into superficial and deep parts. The anterior compartment contains the deep fibular nerve and anterior tibial vessels, the lateral compartment the superficial fibular nerve, and the posterior compartment the tibial nerve with the posterior tibial and fibular vessels.

1.Level of the section, bones and orientation

  • The plane passes through the shafts of both leg bones. The tibia (tibia) is triangular in cross-section, with an anterior border, a medial border and an interosseous border, and corresponding anterior (lateral), medial and posterior surfaces; its anterior and medial borders and its whole medial surface are subcutaneous and therefore palpable through the skin — this flat medial face gives the section its characteristic medial contour.
  • The fibula (fibula) is a thin bone with a small medullary cavity, positioned laterally and slightly posterior to the tibia.
  • The interosseous membrane (membrana interossea cruris) stretches between the interosseous borders of the two bones and completes the separation of the anterior from the posterior compartment; it also gives origin to muscle fibres.
  • Orientation: axial images viewed from below — anterior at the top, the patient's left on the right side of the image. The section is clearly triangular, with the apex anterior (the shin) and the base posterior (the calf).

2.Superficial to deep

  • Skin: thin over the shin and anterior surface, thicker and more mobile posteriorly.
  • Superficial fascia: medially contains the great saphenous vein with the saphenous nerve; posterolaterally the small saphenous vein with the sural nerve (formed by the medial sural cutaneous branch of the tibial nerve and the communicating fibular branch).
  • Deep fascia of the leg (fascia cruris): a tight sleeve that is fused to the periosteum of the anterior and medial borders of the tibia, so the anterior and posterior compartments cannot communicate across the shin.
  • Intermuscular septa: the anterior and posterior intermuscular septa pass from the deep fascia to the fibula and separate the lateral compartment from the anterior and posterior compartments. A transverse intermuscular septum between soleus and the deep flexors separates the superficial and deep parts of the posterior compartment.
  • The tight deep fascia and the unyielding bone are the anatomical reason why swelling inside a compartment raises tissue pressure — the basis of compartment syndrome.

  • Tibial nerve descends in the deep posterior compartment, between the soleus (superficial to it) and the tibialis posterior and flexor digitorum longus (deep to it); it lies just lateral to the posterior tibial artery.
  • Posterior tibial artery runs on the superficial surfaces of the tibialis posterior and flexor digitorum longus, medial to the tibial nerve, and reaches the tarsal tunnel behind the medial malleolus.

Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Compartment syndrome of the leg: after tibial fracture, crush injury or prolonged exercise, bleeding or oedema raises the pressure inside a closed compartment; the anterior compartment is most often affected and the deep fibular nerve is the most sensitive structure, so loss of dorsiflexion and of sensation in the first web space are early signs.   • Superficial fibular nerve lesion: loss of eversion; inversion injuries may stretch it over the fibula.
  • Tibial fracture: the tibia is subcutaneous, so a fracture may be open; the fibula, intact, acts as a splint and may delay displacement.
  • Deep vein thrombosis: the soleal and gastrocnemius veins are the commonest origin of calf DVT; on ultrasound the deep fascia and septa are the landmarks separating the compartments.
  • Fibular artery perforators: the fibular (peroneal) artery is the usual pedicle of the fibular free flap used in reconstructive surgery.

  • Why does the leg have a lateral compartment separate from the anterior one? — The anterior and posterior intermuscular septa run from the deep fascia to the fibula, isolating the fibular muscles.
  • What separates the superficial from the deep posterior compartment? — The transverse intermuscular septum, with the deep transverse fascia.
  • Where is the posterior tibial artery palpable? — Behind the medial malleolus, in the tarsal tunnel, midway between the medial malleolus and the calcaneus.

IV.11Foot (tarsal sinus' level): bones, muscles, tendinous sheaths, tendons, vessels and nerves (one cross-section).

Core answerA transverse section through the foot at the level of the tarsal sinus (sinus tarsi) cuts the talus above and the calcaneus below, with the fat-filled sinus between them containing the interosseous talocalcaneal ligament; the navicular and cuboid lie immediately in front. Dorsally the long extensor tendons with their synovial sheaths and the dorsalis pedis artery with the deep fibular nerve are seen; medially and plantarly the tendons of tibialis posterior, flexor digitorum longus and flexor hallucis longus enter the sole through the tarsal tunnel with the posterior tibial vessels and tibial nerve; laterally the fibularis longus and brevis tendons pass behind the lateral malleolus.

1.Level of the section and the bones cut

  • The plane passes through the talocalcaneal region. The talus (talus) is cut through its body and neck, and the calcaneus (calcaneus) through its body and anterior part including the sustentaculum tali medially.
  • Between the two bones, and opening on the lateral side of the foot, is the tarsal sinus — formed by the calcaneal sulcus on the superior surface of the calcaneus and the sulcus tali on the inferior surface of the talus. It is a wide gap filled with fat and fibrous tissue that is visible when the skeleton is viewed from the lateral side.
  • The interosseous talocalcaneal ligament lies inside the sinus and is a major stabiliser of the subtalar joint; the bifurcate (calcaneonavicular) ligament lies just in front of it.
  • Immediately anterior to this plane lie the navicular (medially, boat-shaped, with a prominent tuberosity for the tibialis posterior tendon) and the cuboid (laterally, with a groove for the fibularis longus tendon); on slightly proximal planes the medial and lateral malleoli of the tibia and fibula are cut.
  • Above the section, the ankle joint; below it, the sole. Orientation as for all axial images: anterior (dorsum) at the top, the patient's left on the right of the picture.

2.Superficial to deep, and dorsal versus plantar

  • Dorsum: thin skin and loose subcutaneous tissue, then the inferior extensor retinaculum holding down the long extensor tendons in their synovial sheaths, then the extensor digitorum brevis and extensor hallucis brevis muscles lying on the lateral part of the calcaneus and the cuboid.
  • Plantar surface: thick keratinised skin firmly bound to the plantar aponeurosis (a thickening of the deep fascia anchored to the medial process of the calcaneal tuberosity), then the first layer of intrinsic muscles — abductor hallucis medially, flexor digitorum brevis centrally, abductor digiti minimi laterally.
  • Medial side: the flexor retinaculum completes the tarsal tunnel over the groove between the medial malleolus, the talus and the calcaneus; septa from the retinaculum divide the tunnel into separate channels for each tendon and for the vessels and nerve.
  • Lateral side: the fibularis longus and brevis tendons pass behind the lateral malleolus, held by the superior and inferior fibular retinacula, and reach the lateral side of the calcaneus and the cuboid.

Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Tarsal tunnel syndrome: compression of the tibial nerve under the flexor retinaculum (flat foot, ganglia, tenosynovitis, fracture) causes burning pain and tingling of the sole with sparing of the heel skin (which is supplied by calcaneal branches given off before the tunnel) — a useful localising sign.

  • Dorsalis pedis pulse is palpable on the dorsum between the extensor hallucis longus and extensor digitorum longus tendons, and is used to assess the distal arterial supply of the foot.

  • Which tendons have synovial sheaths at the ankle? — All the long tendons that pass under a retinaculum: the three extensor tendons, the two fibular tendons, and the three flexor tendons; the sheaths allow the tendons to glide without friction.
  • Which nerve supplies most of the sole? — The lateral plantar nerve (S1–S3); the medial plantar nerve supplies the medial side and the first lumbrical.
  • How do the arches of the foot relate to this section? — The section is at the summit of the medial longitudinal arch (talus, calcaneus, navicular); the arch is maintained by the interosseous and plantar ligaments, the plantar aponeurosis and the tibialis posterior tendon.

IV.12Trunk (level of the primary branches of the aortic arch); (one cross-section).

Core answerThis is an axial CT level through the upper thorax in the superior mediastinum, at the height of the third thoracic vertebra, where the three great arteries of the arch — the brachiocephalic trunk, the left common carotid artery and the left subclavian artery — lie side by side in front of the trachea. In front of them are the thymus and the two brachiocephalic veins (the left crossing the midline), behind them the trachea, the oesophagus and the thoracic duct, with the phrenic and vagus nerves in the fat at the sides.

1.Level of the section and what the plane cuts

  • The plane lies in the superior mediastinum, whose inferior boundary is the transverse plane from the sternal angle to the intervertebral disc between TIV and TV; above, it opens obliquely into the neck at the thoracic inlet.
  • Bone cut: the manubrium of the sternum; alongside it the medial ends of the clavicles and the first ribs, with the second and third ribs cut further back, and posteriorly the body of the third thoracic vertebra with the spinal cord in its canal.
  • Lateral to the mediastinum the section cuts the superior lobes of both lungs, covered by mediastinal pleura.
  • The thymus occupies the retrosternal space. It is large in the child, atrophies after puberty and is largely replaced by fat in the adult, so on an adult CT it appears as a fatty retrosternal triangle rather than a gland.

2.Order of structures from anterior to posterior

  1. Skin and subcutaneous tissue of the anterior chest wall.
  2. Manubrium of the sternum (and the clavicles and costal cartilages at the sides).
  3. Thymus (or its fatty remnant) in the retrosternal space.
  4. Right and left brachiocephalic veins — the left brachiocephalic vein crosses the midline in front of all three arterial branches.
  5. Brachiocephalic trunk, then left common carotid artery, then left subclavian artery — from right to left in one row; at their origin the brachiocephalic trunk is slightly the most anterior.
  6. Trachea — midline, immediately behind the arterial row.
  7. Oesophagus — behind the trachea.
  8. Thoracic duct — posterior to the oesophagus, close to its left border, between the oesophagus and the left mediastinal pleura.
  9. Body of vertebra TIII, with the spinal cord behind it in the vertebral canal.

Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Central venous catheterisation: the internal jugular and subclavian veins are punctured here and the catheter passes through the brachiocephalic veins into the distal SVC or right atrium, so the venepuncture is guided by exactly this level's anatomy.
  • Tracheal compression: enlargement of the thyroid or a mediastinal mass at this level narrows the trachea, which is why the section is used to assess goitre and superior mediastinal masses.

  • Lymphadenopathy at this level (paratracheal nodes) is a common site of spread from lung cancer and is assessed on this slice.

  • Which structure lies immediately in front of the three branches? — The left brachiocephalic vein (with the thymus and manubrium in front of that).
  • Where does the superior mediastinum end? — At the plane from the sternal angle to the intervertebral disc between TIV and TV.
  • Why is the left brachiocephalic vein at risk in infants? — In infants and young children it may rise above the superior border of the manubrium and is therefore less protected.
  • What is the relation of the phrenic nerves to the mediastinal pleura? — They run immediately deep to the mediastinal pleura, between it and the fibrous pericardium, which is why they are at risk in pleural and pericardial disease.

IV.13Trunk (aortic arch's level); (one cross-section).

Core answerThis axial CT level passes through the arch of the aorta in the superior mediastinum at the height of the fourth thoracic vertebra. The arch occupies the middle of the section, with the superior vena cava on its right and the trachea and the oesophagus behind it; the thymus lies in front, the arch of the azygos vein is at the right of the trachea, and the thoracic duct passes behind the oesophagus close to its left border. The phrenic and vagus nerves accompany the lateral aspect of the arch.

1.Level of the section and what the plane cuts

  • The plane is in the superior mediastinum at the level of the fourth thoracic vertebra — the level at which the arch of the aorta ends on the left side (the arch ends at vertebral level TIV/V).
  • Bone cut: the manubrium of the sternum anteriorly, the bodies of the upper thoracic vertebrae posteriorly, ribs laterally.
  • Lung: the superior lobes on both sides, with the mediastinal pleura applied to the mediastinum.
  • At this level the left brachiocephalic vein has already joined the right one, so the superior vena cava has formed on the right, while the arterial branches have already arisen from the top of the arch higher up — so here we see the arch itself, not its three branches.
  • The thymus (or its fatty remnant, and the lower part that extends over the pericardial sac) lies anteriorly.

2.Order of structures from anterior to posterior

  1. Skin and subcutaneous tissue, then the manubrium of the sternum.
  2. Thymus in the retrosternal space.
  3. Arch of the aorta — the large central structure, curving up, back and to the left; the superior vena cava lies to its right.
  4. Trachea — behind the arch, in the midline.
  5. Oesophagus — behind the trachea, immediately in front of the vertebral column.
  6. Thoracic duct — posterior to the arch of the aorta, behind the oesophagus, slightly to the left.
  7. Body of vertebra TIV, with the spinal cord in the vertebral canal behind it.

Two structures are best described as lying at the sides of this anterior-to-posterior sequence: the arch of the azygos vein, which comes forward over the root of the right lung to join the superior vena cava on the right, and the left recurrent laryngeal nerve, which hooks under the arch on the left and ascends in the groove between the trachea and oesophagus.

4.Relations that must be recited

  • The arch is anterior and then lateral to the trachea as it curves backwards; it passes over the left main bronchus and ends on the left side of vertebra TIV/V as the descending aorta.
  • The arch reaches as high as the mid-level of the manubrium of the sternum.
  • In front of the arch: thymus, manubrium, and (higher up) the left brachiocephalic vein.
  • Behind the arch: trachea, oesophagus, thoracic duct, deep cardiac plexus and the left recurrent laryngeal nerve.
  • To the right of the arch: superior vena cava and the right vagus; the right pulmonary artery passes below the arch.
  • To the left of the arch: left phrenic nerve, left vagus, left superior intercostal vein and the mediastinal pleura.
  • Below the arch: the bifurcation of the pulmonary trunk, the left main bronchus, the ligamentum arteriosum and the left atrium.

Must-memorise
  • Level: superior mediastinum, vertebra TIV; the arch ends at the TIV/V level on the left.
  • Anterior to posterior: sternum → thymus → arch of aorta (+ SVC on its right) → trachea → oesophagus → thoracic duct → vertebra TIV.
  • The SVC is on the right of the arch; the azygos arch is at the right of the trachea.
  • The left vagus crosses the left side of the arch, giving the left recurrent laryngeal nerve under the arch just lateral to the ligamentum arteriosum.
  • The left phrenic is lateral to the left vagus and passes over the left surface of the arch.
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Aortic dissection: an intimal flap in the arch divides a true and a false lumen (seen as a linear filling defect on contrast CT); involvement of the arch threatens the coronary and cerebral arteries.
  • Coarctation of the aorta lies just distal to the origin of the left subclavian artery, that is, at the very beginning of the descending aorta immediately below this level.
  • Aortopulmonary window: the space between the aortic arch and the pulmonary artery contains the left recurrent laryngeal nerve; lymph node enlargement there (for example from lung cancer) compresses the nerve and causes left vocal cord palsy and hoarseness.
  • Right-sided aortic arch: the arch passes to the right of the trachea and over the right main bronchus; it may be asymptomatic or associated with congenital heart disease and an aberrant left subclavian artery.
  • Aberrant right subclavian artery: arises from the distal arch and passes behind the oesophagus, forming a vascular ring that can cause dysphagia.
  • Aortic trauma: deceleration injuries occur at the fixed points of the aorta — the aortic valve, the ligamentum arteriosum and the diaphragmatic hiatus — so the region just below the arch is a classic site of traumatic rupture.
Likely follow-up questions
  • What is in front of, behind, above and below the arch? — In front: thymus and manubrium; behind: trachea, oesophagus, thoracic duct and deep cardiac plexus; above: the three primary branches; below: the pulmonary trunk bifurcation and the left main bronchus.
  • Which nerve hooks under the arch, and which under the right subclavian artery? — The left recurrent laryngeal nerve under the arch; the right recurrent laryngeal nerve under the right subclavian artery.
  • Where does the SVC begin and end? — Begins behind the lower edge of the right first costal cartilage (junction of the brachiocephalic veins) and ends at the lower edge of the right third costal cartilage, where it enters the right atrium.
  • How many constrictions has the oesophagus, and which of them is at this level? — Four; the second is where the arch of the aorta crosses it in the superior mediastinum.

IV.14Trunk (pulmonary trunk's level); (one cross-section).

Core answerThis axial CT level passes through the pulmonary trunk just below the aortic arch, at the upper border of the middle mediastinum. Anteriorly the right ventricular outflow tract continues into the pulmonary trunk, which lies in front of and to the left of the ascending aorta; the superior vena cava is on the right of the ascending aorta, and behind them lie the tracheal bifurcation (or the two main bronchi), the left atrium and the oesophagus, with the descending thoracic aorta on the left and the azygos vein on the right of the vertebral column.

1.Level of the section and what the plane cuts

  • The plane lies in the middle mediastinum, immediately below the plane of the aortic arch, at the level at which the pulmonary trunk is still a single vessel before it divides into the right and left pulmonary arteries (the division occurs lower, just inferior to the level of the intervertebral disc between TIV and TV).
  • The ascending aorta and the pulmonary trunk are both inside the pericardial sac, wrapped in a common visceral layer of serous pericardium; the transverse pericardial sinus lies between the arterial reflection anteriorly and the venous reflection (SVC and pulmonary veins) posteriorly.
  • Anteriorly the section cuts the body of the sternum and the costal cartilages; the lower pole of the thymus may still be present over the pericardium.
  • Posteriorly the section cuts the bodies of the upper thoracic vertebrae (about TIV–TV), the oesophagus, the descending thoracic aorta and the azygos vein.
  • At this plane the trachea is at or just above its bifurcation, so the section may show the carina or the right and left main bronchi; the left atrium and the entrance of the pulmonary veins lie behind the aortic root.

2.Order of structures from anterior to posterior

  1. Skin, subcutaneous tissue and the body of the sternum (with costal cartilages at the sides).
  2. Lower pole of the thymus and the fibrous pericardium over the heart.
  3. Right ventricular outflow tract (conus arteriosus) — the most anterior cardiovascular structure, giving rise to the pulmonary trunk.
  4. Pulmonary trunk, anterior and to the left of the ascending aorta; the ascending aorta lies behind it and to its right.
  5. Superior vena cava — to the right of the ascending aorta, its lower half inside the pericardial sac.
  6. Tracheal bifurcation / right and left main bronchi — immediately behind the great arteries.
  7. Left atrium with the entry of the pulmonary veins, behind the aortic root; the oblique pericardial sinus lies behind the atrium.
  8. Oesophagus — behind the left atrium, directly in front of the vertebral column.
  9. Descending thoracic aorta on the left and azygos vein on the right, both anterolateral to the vertebral column; the thoracic duct between the oesophagus and the vertebral column, slightly to the left.
  10. Body of the vertebra (about TIV–TV) with the spinal cord behind it.

4.Relations that must be recited

  • Pulmonary trunk and ascending aorta are enclosed together in a common sheath of visceral serous pericardium — the reason the transverse pericardial sinus can be entered between them and the atria.
  • The pulmonary trunk is anterior and to the left of the ascending aorta; the right pulmonary artery then passes behind the ascending aorta and the SVC, while the left pulmonary artery passes below the arch and in front of the descending aorta.
  • The trachea divides at or just below the plane from the sternal angle to the intervertebral disc TIV/V; the oesophagus continues into the posterior mediastinum.
  • The descending aorta lies to the left of the vertebral column and the azygos vein to its right; the thoracic duct lies between the oesophagus and the vertebrae.
  • The left atrium is the most posterior chamber of the heart and lies directly in front of the oesophagus — which is why left atrial enlargement may compress the oesophagus and cause dysphagia.

Must-memorise
  • Level: middle mediastinum, through the pulmonary trunk just below the aortic arch.
  • Anterior to posterior: sternum → thymus → right ventricular outflow tract → pulmonary trunk + ascending aorta → SVC (right) → carina / main bronchi → left atrium → oesophagus → descending aorta (left) / azygos vein (right) → vertebra.
  • The pulmonary trunk is anterior and to the left of the ascending aorta; the right pulmonary artery passes behind the ascending aorta and SVC.
  • The left atrium is the most posterior cardiac chamber, directly in front of the oesophagus.
  • The transverse pericardial sinus lies between the arterial reflection (aorta and pulmonary trunk) and the venous reflection (SVC and pulmonary veins).
Clinical / radiological correlation
  • Pulmonary embolism: the pulmonary trunk and the main pulmonary arteries are the structures examined on a contrast-enhanced CT pulmonary angiogram; a filling defect in a contrast-filled artery is the diagnostic sign.
  • Pericardial effusion and tamponade: fluid collects in the pericardial cavity, which is visible around the ascending aorta and pulmonary trunk on this slice; the fibrous pericardium limits acute distension.
  • Left atrial enlargement: the atrium is directly in front of the oesophagus, so enlargement may compress it (dysphagia) and is also the anatomical basis of the oblique and transverse pericardial sinuses used in cardiac surgery.
  • Cardiac catheterisation and central lines: a catheter passed from the SVC through the right atrium and right ventricle enters the pulmonary trunk — the route used for right heart catheterisation and pulmonary angiography.
  • Congenital anomalies of the outflow tract: pulmonary stenosis and tetralogy of Fallot involve the right ventricular outflow tract and pulmonary trunk seen in this section.
  • Mediastinal masses: the pretracheal and subcarinal lymph node stations of this level drain the lungs and are staged on this slice.
Likely follow-up questions
  • Where does the pulmonary trunk begin and where does it divide? — It begins at the pulmonary orifice of the right ventricle, anterior to the aortic orifice, and divides into the right and left pulmonary arteries just inferior to the level of the TIV/V intervertebral disc, to the left of the midline.
  • What are the relations of the right pulmonary artery? — It passes to the right, posterior to the ascending aorta and the superior vena cava, to enter the right lung.
  • What are the relations of the left pulmonary artery? — It passes inferiorly to the arch of the aorta and anteriorly to the descending aorta, to enter the left lung.
  • What is the transverse pericardial sinus, and what bounds it? — It is the passage between the two reflections of serous pericardium: the ascending aorta and pulmonary trunk lie in front of it, the superior vena cava and the pulmonary veins behind it, and the left atrium below it.
  • Why is the oesophagus closely related to the left atrium? — The atrium forms the posterior surface of the heart in front of the oesophagus, so left atrial enlargement can compress the oesophagus.
